# Does tramadol have a psychotropic effect of its own — or only an analgesic one that looks like it?
*2026-07-26 · PubMed 27 · trials 2 · books 0*

> Assumed: report in English; "novel knowledge for self curiosity" read as an adjudicated ruling that foregrounds what is genuinely counterintuitive rather than a bedside protocol; all three populations you named (Thai adult OPD polypharmacy, inpatient/CL, adolescents/young adults) carried as boundary conditions rather than as separate reviews.

## The verdict

**Unsettled, and leaning much closer to "no" than the pharmacology advertises.** Tramadol carries real mood-relevant pharmacology, but the only adequately powered human test of a mood endpoint missed its primary outcome (postpartum depression at 4 weeks: 6.6% vs 10.2% vs 10.5%, Bonferroni-corrected p=0.118 and p=0.098) and survives in the literature only through a subgroup. The mood signal that does appear is better explained by three things that are not antidepressant action — relief of pain, μ-opioid reward, and the absence of withdrawal — than by the SNRI mechanism everyone quotes. Do not use it, or teach it, as a psychotropic; do hold it in mind as *an SNRI that happens to be an opioid*, because that is what makes it dangerous next to your patients' other drugs. **Confidence: moderate** — not higher because a 1230-patient randomised trial did find a consistent direction of effect on a DSM-5-diagnosed endpoint, and no trial has ever tested tramadol against placebo in a depressed or obsessional population at all.

The single most counterintuitive finding below: in animals, tramadol's antidepressant-like effect is **noradrenergic and naloxone-insensitive**, and it appears **only in nerve-injured animals**. Both facts point away from the "opioids treat depression" story that the drug is usually recruited into.

## The one adequately powered human test, and what it actually showed

Only ten trials have ever been registered anywhere at the intersection of tramadol and depression, anxiety or OCD, and exactly one was powered for a psychiatric endpoint: 1230 women randomised 1:1:1 after elective caesarean to tramadol PCIA (4 mg/mL), hydromorphone PCIA (0.04 mg/mL), or ropivacaine epidural (1.5 mg/mL) for 48 hours, with incidence of postpartum depression by DSM-5 criteria at 4 weeks as the primary outcome.

Intention-to-treat, tramadol gave 27 cases (6.6%) versus 10.2% on hydromorphone (OR 1.62, 95% CI 0.98–2.68, p=0.059) and 10.5% on ropivacaine (OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.01–2.75, p=0.046). After Bonferroni correction for the two comparisons, both became non-significant (p=0.118 and p=0.098). **The trial did not meet its primary endpoint.** What it is cited for — and titled for — is the pre-operative EPDS ≥10 subgroup, where PPD ran 16.5% on tramadol against 32.6% and 30.9% (corrected p=0.022 and p=0.038): a 16-point absolute difference, NNT ≈ 6 if it is real. A subgroup that large after a failed primary, in a single centre, in a design that could not be fully blinded (an epidural arm cannot be masked against two intravenous arms), with 48 hours of drug exposure and a 4-week endpoint, is a hypothesis. It is not an effect.

The supporting human evidence is thinner still and points the same way. A 106-patient trial after repeat caesarean found lower HADS scores at one week on tramadol than hydromorphone (anxiety 1.9 vs 3.6, p=0.033; depression 0.8 vs 2.7, p=0.023) — but in the same patients tramadol controlled visceral pain *worse* (2.9 vs 2.3 at 4 h, p=0.011; 2.4 vs 1.8 at 8 h, p=0.028), which is the wrong direction for an analgesia-mediated mood effect and the right direction for something about the comparator. A 170-patient trial using tramadol in both arms found no EPDS difference at all (p=0.154). And the 2026 synthesis of every placebo-controlled tramadol trial in chronic pain — 19 trials, 6506 participants — **pre-specified depressive symptoms as a main outcome measure and reports no estimate for it**, alongside "it was not possible to conduct a meta-analysis of the quality of life due to a lack of data." Thirty years of prescribing, and the mood outcome is unpoolable. That absence is the finding.

For scale: in that same synthesis tramadol's *analgesic* effect was −0.93 NRS points (97.5% CI −1.26 to −0.60), below the 1.0-point minimal important difference the authors set, against serious adverse events at OR 2.13 (97.5% CI 1.29–3.51) and numbers-needed-to-harm of 7 for nausea, 8 for dizziness, 9 for constipation, 13 for somnolence. A drug whose primary indication sits under its own MID is a poor candidate for an off-label second one.

## Why the preclinical signal doesn't transfer — and the control experiment psychiatry already ran

The mechanistic case is genuinely attractive, which is why the claim survives. Tramadol is a racemate whose two enantiomers do different jobs: (+)-tramadol inhibits serotonin reuptake and, with its CYP2D6-derived metabolite M1, supplies the μ-opioid agonism; (−)-tramadol inhibits noradrenaline reuptake. Parenteral analgesic potency is about 10% of morphine's, elimination half-life ~6 hours, protein binding ~20%. Structurally and functionally it sits close to venlafaxine with an opioid bolted on.

In animals that reads as an antidepressant. Racemic and (−)-tramadol dose-dependently cut immobility in the forced swim test while the (+) enantiomer did not; the effect was abolished by inhibiting noradrenaline synthesis and by phentolamine, yohimbine and propranolol — and **not** by naloxone or methysergide. In other words the antidepressant-like action was noradrenergic, not opioid and not serotonergic. It replicated in learned helplessness (fewer escape failures at 10–20 mg/kg) and against an active comparator (10 and 20 mg/kg comparable to fluoxetine 20 mg/kg over 7 days, while 40 mg/kg was *worse* than fluoxetine — a non-monotonic dose-response that ought to worry anyone extrapolating upward). Tramadol even produces the sleep-EEG signature of an antidepressant: dose-dependent REM suppression, longer REM onset latency, increased delta and reduced alpha power in non-REM.

Then the finding that reframes all of it. In the chronic constriction injury model, tramadol increased open-arm time by 67% (p<0.05) and reduced forced-swim immobility by 22% (p<0.001) **in nerve-injured rats, with only minor effects in animals without injury** — while raising the paw withdrawal threshold 336%. The anxiolytic-antidepressant behaviour was contingent on there being pain to relieve. That is precisely the confound a psychotropic claim has to exclude, and the animal literature says it cannot be excluded.

Psychiatry has already run the clean version of this experiment, and it caps the upside. Buprenorphine/samidorphan was purpose-built to test μ-opioid modulation as an antidepressant with the reward arm blocked: proof-of-concept gave HAM-D17 p=0.032 and MADRS p=0.054 after one week; the phase 3 pair split, FORWARD-5 hitting (MADRS-6 −1.5, p=0.018; MADRS-10 −1.9, p=0.026) and FORWARD-4 missing (MADRS-10 −1.8, p=0.109); pooled Hedges' g was 0.22, and across 11 studies of buprenorphine in depression (N=1699) the pooled effect was g=0.17 (95% CI 0.05–0.29). Roughly two MADRS points is the ceiling that a rationally designed opioid modulator achieved with dedicated trials and no abuse liability. Tramadol cannot plausibly beat that while carrying dependence, seizures and a CYP2D6 lottery — and it has never been asked to try.

## The confound that makes any bedside mood benefit unreadable

This is the part worth carrying into clinic. Abrupt cessation of tramadol can precipitate **two** withdrawal syndromes simultaneously — an opioid withdrawal and an SNRI discontinuation syndrome. So a patient stabilised on tramadol who becomes dysphoric, anxious, insomniac and agitated on stopping it looks exactly like a patient whose antidepressant has been removed, and a patient who feels better on restarting it looks exactly like a treatment response. The same mechanism generates the case literature in both directions: mood improvement on initiation, and mood deterioration including suicide reported on tramadol, both from single cases. The historical framing this drug gets recruited into — the "opium cure", the claim that methadone, buprenorphine and tramadol have durable rapid antidepressant properties — rests substantially on this indistinguishability. The question "is tramadol an antidepressant?" was asked in print in 1999 and has never been answered by a trial designed to answer it.

## The OCD strand — a real pharmacological hook, never tested

Worth knowing because it is the one place the *opioid* half of the molecule made a specific prediction. Naloxone acutely exacerbates obsessive-compulsive symptoms, which implies endogenous opioid tone is doing something in OCD; a μ-opioid receptor A118G transmission study in 51 OCD trios found a trend confined to the comorbid-tic group (χ²=3.6, p=0.065). Tramadol was reported effective in treatment-refractory OCD in an open-label pilot (enrolment, dose and Y-BOCS change `[unverified]` — the record carries no indexed abstract) and in a single case of rapid remission on tramadol added to fluoxetine, in 1999. In animals, atypical opioids with combined serotonergic and opioid properties suppressed 5-HT2A-mediated head-twitch behaviour in a naloxone-reversible way, which is a coherent story about compulsions.

Twenty-seven years later there is not one randomised controlled trial of tramadol in OCD, registered or published, anywhere. A hypothesis this cheap to test and this long untested is not a promising lead; it is an abandoned one, and the reason is almost certainly that nobody will fund an opioid for an illness treated with SSRIs.

## Where the claim stops being merely wrong and starts being harmful

The three populations you asked me to cover fail differently, and the numbers matter more than the direction.

**Thai adult OPD on 2–4 psychotropics.** This is where the SNRI-with-an-opioid framing earns its keep. Tramadol plus a serotonergic antidepressant is a documented serotonin-toxicity combination — the case literature includes a woman on venlafaxine and tramadol meeting Hunter criteria with tremor, hyperreflexia, diarrhoea, tachycardia, hypertension and fever after a dose increase, resolving within 24 hours of stopping both. Layered on that, M1 formation depends on CYP2D6, so the analgesia a given patient gets is genotype-dependent while the serotonergic parent drug accumulates regardless — and the SSRIs most likely to be co-prescribed (paroxetine, fluoxetine) are CYP2D6 inhibitors, which pushes the balance toward the monoaminergic effects and away from the opioid ones. The interaction is not exotic; it is the default prescription in a Thai OPD.

**Inpatient and consultation-liaison.** Note that the *only* positive human mood data in existence come from exactly this setting — perioperative, opioid-comparator, 48 hours. That is a trap, not a licence: the finding cannot be separated from better analgesia and from what the comparator did, and the endpoint was prevention in non-depressed women, not treatment of anyone's depression. If a surgical team proposes tramadol partly "for her mood", the evidence does not support the mood claim and the serotonergic risk is live if she is on an antidepressant.

**Adolescents and young adults.** Here the harm profile is not theoretical. Among 525 pure tramadol poisonings, 68.4% were deliberate self-poisoning and 27.8% abuse, with 21.7% carrying a history of tramadol abuse; apnoea occurred in 3.6%, at a mean ingested dose of 2125 ± 1360 mg versus 1383 ± 1088 mg in those without (p<0.001). Tramadol accounted for 7.7% of opioid prescriptions dispensed to young children in a 4-million-prescription US analysis in which 45.6% of paediatric opioid prescriptions were high-risk on at least one metric. Any framing of tramadol as mood-helping is, in this group, a framing of a dependence-forming drug as a psychotropic.

One correction to the folklore, in the other direction, because it is the kind of thing worth getting right. The seizure risk at **therapeutic** doses is weaker than the teaching implies: pooled event rate 3% (95% CI 2–3%) at therapeutic dosing versus 37% (95% CI 12–62%) in abusers and 38% (95% CI 27–49%) in poisoning across 51 studies and 101,770 patients; a nested case-control of 96,753 seizure cases against 888,540 controls found no excess versus codeine on a broad outcome definition (OR 1.03, 95% CI 0.93–1.15) and an excess only on a hospital-diagnosis definition (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.11–1.79); a Korean case-case-time-control of 2523 incident cases found no association (adjusted OR 0.94, with a case-crossover OR of 1.19 and a control-crossover OR of 1.27 — the latter showing exactly the time trend that naive designs mistake for signal). The seizure hazard is a **dose and misuse** phenomenon, not an intrinsic property of a therapeutic dose. Male sex carried a pooled OR of 2.24 (95% CI 1.80–2.77), and naloxone administration was not associated with seizures (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.15–1.49). So: fear the dose and the patient's pattern of use, not the molecule at 50 mg.

## What would change the verdict

Very little is coming, and that is itself informative. No trial of tramadol for major depression, OCD or an anxiety disorder is registered anywhere — not completed, not ongoing, not planned. The registry sweep returns ten studies at this intersection in total, of which the only forward-looking one is a 90-patient comparison of preoperative dexmedetomidine against tramadol on anxiety, pain and haemodynamics in haemorrhoidectomy, reading out around mid-2026 — a perioperative anxiolysis endpoint that cannot settle a psychotropic claim. The 1230-patient caesarean trial has no results posted to the registry; its published subgroup is what exists.

Three things would actually move this. A placebo-controlled trial of tramadol in a depressed population with a taper-controlled discontinuation phase, which would separate antidepressant action from withdrawal prevention — nobody is running it. Replication of the EPDS ≥10 subgroup as a *pre-specified primary* endpoint in high-risk women, which would convert the hypothesis into an effect. Or a within-patient pain-controlled design that reproduces the animal finding in humans: if the mood benefit disappears in patients without pain, the question closes. Absent those, the verdict does not move, and it is fair to treat it as closed for practical purposes.

**What I would repeat at a case conference:** tramadol is a weak SNRI with an opioid metabolite, so treat it as a serotonergic drug for interaction purposes and as an opioid for dependence purposes — and note that its best human mood evidence is a failed primary endpoint rescued by a subgroup. **What I would stop repeating:** that tramadol has antidepressant properties, that the "opioid antidepressant" literature supports using it that way, and that a therapeutic dose is intrinsically proconvulsant.
