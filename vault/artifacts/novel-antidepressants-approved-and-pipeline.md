## Definition & nosology

"Novel antidepressant" is a clinical phrase with no regulatory referent, and the vagueness matters, because the two things it can mean pull in opposite directions. It can mean *molecular* novelty — a new chemical entity acting on the monoamine transporters and receptors that antidepressants have acted on since imipramine — or *mechanistic* novelty, a drug whose primary target sits outside the monoamine system altogether. The approval cohort of the last fifteen years contains both kinds, in roughly equal number, and conflating them is how a field talks itself into believing more has changed than has.

The operational boundary used here is regulatory rather than pharmacological: agents the FDA approved for a depressive disorder from 2009 onward, plus the agents now in phase 3. That cohort is countable. A systematic review of the FDALabel database and the trial registry found **15 medications approved for depressive disorders between 2009 and April 2025**, alongside **18 medications then in phase 3** — and noted that **every oral antidepressant approved in that window is dosed once daily**, a formulation convergence that is itself a finding about what the field decided patients would tolerate.

Within the approved group, the mechanistically novel agents are esketamine (2019), brexanolone (2019), dextromethorphan–bupropion (2022), and zuranolone (2023). The monoamine-based agents are vilazodone (2011), levomilnacipran (2013), vortioxetine (2013), and gepirone extended-release (2023) — new molecules, familiar targets, with gepirone the interesting edge case: a selective 5-HT1A agonist without reuptake inhibition is a genuinely different way to work inside the serotonin system without leaving it. Two antipsychotics also acquired MDD indications in the window, cariprazine as adjunct in 2022 and lumateperone as adjunct on 6 November 2025, which is how the pharmacological boundary of "antidepressant" erodes in practice even when it holds in principle.

The nosological consequence is the more important half of this section, and it is easy to miss. **Not one of the four mechanistically novel agents was approved for major depressive disorder as DSM-5 defines it.** Esketamine was approved for treatment-resistant depression and for depressive symptoms in MDD with acute suicidal ideation or behaviour; brexanolone and zuranolone for postpartum depression only. The novel agents entered the formulary through indications *narrower* than the diagnosis — a resistance stratum, a peripartum window, an acute symptom state. That is a reversal of the twentieth-century pattern, in which a drug was approved for depression and clinicians then discovered its niches. Here the niche came first, and the diagnosis fractured to accommodate the pharmacology. Dextromethorphan–bupropion is the one exception, approved for MDD broadly, and gepirone likewise within the monoamine group.

Classification systems have been catching up to this. The indication-based label "antidepressant" is a poor description of a drug that treats one two-week postpartum episode by positive allosteric modulation of GABA-A receptors, and the pharmacology-based nomenclature effort — describing agents by target and mode of action rather than by the disease they were first sold for — exists precisely because the old label stopped carrying information. The authoritative pharmacological review of treatment-resistant depression makes the same argument from the drug-discovery side: advances in mechanism have themselves forced a reclassification of antidepressants, because roughly **30%** of patients are treatment-resistant and that residual is defined by mechanisms — glutamatergic, immune-inflammatory, neurotrophic, epigenetic — the monoamine label cannot name.

## Epidemiology

Two epidemiological facts created this drug cohort: depression is growing as a burden, and a large, expensive, well-delineated fraction of it does not respond to what already exists.

On the first, the Global Burden of Disease 2021 analysis places depressive disorders among the three Level 3 causes whose age-standardised DALY rates rose most between 2010 and 2021, at **+16.4% (95% UI 11.9–21.3)** — behind anxiety disorders (+16.7%) and ahead of diabetes (+14.0%) — in a period when age-standardised all-cause DALY rates *fell* by 14.2% before the pandemic reversed the trend. A rising burden inside a falling one is the kind of signal that reallocates research budgets.

On the second, the treatment-resistant fraction has been sized carefully in the United States using a bottom-up synthesis of four claims databases plus a national survey. Of an estimated **8.9 million US adults with medication-treated MDD, 2.8 million (30.9%) met the common definition of treatment-resistant depression**. The cost asymmetry is the part that moved payers: the total annual burden of medication-treated MDD was **$92.7 billion**, of which **$43.8 billion (47.2%) was attributable to TRD** — and TRD accounted for **56.6% ($25.8 billion) of the healthcare burden** and 47.7% ($8.7 billion) of the unemployment burden. A third of patients generating half the cost is the commercial case for an expensive TRD-specific drug, stated in one line.

The proportion is not universal, and the difference is instructive rather than merely local. In a Thai tertiary public hospital, a combined chart-review and survey study of **500 antidepressant-treated MDD patients and their caregivers found TRD in 19.6%** — meaningfully below the US 30.9%, with TRD associated with younger age, younger onset, lower BMI, history of suicide attempt or self-harm, and frequent smoking. Whether the gap reflects true differences in illness, in prescribing intensity, or in what counts as an adequate trial is unresolved, but it means the 30.9% figure is a claims artefact of one health system as much as a fact about depression, and the market sizing built on it travels less well than it appears to.

Postpartum depression, the other indication that acquired dedicated drugs, affects roughly one in eight deliveries; the six randomised trials of neurosteroid modulators in the condition enrolled **674 women in total**, all in the USA — a small evidence base for two approvals, and a limitation §7 returns to.

## Etiology & pathophysiology — the mechanistic rationale

The search for novel antidepressants did not begin because monoamine drugs fail. It began because of a ceiling that the definitive comparative synthesis quantified precisely: across **522 trials and 116,477 participants**, all 21 antidepressants beat placebo, with odds ratios for response running from **2.13 (95% CrI 1.89–2.41) for amitriptyline down to 1.37 (1.16–1.63) for reboxetine**. Those are real effects and unimpressive ones, achieved after weeks of latency, and only **agomelatine (OR 0.84, 0.72–0.97) and fluoxetine (0.88, 0.80–0.96)** were better tolerated than placebo by dropout. A ceiling that low, reached that slowly, is what a novel mechanism is supposed to break.

**Glutamate and rapid plasticity** is the mechanism that actually did break it, and it is the best-characterised story in the chapter. Ketamine's antidepressant action is not adequately described by NMDA receptor blockade; the downstream account is a burst of glutamate release, AMPA receptor activation, BDNF and mTOR-mediated signalling, expression of synaptic proteins, and measurable synaptogenesis in prefrontal pyramidal neurons. The striking convergence is that the classical psychedelics — psilocybin, LSD, DMT — arrive at the same downstream endpoint through an entirely different primary receptor, 5-HT2A, producing the same synaptic, structural, and functional changes in the same cells. Two receptor systems, one plasticity endpoint. This is the single most productive idea in the field: it reframes the target from a neurotransmitter deficit to a capacity for adaptive rewiring, and it predicts (correctly, so far) that a drug can be given once or twice and have effects that outlast its own pharmacokinetics.

**GABA-A neurosteroid modulation** rests on a narrower and more physiological rationale. Allopregnanolone, a progesterone metabolite and positive allosteric modulator of both synaptic and extrasynaptic GABA-A receptors, rises through pregnancy and falls abruptly at delivery; the hypothesis is that postpartum depression is in part a withdrawal state in a GABAergic system that has adapted to high neurosteroid tone. Brexanolone is allopregnanolone itself, given intravenously; zuranolone is an orally bioavailable analogue. The mechanism explains the indication's narrowness, and it also predicts the class's central clinical oddity — a two-week course rather than chronic dosing — because a withdrawal state is a bounded event, not a permanent lesion.

**Kappa opioid receptor antagonism** is the field's most instructive failure, and it earns space here for that reason rather than despite it. Dynorphin signalling at KOR suppresses mesolimbic dopamine release, which makes KOR blockade a mechanistically clean approach to anhedonia specifically. The two lead compounds were pharmacologically excellent: **navacaprant shows 300-fold selectivity for KOR over the mu receptor and occupies 87–95% of KORs at clinically relevant doses; aticaprant shows 30-fold selectivity and 73–94% occupancy**. Near-complete engagement of a well-reasoned target, in the symptom domain the target governs, and — as §10 records — no efficacy in phase 3. This is the strongest available caution against inferring clinical benefit from mechanistic plausibility plus receptor occupancy, and it is a caution the chapter's remaining pipeline mechanisms have not yet answered.

The other mechanisms currently in late development are, in decreasing order of evidential support: **AMPA receptor potentiation** (NBI-1065845), **orexin-2 receptor antagonism** in depression with insomnia (seltorexant), **Kv7 potassium channel opening**, which reduces excitability in mesolimbic circuits (azetukalner), **sigma-1 agonism** combined with NMDA channel blockade (the dextromethorphan component of the approved combination), and **immune-inflammatory targeting**, which remains stratification-dependent and has not produced an approved agent.

## Clinical features & course — what these drugs are aimed at

The novel agents are not aimed at "depression". They are aimed at four specific features of its presentation and course, and knowing which feature a drug was built for predicts more about its clinical use than knowing its receptor.

**Speed of onset** is the feature the glutamatergic and neurosteroid agents were designed around, and it is the one where the numbers are least ambiguous. In the pooled HUMMINGBIRD analyses of brexanolone, **median time to HAMD-17 response was 24 hours versus 36 hours on placebo (p = 0.0265)**, with an **hour-60 cumulative response rate of 81.4% versus 67.3%**. Esketamine separates from placebo at **day 2**: standardised mean difference **−0.30 (95% CI −0.47 to −0.12)** for MADRS reduction 24 hours after the first dose in patients with suicidal ideation, and in the monotherapy trial a between-group difference of **−3.8 points (56 mg) and −3.4 points (84 mg)** at roughly 24 hours. Weeks-to-hours is a genuine change in the clinical unit of time, and it is what makes an acute-suicidality indication conceivable at all.

**Treatment resistance** defines the population for esketamine and for most of the pipeline. Its clinical texture — chronicity, functional collapse, high relapse — is why a maintenance rather than acute framing dominates the durability evidence in §9, and why the field's consensus definitional work (§5) was necessary before trials could be compared.

**Anhedonia** became a target in its own right, on the reasoning that reward-circuit deficits are poorly served by serotonergic drugs and are mechanistically tied to dynorphin–KOR signalling. Trials accordingly adopted the Snaith–Hamilton Pleasure Scale as a key secondary endpoint and, in the aticaprant programme, enrolled specifically for **moderate-to-severe anhedonia**. The domain survived; the drugs did not.

**The bounded peripartum episode** is the most distinctive course feature in the cohort. Postpartum depression has a defined onset window and — unlike MDD — a treatment model with a defined end: brexanolone as a single **60-hour intravenous infusion at 90 µg/kg/h**, zuranolone as a **once-daily 14-day oral course**. Both were approved on the strength of effects measured at day 30 to day 45. Nothing in either evidence base speaks to what happens at month six, which is a course question the approvals did not answer and §9 flags as the class's largest gap.

Finally, **insomnia** has emerged as a stratifying feature rather than a target: the seltorexant programme stratified patients by Insomnia Severity Index (**ISI ≥ 15 versus < 15**), on the premise that an orexin antagonist should work best where sleep disturbance is prominent. Solriamfetol is being developed for the mirror-image feature, excessive daytime sleepiness in MDD.

## Diagnosis & assessment — selecting patients and measuring effect

For a drug class defined by narrow indications, the assessment question is not "does this patient have depression" but "does this patient belong to the stratum the drug was approved in", and the honest answer is that the instruments for that are weaker than the approvals imply.

**Treatment resistance had no agreed operational definition when esketamine was approved.** A Delphi-method consensus exercise assembled international clinicians, academics, industry, regulators including EMA and BfArM staff, and a person with lived experience, and produced criteria for treatment-resistant and partially responsive depression intended specifically for regulatory-grade clinical trials — published in 2021, two years after the first TRD-specific approval. The sequence is worth stating plainly: the indication preceded its definition.

What follows from that is visible in the individual-patient-data re-analysis of the esketamine registration trials. Across **7 RCTs and 1,505 patients**, there was **no moderation of treatment effect by age or by level of resistance, and most enrolled patients had low-stage TRD** — that is, the trials that established a treatment-resistant indication were populated largely by patients at the shallow end of resistance, and depth of resistance did not predict who benefited. Resistance staging, in this evidence, is an enrolment criterion rather than a predictive test.

The measurement side has one number that deserves to be carried through the rest of the chapter. The pivotal esketamine trials were **designed against a 6.5-point MADRS difference as the threshold of clinical significance**. That figure is the yardstick the IPD meta-analysis then applied to the pooled result, and it is the reason a statistically significant finding could be judged clinically ambiguous (§7). Anyone reading a MADRS difference in this literature should hold 6.5 points in mind; most of the differences in this chapter, including those of two drugs that succeeded in phase 3, fall well below it.

Symptom-domain instruments have become endpoints rather than descriptors — the **SHAPS** for anhedonia, the **ISI** for insomnia stratification, the SF-36 and the Barkin Index of Maternal Functioning for function. This is progress in measurement, and it also creates the multiplicity problem §10 describes, where a drug misses on MADRS and is reported as positive on HAM-D17 and SHAPS.

Pharmacogenetics is directly load-bearing for part of this cohort rather than optional. The current CPIC guideline covers **CYP2D6, CYP2C19, CYP2B6, SLC6A4, and HTR2A** for serotonin reuptake inhibitors and includes vilazodone and vortioxetine among the serotonin modulators whose metabolism is affected. The point sharpens for dextromethorphan–bupropion, where **CYP2D6 inhibition is not a drug interaction but the design**: bupropion is included partly to inhibit CYP2D6 and raise dextromethorphan's bioavailability. In a CYP2D6 poor metaboliser that mechanism has nothing left to inhibit, and the pharmacokinetic premise of the combination changes. Genotype-stratified efficacy data for the combination are not available `[unverified]`.

## Differential & comparators — where these agents actually sit

The question a clinician faces is never "novel or not" but "this new agent or the cheap thing that already works", and the comparator evidence is less flattering to the cohort than the placebo-controlled evidence.

**Against the incumbent monoamine drugs**, the ceiling from §3 is also the floor a new agent must clear: response ORs of 1.37–2.13 versus placebo across 21 established antidepressants. Vortioxetine is the cohort member with the most direct comparisons, and they are unfavourable to the differentiation story. A systematic review of 19 RCTs found vortioxetine superior to placebo but, against SNRIs, **better tolerability (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.86–0.94, p < 0.001) with no significant difference in response (RR 0.91, 0.82–1.00, p = 0.06) or remission (RR 0.99, 0.81–1.20)** — and against SSRIs, no difference in either response (RR 1.08, 0.88–1.32) or remission (RR 1.00, 0.41–2.44). A 2025 meta-analysis of 16 RCTs (3,127 versus 3,102 patients) reached the same place: a small but significant advantage over placebo, efficacy similar to duloxetine and venlafaxine.

Within the monoamine subgroup, a Bayesian re-analysis of the FDA review documents for 16 antidepressants approved between 1987 and 2016 — using the regulator's own pre-marketing data, which is less exposed to publication bias than the journal literature — found strong evidence of efficacy for every drug **except bupropion and vilazodone**. Vilazodone is therefore the weakest-evidenced member of this approval cohort, on the least biased data available, and its position in practice should reflect that.

**Against the standard TRD comparators**, esketamine has the only adequate head-to-head in the cohort, and it wins. In ESCAPE-TRD, an open-label rater-blinded phase 3b trial of **676 patients** randomised to esketamine nasal spray or extended-release quetiapine, each added to an SSRI or SNRI, **remission at week 8 was 27.1% (91/336) versus 17.6% (60/340), p = 0.003**, and **no relapse through week 32 after week-8 remission was 21.7% versus 14.1%**. In the subgroup dosed according to US prescribing information (n = 636), the separation widened over time: **remission 28.3% versus 18.6% at week 8 (p = 0.005), and 55.7% versus 36.3% at week 32 (p < 0.001)**. This is the cohort's strongest comparative claim. It is also open-label against a sedating comparator, which is exactly the design in which an unblinded expectancy effect would look like this.

A meta-analysis assembling **12 pivotal trials and 4,276 patients** compared esketamine and atypical antipsychotics indirectly and produced a more careful verdict: relative to their own control arms, the mean difference in response rates was **25% for esketamine versus 9% for the antipsychotics**, a **16% advantage (p = 0.0004)** — but the difference-of-differences in MADRS reduction was **1.87 points in esketamine's favour and not statistically significant (95% CI −4.49 to 0.74, p = 0.16)**. The same analysis found the *control* arms of the esketamine trials improved 7.57 points more than the control arms of the antipsychotic trials (p < 0.0001), which is a warning that these two literatures were run in different populations and should not be pooled casually.

**In older adults**, the relevant comparator is not a novel agent at all. The OPTIMUM trial randomised **619 adults aged 60 or older** with treatment-resistant depression and found aripiprazole augmentation improved well-being by **2.79 points more than switching to bupropion (95% CI 0.56–5.02, p = 0.014)**, with **remission in 28.9% (aripiprazole augmentation), 28.2% (bupropion augmentation), and 19.3% (switch to bupropion)**; in step 2, lithium augmentation and a switch to nortriptyline were indistinguishable (**remission 18.9% versus 21.5%**). A generic antipsychotic augmentation achieving 28.9% remission in genuinely resistant older patients is the benchmark any novel agent must beat in this population, and none has been tested against it.

## Treatment — the approved agents

### Glutamatergic agents

**Esketamine (intranasal)** is the cohort's most consequential approval and its most contested. The regulatory arc runs from adjunctive treatment of treatment-resistant depression in March 2019, through depressive symptoms in MDD with acute suicidal ideation or behaviour in 2020, to **monotherapy for treatment-resistant depression on 21 January 2025** — the first monotherapy approval in TRD.

The monotherapy trial is the newest primary evidence and the cleanest design in the programme: a phase 4 double-blind placebo-controlled trial at 51 US outpatient centres, **378 patients** randomised 1:1:2 to fixed-dose esketamine 56 mg, 84 mg, or placebo twice weekly for four weeks after an antidepressant-free period of two weeks or more, with a baseline mean MADRS of 37.3. At day 28 the least-squares mean differences versus placebo were **−5.1 points (95% CI −7.91 to −2.33) for 56 mg and −6.8 (−9.48 to −4.07) for 84 mg**, both p < 0.001, with **observed effect sizes of 0.48 and 0.63**; **remission at four weeks was 22.5% versus 7.6%**. Separation was present at 24 hours. Treatment-emergent adverse events across both doses were nausea **24.8%**, dissociation **24.3%**, dizziness **21.7%**, and headache **19.0%**.

The adjunctive evidence, re-examined at the level of individual patients, reads differently — and this is the single most important recent finding about the drug. An independent registered individual-patient-data meta-analysis obtained data through the Yale Open Data Access project for **7 RCTs and 1,505 patients**. Across the **five initiation trials of esketamine plus an oral antidepressant, the MADRS difference at four weeks was −2.94 points (95% CI −5.39 to −0.48), GRADE moderate certainty** — statistically significant, and **less than half the 6.5-point threshold the pivotal trials were designed around**. The continuation trial showed a real durability benefit (**relapse HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.26–0.57**), though the authors could not address FDA concerns about one study centre because of data-privacy restrictions. The monotherapy signal was larger (**MD −6.32, −8.62 to −4.03**) but available only as aggregate data and flagged for possible selection bias and unblinding. On harms, esketamine increased **sedation (RR 3.70, 2.02–6.78)**, **dissociation (RR 2.36, 2.10–2.65)**, and adverse events overall (**IRR 3.91, 2.37–6.45**), with **no increase in serious adverse events (IRR 1.35, 0.54–3.40)**. The authors' conclusion — an advantage over placebo that is "statistically significant but small", of unclear clinical relevance given the adverse-event burden — is the most defensible summary of the adjunctive indication currently available. A separate meta-analysis reaches compatible numbers from the aggregate literature: **MADRS SMD −0.24 (−0.38 to −0.09)** at day 28 without suicidal ideation, **−0.30 (−0.47 to −0.12)** at day 2 with it, **relapse RR 0.60 (0.45–0.80)**, and increased dizziness (**RR 3.55, 2.37–5.32**) and nausea (**RR 3.88, 2.10–7.18**).

Long-term tolerability is better documented than for anything else in the cohort. The open-label SUSTAIN-3 extension enrolled **1,148 participants** from six phase 3 parent studies, accumulating a **mean cumulative maintenance exposure of 31.5 months (median 37.7) and 2,769 patient-years**; adverse events at ≥20% were headache, dizziness, nausea, dissociation, somnolence, and nasopharyngitis, and the MADRS reduction achieved during induction persisted through optimisation and maintenance.

The practical constraint on esketamine is not efficacy but delivery. The REMS programme requires administration in a certified setting with at least **two hours** of post-dose observation and forbids take-home dosing, so induction means twice-weekly supervised visits for four weeks. Reported self-pay costs run **$600–1,200 per session**, with drug cost up to about **$6,785 for the first month**; providers describe inadequate reimbursement for monitoring time, prior authorisations taking one to four weeks, and few certified centres outside metropolitan areas. For a drug whose adjunctive effect is under three MADRS points, that delivery burden is not a footnote — it is most of the clinical decision.

**Dextromethorphan–bupropion (45 mg/105 mg)**, an NMDA receptor antagonist and sigma-1 agonist paired with a CYP2D6 inhibitor that raises its own partner's bioavailability, was approved for MDD in August 2022 and is the cohort's only mechanistically novel agent with a full MDD indication. In the phase 3 GEMINI trial, **327 patients** with baseline MADRS of about 33 were randomised to the combination or placebo for six weeks; **the least-squares mean MADRS change was −15.9 versus −12.0 points**, a placebo-adjusted difference of **3.9 points**, with separation reported from week 1. The phase 2 ASCEND trial used the harder comparator — sustained-release bupropion alone — in a small efficacy population of 80 patients, and supported the combination's advantage over its own active component. A network meta-analysis of the two trials reports **remission rates of 46.5% and 39.5% for the combination versus 16.2% for bupropion alone and 17.3% for placebo**, with adverse events more frequent (**72.9% versus 64.6%**) but no significant excess of discontinuation; dizziness, nausea, headache, and somnolence dominate. Two caveats belong with those numbers: the pivotal evidence base is two trials, and the CYP2D6-dependent design creates the metaboliser-status question raised in §5.

### GABA-A neurosteroid modulators

**Brexanolone** is allopregnanolone delivered as a **60-hour intravenous infusion titrated to 90 µg/kg/h**, approved for postpartum depression in March 2019 — the first drug ever approved specifically for the condition. The pooled HUMMINGBIRD programme (one phase 2 and two phase 3 trials; **102 patients on brexanolone 90 versus 107 on placebo**) showed faster response as described in §4: median HAMD-17 response at **24 versus 36 hours (p = 0.0265)** and hour-60 cumulative response of **81.4% versus 67.3%**, with parallel gains on the anxiety and insomnia subscales.

The Cochrane review of the whole neurosteroid class in postnatal depression — **six RCTs, 674 women** — is considerably less encouraging about the intravenous agents than the sponsor analyses. Pooling three intravenous studies (267 women), **depression response at 30 days was RR 1.24 (95% CI 0.74–2.06) and remission RR 1.18 (0.59–2.38), both low-certainty with substantial heterogeneity (I² 78% and 73%)**, and depression severity **MD −4.22 points (−8.46 to 0.02)** — that is, confidence intervals crossing no effect. Acceptability was worse than placebo: **dropout RR 2.77 (1.22–6.26), moderate certainty**. Excessive sedation and loss of consciousness drive a REMS requiring continuous monitoring throughout the infusion, which together with a multi-day inpatient stay explains why uptake has been limited and why an oral successor was the commercial priority.

**Zuranolone (50 mg once daily for 14 days)** is that successor, approved in August 2023 **for postpartum depression only**. In the same Cochrane analysis the oral evidence is the stronger half: pooling two trials (349 women), **depression response at day 45 was RR 1.26 (1.03–1.55), remission RR 1.65 (1.22–2.22), and severity MD −3.79 points (−5.60 to −1.97), all moderate certainty**, with **maternal adverse events increased (RR 1.24, 1.03–1.48)**, somnolence most frequent, and acceptability no different from placebo (**RR 0.95, 0.50–1.81**). One trial found improved maternal functioning on the Barkin Index (**MD 7.20, 1.42–12.98**), low certainty. An integrated SF-36 analysis across three MDD trials and one PPD trial (**1,003 patients**) found benefit on functioning and well-being sustained to day 42 in responders.

The MDD story is where zuranolone's limits show. A meta-analysis of six studies (**1,707 participants**) found the HAM-D reduction versus placebo at day 15 was **−4.06 points (−4.25 to −3.87) in postpartum depression but only −2.40 points (−3.07 to −1.63) in MDD** — statistically significant and, in the authors' judgement, not clinically significant for MDD. The FDA reached the same conclusion in substance, approving the postpartum indication and issuing a complete response letter for major depressive disorder. Europe followed the same logic on a two-year delay: the CHMP adopted a positive opinion on 25 July 2025 and the **European Commission authorised zuranolone for postpartum depression on 17 September 2025**, making it the first and only PPD-indicated treatment in the EU, again with no MDD indication.

One structural caveat governs this entire subsection. Cochrane records that **all six neurosteroid trials were sponsored by the manufacturers of the drugs, which "appear to have had a considerable role in the design and conduct" of the studies**, all were conducted in the USA, sample sizes ranged from 21 to 196, and none measured quality of life or child outcomes. For a class whose patients are postpartum women and whose infants are exposed through breast milk, the absence of child outcomes across the entire evidence base is the finding, not a limitation to be noted in passing.

### Serotonergic and monoamine-based agents

**Vortioxetine (5–20 mg)**, approved in 2013, is a serotonin reuptake inhibitor with additional 5-HT1A agonist, 5-HT1B partial agonist, and 5-HT3/5-HT7/5-HT1D antagonist activity — the "multimodal" claim. Its comparative efficacy is covered in §6: superior to placebo, indistinguishable from SSRIs and SNRIs on response and remission, better tolerated than SNRIs. The differentiating claim has always been **procognitive effect**, and the evidence there is genuinely contested rather than settled. The 2025 meta-analysis of 16 RCTs found **cognitive improvement on the Digit Symbol Substitution Test that was small and not statistically significant**, alongside a non-significant trend toward fewer sexual adverse events; an umbrella review of **35 systematic reviews and 278 meta-analyses**, assessed with AMSTAR-2 and GRADE, judged the evidence on cognitive performance and depressive symptoms "notably robust" relative to placebo. These two syntheses disagree, and the disagreement is largely about comparator: robust against placebo, unproven against an active antidepressant. The defensible clinical position is that vortioxetine's cognitive advantage over another antidepressant is not established, while its tolerability advantage over SNRIs is.

**Vilazodone (2011)** and **levomilnacipran (2013)** add little that the incumbents did not offer. Vilazodone combines serotonin reuptake inhibition with 5-HT1A partial agonism; levomilnacipran is an SNRI with relatively greater noradrenergic potency. On the FDA's own pre-marketing data, vilazodone is one of only two agents in a 16-drug Bayesian analysis lacking strong evidence of efficacy (§6). Neither has a demonstrated niche.

**Gepirone extended-release (20–80 mg)**, approved in September 2023, is the **first oral selective 5-HT1A receptor agonist** for MDD — a mechanism inside the serotonin system that avoids reuptake inhibition entirely. Phase 3 trials established efficacy across that dose range; common adverse effects are dizziness, nausea, headache, fatigue, and insomnia. The published appraisals are candid about where it belongs: its **antidepressant effect size is modest relative to other monoamine-based antidepressants**, and its likely value is in patients who cannot tolerate SSRI-related sexual dysfunction or weight gain — a tolerability niche rather than an efficacy advance. QT prolongation constrains dosing and combination.

**Adjunctive antipsychotics** complete the approval record without belonging to this review: cariprazine as adjunctive treatment of MDD in December 2022, and lumateperone as adjunctive treatment on 6 November 2025 — the latter extending a drug already approved for schizophrenia and for bipolar I and II depression into a fourth indication. Their relevance here is comparative (§6): they are the cheap, oral, unmonitored option against which every novel agent's delivery burden is judged.

## Special populations

**Children and adolescents** are the cohort's largest void. No agent in this review is approved below age 18 for depression, and the background evidence explains the reluctance as much as the caution does. A Cochrane network meta-analysis of **26 studies** of newer-generation antidepressants in paediatric MDD found no usable data for its two primary outcomes and, for clinician-rated symptoms on the CDRS-R, effects that were mostly "small and unimportant": high-certainty **paroxetine MD −1.43 (−3.90 to 1.04)** and **vilazodone MD −0.84 (−3.03 to 1.35)**; moderate-certainty **sertraline −3.51 (−6.99 to −0.04)**, **fluoxetine −2.84 (−4.12 to −1.56)**, **escitalopram −2.62 (−5.29 to 0.04)**; and low-certainty **vortioxetine MD +0.60 (−2.52 to 3.72)** — a point estimate in the wrong direction. Two novel-agent paediatric programmes are now running: intranasal esketamine 84 mg in adolescents with MDD and acute suicidal ideation or behaviour, and a Japanese vortioxetine trial in ages 12–17.

**Older adults** are underserved by a different mechanism — the comparator is good and untested against. OPTIMUM's 28.9% remission with aripiprazole augmentation (§6) sets the bar, and the esketamine IPD meta-analysis found **no effect moderation by age**, which is reassuring about generalisability and silent about the specific geriatric risks of dissociation and blood-pressure elevation in patients with cerebrovascular disease. Two trials now address the gap directly: lumateperone in late-life treatment-resistant depression and a head-to-head of esketamine versus aripiprazole augmentation in elderly TRD.

**The perinatal population** is the only one with dedicated approvals, and the evidence is correspondingly specific and correspondingly incomplete. Efficacy and harms are in §7; what remains for this section is what the trials did not measure. Across all six neurosteroid RCTs, **no study measured quality of life, and none measured parenting- or child-related outcomes**. Zuranolone carries warnings for driving impairment and somnolence requiring a delay of at least 12 hours after dosing before driving, and recommends contraception during and after treatment; infant exposure data through breast milk are limited, and the balance of continuing breastfeeding through a 14-day course is not settled by trial evidence `[unverified]`. Reviews of postpartum pharmacotherapy note the broader problem this class sits inside: the antidepressant evidence in PPD generally rests on few, small, short trials.

**Bipolar depression** is outside the locked scope, and the boundary is nonetheless worth marking, because molecules cross it. Lumateperone is approved in bipolar I and II depression; navacaprant was tested in bipolar II depression; psilocybin bipolar II trials are running. A drug's behaviour in unipolar MDD does not transfer, and the switch risk of rapid-acting agents in bipolar spectrum patients is not characterised in this cohort.

**Cardiac risk** deserves a specific note for the psychedelic pipeline rather than the approved agents. LSD and psilocybin share structural features with methysergide, pergolide, and fenfluramine, all of which raise the risk of cardiac fibrosis and valvulopathy through **5-HT2B** agonism on repeated exposure. The concern applies to sustained or microdosing regimens rather than one or two supervised doses, which is the model in phase 3 — but it is the reason a maintenance psychedelic strategy cannot be assumed safe by extrapolation from single-dose trials.

## Prognosis — durability and maintenance

Durability is where the cohort splits most cleanly, and it splits by dosing model rather than by mechanism.

**Chronically dosed agents have relapse-prevention evidence, and it is good.** For esketamine, the continuation trial in the IPD meta-analysis gives **relapse HR 0.38 (95% CI 0.26–0.57)** among patients in stable remission, and the aggregate meta-analysis puts the same effect at **RR 0.60 (0.45–0.80)**. ESCAPE-TRD's key secondary endpoint carries this into a head-to-head: **21.7% versus 14.1%** of all randomised patients achieved week-8 remission and stayed relapse-free through week 32, and in the US-label subgroup remission rose to **55.7% versus 36.3% by week 32** — the separation widening with time rather than decaying. SUSTAIN-3 shows the exposure is sustainable, with a mean 31.5 months of maintenance treatment across 2,769 patient-years and no new safety signal.

**Course-based agents have no maintenance evidence at all**, and this is the largest single gap in the chapter. Brexanolone's evidence ends at day 30, zuranolone's at day 45, with responder-level SF-36 benefit shown to day 42. What happens at three months, at six, or on relapse after a completed course — whether the course can be repeated, and with what cumulative risk — is unaddressed by every trial in the class. A treatment model that is bounded by design needs relapse data more than a chronic one does, not less.

**Single-dose psychedelics** occupy an awkward middle position where the durability claim is the whole proposition and the evidence is mostly uncontrolled. The Johns Hopkins cohort followed **24 of 27 randomised participants for 12 months** after two psilocybin sessions, with large decreases in GRID-HAMD scores throughout and **Cohen's d of 2.3–2.6** at the later timepoints — but with no control group beyond the initial waiting-list phase, so this measures the course of treated patients rather than an effect. The phase 3 evidence is better: COMPASS reports that participants achieving a clinically meaningful MADRS reduction maintained it **through at least week 26 after one or two doses**.

Across the whole cohort, the prognostic asymmetry is the practical message: the drugs with the most impressive acute effects have the least evidence about month six, and the drug with the best month-six evidence is the one that is hardest and most expensive to keep delivering.

## Controversies & future directions

**The field's central argument is about effect size, not statistical significance, and everyone is now arguing on the same axis.** Set the numbers side by side against the 6.5-point MADRS threshold the pivotal esketamine trials were designed around: adjunctive esketamine **−2.94** in IPD re-analysis; COMP360 psilocybin **−3.6** in its first phase 3 and **−3.8** in its second, both p < 0.001; azetukalner **−3.04, p = 0.14** at week 6 — a result its investigators described as clinically meaningful but not statistically significant, while reporting significance on HAM-D17 (**−13.3 versus −10.2, p = 0.04**), on SHAPS (**p = 0.046**), and on MADRS at week 1 (**−2.66, p = 0.047**). Against that background, DT120's reported **−8.1 points (p < 0.0001)** at week 6 is the outlier that would matter most if it replicates. Two things follow. First, several drugs now succeed statistically at magnitudes the field itself once defined as the minimum worth having. Second, when a primary endpoint misses and secondary endpoints hit, the resulting narrative is a multiplicity artefact until a confirmatory trial says otherwise.

**Blinding is not a technicality for drugs that produce dissociation or hallucinogenesis; it is the main threat to the whole literature.** The most uncomfortable single result in this chapter is a placebo-controlled fixed-order psilocybin trial with enhanced blinding procedures, in which depression and anxiety improved after both placebo and psilocybin **with no significant difference in the degree of change between conditions**, even though the psilocybin effect sizes were larger (**d 1.02–2.27 versus 0.65–0.99**). One small exploratory trial does not overturn two positive phase 3 programmes, but it demonstrates that the effect measured in an unblindable design is not cleanly attributable to the drug. A cross-sectional analysis of **40 ketamine, esketamine, and arketamine protocols** on ClinicalTrials.gov found exactly the systemic version of this: wide variability in blinding technique, many trials failing to mask perceptual effects, inconsistent handling of expectancy, and non-uniform safety monitoring for dissociation and abuse potential. ESCAPE-TRD's open-label design against a sedating comparator sits inside this same concern.

**The rising-placebo-response story, invoked constantly to explain failed trials, is not straightforwardly true.** An analysis of **121 studies published 2001–2015** found a significant *linear decrease* in placebo response over the period, together with a significant cubic pattern and wide year-to-year fluctuation in which placebo and drug response peaked and troughed together. Failed novel-agent trials need a better explanation than a placebo trend that the data do not support.

**FDA and EMA have diverged, and the pattern of divergence is informative.** Both approved esketamine for treatment-resistant depression. Both confined zuranolone to postpartum depression — the FDA with a complete response letter for MDD in 2023, Europe by authorising only the PPD indication in September 2025. Brexanolone's European status is not established from the sources consulted here `[unverified]`. Gepirone ER is a US approval without a corresponding European one `[unverified]`. Read together, the two regulators agreed on the substantive scientific judgement that separates this cohort's indications — that the neurosteroid effect is real in the peripartum window and too small in MDD — while differing by two years in timing. That agreement is more reassuring about the narrow indications than any single approval decision is.

**The mechanism graveyard of 2025–2026 is the chapter's hardest lesson.** Kappa opioid antagonism entered phase 3 with the best mechanistic credentials in psychiatry — a specific symptom domain, a validated circuit, 300-fold selectivity, 87–95% receptor occupancy — and produced nothing. Johnson & Johnson discontinued the five-trial **VENTURA** programme for aticaprant on **6 March 2025** for insufficient efficacy in adjunctive MDD with moderate-to-severe anhedonia, noting no new safety signal; the pivotal trial had completed at n = 513, and the long-term extension (n = 871) and a second efficacy trial (n = 101) were terminated. Neumora's navacaprant missed its primary and key secondary endpoints in **KOASTAL-1** in January 2025, then failed **KOASTAL-2** (change from baseline **−12.2 versus −12.0** for placebo) and **KOASTAL-3** (placebo numerically better) in June 2026, after which the programme was discontinued and the company cut roughly 35% of staff. Esmethadone's RELIANCE-II was terminated. The mechanistic rationale in §3 was not wrong about the biology; it was wrong that engaging the biology would move a depression rating scale. Every remaining pipeline mechanism should be read with that in mind.

**What is actually coming, and when.** Psilocybin is the nearest novel mechanism to approval: COMPASS has two positive phase 3 trials (**COMP005**, n = 255, and **COMP006**, n = 572), with durability to week 26 and adverse events described as mild-to-moderate and mostly resolving within 24 hours — and a magnitude that will be the review question. A second psilocybin phase 3 from Usona (n = 238) reads out in 2026, and a VA multi-site trial in veterans runs to 2030. Lysergide has moved fastest of the psychedelics on paper: **DT120** met its primary and all key secondary endpoints in the phase 3 Emerge study (n = 149) in June 2026 — placebo-adjusted MADRS **−8.1 at week 6**, **−14.2 at week 1**, **−7.3 at week 12**, response **35% versus 7%**, remission **24% versus 3%** — with a confirmatory phase 3 (Ascend) reading out in 2027. Outside the psychedelics, the AMPA potentiator NBI-1065845 has four phase 3 trials running to 2027–2029, the orexin-2 antagonist seltorexant has an adjunctive trial (n = 752) reading out at the end of 2026 and a monotherapy trial to 2028, and azetukalner has two phase 3 trials despite its equivocal phase 2. Deuterated psilocybin analogue CYB003 has two phase 3 trials, and a Chinese generic-equivalent dextromethorphan–bupropion phase 3 (n = 388) reads out in 2026.

**Access, not efficacy, is the binding constraint on the agents already approved.** Esketamine's REMS-mandated two-hour supervised observation, its $600–1,200 per-session self-pay cost, its ~$6,785 first month, its four-to-one-week prior-authorisation delays, and its scarcity outside cities together determine who receives a drug whose adjunctive benefit is under three MADRS points. Brexanolone's 60-hour monitored infusion had the same effect on its uptake. Any honest forecast of this cohort's clinical impact is a forecast about delivery models — proposed fixes include a dedicated monitoring billing code, price reduction, and shortening the observation window — not about pharmacology.

**The gaps worth naming, because they are where the next decade's evidence should go.** There is no head-to-head trial between any two novel agents. There is no relapse-prevention evidence for either course-based neurosteroid. There is no approved novel agent for anyone under 18, and the paediatric background evidence is weak enough that the caution is warranted rather than merely conservative. No approval in this cohort is biomarker-stratified, despite fifteen years of stratification rhetoric — the one programme that enrolled by symptom domain rather than diagnosis, aticaprant in anhedonia, is also the one that failed most decisively. And the two novel drugs with the most compelling acute effects are the two whose long-term consequences are least characterised.
