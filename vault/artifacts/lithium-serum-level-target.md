# What serum lithium level to target — and when to deliberately accept a lower one
*2026-07-29 · PubMed 24 · trials 5 · books 0*

> Locked (intent-lock): the decision is which serum lithium concentration to aim for in the
> patient in front of you; scope covers bipolar maintenance and acute mania, lithium
> augmentation in unipolar depression, and older adults; the counterweight is long-term organ
> harm **plus** acute toxicity **plus** the tolerability/adherence cost of a higher level.
> Assumed: "efficacy" means relapse prevention and acute anti-manic effect — not the
> anti-suicide, neuroprotective, or anti-dementia claims, which are handled only where they
> constrain what may be promised. All levels are 12-hour troughs; mmol/L and mEq/L are
> interchangeable for lithium.
> Skipped: children and adolescents (no consensus target exists); pregnancy and the peripartum,
> where the target moves for reasons unrelated to efficacy — carried as a boundary condition,
> not as scope.

## The target

**Aim for 0.60–0.80 mmol/L in adult bipolar maintenance, and treat 0.60 as a floor rather than
a midpoint** — in the largest level-stratified maintenance comparison (n=338 on lithium vs
n=404 on placebo), patients whose median level fell below 0.6 mEq/L had a time to any mood
recurrence no different from placebo, while 0.6–1.2 mEq/L beat both placebo and the low-level
group. Move up to **0.80–1.00** only for insufficient response with good tolerance, or when the
morbidity is predominantly manic; that is also the range in which acute mania is treated
(0.80–0.99 mEq/L in the only age-specific RCT, with a 79% response rate). Move down to
**0.40–0.60** deliberately — good response, poor tolerance, stable patient — and never by more
than 0.2 mmol/L in one step. For **lithium augmentation of an antidepressant**, the
placebo-controlled evidence rests on ≥800 mg/day or ≥0.5 mEq/L (OR 3.31, 95% CI 1.46–7.53,
NNT 3.7); a trial run at 0.3–0.4 is not a failed augmentation, it is an untested one. For
**older adults**, maintain at 0.40–0.60 (up to 0.70–0.80 at 65–79 years, ≤0.70 over 80) but
expect to go to 0.80–0.99 for acute mania, accepting more tremor and slow attainment.

**Do not promise** that a level "in range" prevents toxicity (chronic toxicity happens at
therapeutic levels when volume status or renal function shifts), that keeping the level low
protects the kidney (level is a weak and inconsistent predictor of eGFR decline; toxicity
episodes, age, and nephrotoxic co-prescriptions dominate), or that lithium at ~0.5 confers
anti-suicide protection (the 519-patient VA trial that tested exactly that was stopped for
futility at mean levels of 0.54/0.46 mEq/L).

**Confidence: moderate** for the adult 0.60–0.80 target, **moderate-low** for the geriatric
target and for every level-specific harm threshold — because no trial has randomised patients
to competing target levels since a 94-patient study in 1989, and none is planned.

## Where 0.6 comes from, and why the ceiling is soft

The floor is the better-evidenced half of the recommendation. The 1989 randomised comparison of
a "standard" target (0.8–1.0 mmol/L, group median 0.83) against a "low" target (0.4–0.6, median
0.54) in 94 patients found relapse in 6/47 (13%) versus 18/47 (38%), a 2.6-fold risk (95% CI
1.3–5.2) — with more tremor, diarrhoea, urinary frequency, weight gain, and metallic taste in
the standard arm. That trial is still the only randomised level-assignment study, and it
overstates the case: patients were moved abruptly from established levels into the low range,
and abrupt reduction of more than 0.2 mmol/L is itself associated with relapse, so part of the
"low-level" failure was a discontinuation effect rather than a dose–response effect. The
level-stratified post hoc of the 104-week quetiapine-withdrawal trial removes that artefact and
lands in the same place from the other direction: 0.6–1.2 mEq/L (n=201) prolonged time to any
mood event, to mania, and to depression versus both placebo and lithium <0.6 mEq/L (n=137),
while <0.6 was indistinguishable from placebo — with the honest caveat that level groups were
not randomised.

Above 0.75 the return flattens. The systematic review of long-term trials put the minimum
efficacious level at 0.4 mmol/L, optimal response at 0.6–0.75, and found that levels >0.75 add
no further protection against overall morbidity while improving control of inter-episode manic
symptoms — which is exactly the shape of the recommendation to reserve 0.8–1.0 for
manic-predominant illness. The pragmatic 330-patient maintenance trial that allowed a wide
0.4–1.0 mmol/L band still beat valproate monotherapy (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.51–1.00), showing the
band is tolerant of individual variation rather than the interval being precise.

The uncomfortable part: when the ISBD/IGSLI task force applied strict inclusion criteria to
this literature, **zero** studies qualified; after loosening them, seven were admitted, four of
which supported a threshold somewhere around 0.45–0.60 with benefit up to 0.80–1.00. The
0.60–0.80 standard is therefore a Delphi consensus (≥80% endorsement) built on a thin trial
base — which is why it is a target to titrate around, not a number to defend.

## What the top of the range costs — and what it does not

Three harms, and only two of them track the level.

**Tolerability tracks it, and that is the strongest argument for 0.6–0.8 as default.** Tremor,
polyuria, nausea, diarrhoea, weight gain, and cognitive dulling are the side effects patients
actually stop lithium for; the standard management sequence is to minimise the level within the
therapeutic range and adjust dose timing before adding antidotes. A level of 0.9 that produces
non-adherence delivers less prophylaxis than a level of 0.65 that is taken.

**Thyroid and parathyroid track it, cheaply and treatably.** Against placebo, clinical
hypothyroidism runs at OR 5.78 (95% CI 2.00–16.67) with TSH raised on average 4.00 IU/mL;
calcium rises 0.09 mmol/L and PTH 7.32 pg/mL. In a 4,752-patient incident-bipolar cohort,
lithium versus non-lithium mood stabilisers gave hypothyroidism aHR 2.00 (1.72–2.33), and
patients with higher mean levels had aHR 2.08 for hypothyroidism and 1.81 for hyperthyroidism;
the ROC-derived level cut-points were low — above roughly 0.50 mEq/L for both. Read those
cut-points as "thyroid harm begins inside the therapeutic range", not as a reason to dose below
it: the response to a rising TSH is thyroxine, not a lower lithium target.

**The kidney is where the level story is weakest, and where clinicians over-adjust.** The
meta-analysis of 385 studies found mean GFR reduced by only 6.22 mL/min (95% CI −14.65 to 2.20,
p=0.148), urinary concentrating ability down 15% of normal maximum (−158 mOsm/kg, 95% CI −230
to −87), and renal replacement therapy in 18/3,369 patients (0.5%). Two large registry analyses
do implicate the level — the Oxford laboratory series found stage 3 CKD at HR 1.93 (1.76–2.12)
with above-median lithium concentrations raising the risk of every adverse outcome, and the
Danish nationwide cohort found definite CKD at HR 3.65 (2.64–5.05) after ≥60 prescriptions —
but neither found an excess of **end-stage** disease attributable to lithium, and both authors
flag confounding by indication. The best-controlled longitudinal test contradicts them on the
mechanism: in 1,120 patients contributing 13,963 eGFR values over 12 years, annual decline was
1.3 mL/min/1.73 m² on lithium versus 0.9 on comparator drugs (not significant), and the
significant predictors were age, baseline eGFR, comorbidity, nephrotoxic co-prescriptions, and
**episodes of lithium toxicity** — not duration of exposure and not mean serum level. Supporting
data run the same way: 22 of 2,169 patients reached CKD stage 4 over a decade, and the hazard
did not fall across three cohorts even as guideline compliance improved and mean levels fell;
in 766 Korean patients, CKD stage 3 occurred in 0.8% on lithium versus 1.5% on valproate, with
median annual eGFR change −1.3 versus −1.1 mL/min/1.73 m² (p=0.389).

The synthesis that follows: **the difference between 0.65 and 0.85 is not what damages kidneys —
toxicity episodes, dehydration, age, and interacting drugs are.** Chasing a lower level for
renal protection buys a real loss of prophylaxis against a benefit that the best-designed study
could not detect. What does buy renal protection is preventing spikes: ACE inhibitors,
angiotensin receptor blockers, thiazides, and NSAIDs raise the level with no change in dose,
and each toxicity episode is independently associated with later CKD.

## The three patients where the target moves

**Unipolar depression, lithium added to an antidepressant.** The positive placebo-controlled
trials used at least 800 mg/day or levels ≥0.5 mEq/L for at least two weeks: pooled OR 3.31
(95% CI 1.46–7.53), relative response 2.14, absolute gain 27% (95% CI 9.8–44.2), NNT 3.7. Aim
into the standard maintenance band and give it a fair two to four weeks; the common failure is
an underdosed trial declared negative.

**Older adults.** Consensus, not evidence, sets maintenance lower — 0.40–0.60 mmol/L, with
0.70–0.80 permissible at 65–79 and ≤0.70 above 80 (the task force reached no formal consensus
here; this was the majority position). Acute mania is the documented exception: 224 patients
aged ≥60 randomised to lithium (target 0.80–0.99 mEq/L) or divalproex showed 9-week response of
79% versus 73%, greater YMRS improvement on lithium (3.90 points, 97.5% CI 1.71–6.09), similar
sedation, and more tremor on lithium — but only 57% reached the target concentration and 51%
had left the study by week 9. The clinical translation is that the geriatric target is limited
by attainability and tremor, not by an evidence of lost efficacy at the higher end.

**The patient whose kidney or tolerance is already loaded.** Sit at 0.40–0.60 and defend it:
reduce in steps of ≤0.2 mmol/L, hold a stable dose through intercurrent illness rather than
guessing, review every ACE inhibitor/ARB/thiazide/NSAID at each level check, and treat
established polyuria as a management problem (dose timing, fluid access) rather than an
automatic signal to lower the target. Once CKD is established, four studies found that
switching off lithium does not slow progression — so a declining eGFR is a nephrology
conversation about the whole medication list, not a reflex to abandon a level that is working.

## The number is only as good as the blood test that produced it

Most lithium samples are not drawn at 12 hours. An equation estimating the 12-hour level from
the measured level and the patient-reported time since the last dose deviated from the true
12-hour value by a mean of 10%, versus 25% for the raw measurement, and was closer to the
12-hour level in 99 of 102 (97%) samples drawn between 3 and 24 hours post-dose. A 0.15 mmol/L
sampling artefact is the width of the entire decision band being argued over above — so before
changing a dose for an out-of-range result, establish when the last dose was taken.

The corollary is that real-world levels drift below the target more often than clinicians
believe: in a 519-patient randomised trial with protocolised titration, mean concentrations at
three months were 0.54 mEq/L in bipolar disorder and 0.46 in major depression. If a trial cannot
hold 0.6, a clinic will not hold it by intention alone.

## Where this verdict inverts

- **Chronic toxicity does not wait for a high level.** The commonest poisoning pattern is
  chronic — intake exceeding elimination during volume depletion, intercurrent illness, or
  drug-induced renal impairment — and neurotoxicity, including irreversible cerebellar syndrome,
  can follow from a level that was therapeutic the week before. In sepsis, dehydration, or acute
  renal impairment, the correct action is to withhold and re-measure, not to trust the last
  result.
- **Pregnancy and the peripartum.** Lithium at any point in pregnancy carries any-malformation
  OR 1.81 (95% CI 1.35–2.41, NNH 33) and cardiac-anomaly OR 1.86 (1.16–2.96, NNH 71), with
  levels <0.64 mEq/L and doses <600 mg/day associated with more reactive newborns without excess
  cardiac malformation — the lowest effective level is right here, particularly in the first
  trimester. That logic does **not** extend to delivery: level/dose ratios did not change across
  the peripartum week in 233 measurements, so routine pre-delivery dose reduction only buys
  subtherapeutic levels when postpartum relapse risk peaks.
- **The long-stable excellent responder at 0.5.** Years of euthymia at a level below the band is
  not a reason to raise it; the level that has been holding is the evidence for that patient.

## What would change it

Very little, and that is itself the finding. The registry sweep found **no trial anywhere
randomising patients to competing lithium target levels** — the 0.60–0.80 recommendation will
not be tested prospectively, and the evidence base for the target will stay post hoc,
observational, and consensus-driven. What is registered instead concerns measurement and
adjacent questions: a Danish feasibility trial of the estimated-12-hour-level equation in
routine care (NCT07306039, n=200, completing December 2027); an observational study of basal
plasma level against suicidal events (NCT05409794, n=158, completing January 2026), the one
study that could move the "levels and suicide" claim; a lithium-versus-lamotrigine trial in
bipolar II (NCT06184581, n=200, 2027); and a trace-dose cognition trial (NCT06662526, n=250,
2029) which is about a different question entirely. One publication-bias flag: the amiloride
trial for lithium-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (NCT05044611, n=148) reached primary
completion on 2025-03-03 with no results posted — a negative result there would matter for the
patient with polyuria whose alternative is a lower target.
