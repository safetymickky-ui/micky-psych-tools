# Oral disease → psychiatric illness — is the link real, and at what strength?

*2026-08-04 · PubMed 22 · trials 7 · books 0*

> Assumed: all three outcome families carried (cognition/dementia, depression/anxiety, psychosis) rather than one, and both evidence tiers appraised — whether the association survives confounding, and whether intervening on the mouth changes the mind — with the verdict labelled by the tier it rests on. These were the open forks when the interview was stopped.
> Skipped: the reverse arrow (psychiatric illness and psychotropics wrecking the mouth) except where it is load-bearing as the confounder; screening protocols and service design; caries and oral cancer as outcomes.

## The verdict

**The association is real, replicated, and small — and it is not causal on current evidence.** Poor periodontal health and tooth loss track later cognitive decline and dementia at pooled RR ≈ 1.2–1.3, but every design capable of breaking the arrow returns null: Mendelian randomization across two independent periodontitis GWAS datasets, the life-course cohorts, and the one adequately sized human intervention. For depression the association does not even survive the move from cross-sectional to prospective designs (RR 1.07, 95% CI 0.95–1.21); for psychosis there is no usable evidence in this direction at all.

Hold and teach it as a **marker of accumulated disadvantage**, not a modifiable cause. Do not say that treating gum disease prevents dementia or depression — no trial supports it. Confidence: **moderate-high** that causality is unestablished; **moderate** that the observational association itself is genuine rather than pure measurement artifact. Not higher because no trial has yet tested periodontal treatment against a cognitive or mood endpoint at adequate size and duration — the absence is of evidence, and the field has not yet generated the evidence that would settle it.

## What the association is worth, and what makes it move

The observational tier is large and internally consistent, which is why it persuades. Pooling 46 studies screened from 7,363 records, periodontal disease carries RR 1.25 (95% CI 1.11–1.40) for cognitive disorders in cross-sectional analysis and RR 1.22 (1.10–1.36) for dementia. An independent pooling restricted to longitudinal designs — 47 studies — returns OR 1.23 (1.05–1.44) for cognitive decline and HR 1.21 (1.07–1.38) for dementia. A third, across 39 studies, gives RR 1.33 (1.13–1.55) and RR 1.22 (1.14–1.31). Three teams, three search strategies, the same answer to one decimal place.

Tooth loss behaves dose-dependently, which is the strongest single argument for the biological reading. Across 14 longitudinal studies (34,074 participants, 4,689 cases), more tooth loss carries RR 1.48 (1.18–1.87) for cognitive impairment and RR 1.28 (1.09–1.49) for dementia; each additional tooth lost adds 0.014 to the relative risk of cognitive impairment, and the edentulous run 1.54× and 1.40× respectively. Severity grading in the periodontal literature does the same thing — moderate disease RR 1.14 (1.07–1.22), severe RR 1.25 (1.18–1.32).

Then the estimate starts moving with things that have nothing to do with biology. In the same meta-regression, self-reported periodontal disease produces a *lower* risk than clinically classified disease (RR 0.77, 0.65–0.91 for cognitive decline; 0.86, 0.77–0.96 for dementia) — the exposure's measurement instrument, not the exposure, shifts the answer. And for every 10% more women in a study sample, the risk of cognitive decline rises by 34% (RR 1.34, 1.16–1.55). No proposed mechanism — inflammatory, masticatory, nutritional, or microbial — predicts that a cohort's sex composition should scale the effect of gum disease on the brain. That is the signature of residual confounding by whatever else tracks sample composition, and it is the point at which an umbrella review of this whole literature judged the overall strength of evidence "unfavourable and with methodological inconsistencies" despite 28 systemic diseases showing strong nominal associations.

One more number belongs here, because it is the one usually read backwards. The dose–response analysis found that participants who *used dentures* had no significant excess risk (RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.90–1.11). This is routinely quoted as evidence that restoring occlusion protects the brain. It is equally, and more parsimoniously, evidence that people who obtain and wear dentures differ from people who lose teeth and do not replace them — in income, in dental access, in executive function, in exactly the variables that also predict dementia.

## Three attempts to break the arrow — and the arrow that survives runs backwards

**Genetics.** A comprehensive bidirectional Mendelian randomization using two separate periodontitis GWAS datasets as exploration and replication cohorts, against four brain-atrophy metrics and eight cognitive metrics from mild cognitive impairment through Alzheimer's, Lewy body, vascular and frontotemporal dementia, found **no causal association in either direction**, in either cohort, including for longitudinal rates of change. For depression the result is the same and cleaner: linkage-disequilibrium score regression puts the genetic correlation between depression and periodontitis at rg = 0.06 (p = 0.619), ρ-HESS finds no locus of significant local covariance, and three separate causal-inference frameworks return odds ratios between 1.00 and 1.06 in both directions across samples of 45,563 to 797,563 people. Against schizophrenia, genetically proxied periodontitis gives OR 1.03 (p = 0.50); against major depression, OR 1.06 (p = 0.11).

The instructive failure is Alzheimer's. Using five SNPs, genetically predicted periodontitis was associated with AD at OR 1.10 (1.02–1.19, p = 0.02) — a publishable positive. The same authors then re-ran it with seven non-overlapping SNPs from a newer, larger periodontitis GWAS: OR 0.97 (0.87–1.08, p = 0.59). The effect did not replicate against a better instrument, in the same paper, by the same team. That is a dying effect, and saying so is the finding. The genre's other tell is an MR reporting that loose teeth raise AD risk with OR 187.36 and a 95% CI spanning 2.54 to roughly 13,800 — a confidence interval four orders of magnitude wide is a weak-instrument artifact, not a discovery, and its own companion analysis found no causal relationship between periodontal disease and AD at all (p = 0.395).

**Life course.** The best-supported alternative explanation does not require inflammation to reach the brain. Childhood cognitive function predicts both adult oral health and late-life cognition: children who score better acquire better oral hygiene and sustained dental access, lose fewer teeth across the following five decades, and also arrive at old age with more cognitive reserve. Compare the two groups at any age from the mid-twenties onward and the less cognitively able children have more missing teeth — a gap that widens with age, exactly reproducing the "dose–response" the mechanistic reading claims. The three proposed biological mechanisms — malnutrition from impaired chewing, loss of periodontal somatosensory feedback, and periodontitis-driven neuroinflammation — are, on this appraisal, not supported by compelling empirical evidence.

**Direction.** Where the genetic evidence *does* find a causal signal, it runs the other way. Genetic liability to depression raises the risk of mouth ulcers, toothache, loose teeth, bleeding gums, painful gums, chronic periodontitis and excessive tooth attrition. The magnitude of that reverse arrow is what makes the forward arrow so hard to see: people with severe mental illness have 2.8× the odds of having lost all their teeth (95% CI 1.7–4.6), a mean DMFT excess of 5.0 teeth (2.5–7.4) and a DMFS excess of 14.6 surfaces (4.1–25.1) across 5,076 patients against 39,545 controls. Eating disorders add caries, erosion, reduced salivary flow and lowered salivary pH; psychotropic xerostomia, smoking, and the collapse of self-care during illness supply the rest. Prodromal cognitive decline degrades oral hygiene years before dementia is diagnosed — one of the longitudinal meta-analyses says so explicitly, concluding its own positive findings were "at least partly due to reverse causality."

## The only human test of the causal claim, read properly

The strongest version of the causal hypothesis is bacterial: *Porphyromonas gingivalis* reaches the brain, and its gingipain proteases cleave tau and amyloid precursor protein, driving neurodegeneration. That claim was made on the strength of gingipains detected in AD brains correlating with tau and ubiquitin pathology, oral infection producing brain colonisation and increased Aβ in mice, and small-molecule gingipain inhibitors rescuing hippocampal neurons — work published by, and largely funded by, the company that owned the inhibitor.

It was then tested. A phase 2/3 randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial gave the gingipain inhibitor to 643 patients with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's (214 at 80 mg BID, 212 at 40 mg, 217 placebo) for 48 weeks against co-primary endpoints of ADAS-Cog 11 and ADCS-ADL. Target engagement was real: serum anti-*P. gingivalis* IgG fell from 191.6 to 77.8 on high dose against 193.1 to 134.4 on placebo. The clinical result was not. ADAS-Cog rose (worsened) 2.9 points on 80 mg against 4.8 on placebo — the one number the hypothesis's supporters quote. But the co-primary functional endpoint moved 0.4 points (ADCS-ADL −4.5 vs −4.9), and the global measure moved not at all (CDR-SB +1.5 vs +1.5), with the 40 mg arm doing *better* than 80 mg on CDR-SB while doing worse on ADAS-Cog. A co-primary design requires both endpoints; this trial did not deliver one of them, and its dose-ordering is incoherent across scales.

The attrition finishes the argument. 85 of 214 (39.7%) discontinued on 80 mg and 85 of 212 (40.1%) on 40 mg, against 54 of 217 (24.9%) on placebo — so the week-48 cognitive scores compare 60% of the drug arm against 75% of the placebo arm, with the differential loss falling on the arm that looked better. Non-serious adverse events ran 139/214 and 150/212 against 88/217, and there were **5 deaths on 80 mg and 1 on 40 mg against 0 on placebo**. A 1.9-point ADAS-Cog difference generated under 15 percentage points of excess differential dropout, unsupported by function or global rating, alongside excess mortality, is not evidence that killing an oral pathogen protects the brain.

## Three outcomes, three different answers

**Cognition and dementia — associated, causality unsupported.** The consistent RR 1.2–1.3 above, with dose-response, defeated by MR in both cohorts and by the life-course account. This is the strongest of the three and still does not reach causal.

**Depression and anxiety — the association is a design artifact.** Case-control and cross-sectional pooling across 40 studies gives OR 1.70 (1.01–2.83) for depression, OR 1.36 (1.11–1.66) for anxiety, and large scale-score gaps (depression SMD 1.05, 0.68–1.41; anxiety SMD 0.70, 0.44–0.96) — with high heterogeneity acknowledged throughout. Move to prospective designs and it evaporates: RR 1.07 (0.95–1.21), non-significant. Move to genetics and there is not even shared heritability. Note the one exception worth holding: genetically proxied periodontitis against **bipolar disorder** returned OR 1.10 (p = 0.0086), from a single MR using 8 SNPs, unreplicated — interesting, not yet a fact. Note also what psychological state does to periodontal *treatment*: patients with stress and stress-related disorders achieve 0.78 mm less clinical attachment gain (p = 0.01) and 1.02 mm less pocket-depth reduction after steps 1–2 therapy. That is a real, clinically actionable finding, and it runs psychiatry → mouth.

**Psychosis — no usable evidence in this direction.** The oral microbiome literature in schizophrenia comprises a handful of studies, several from a single research group, with the largest imaging-linked analysis (213 participants, 139 scanned) cross-sectional, association-only, and still a preprint. It reports a compositional shift in beta diversity and a 0.46 correlation between a microbial and a functional-connectivity component — hypothesis-generating, and confounded by the antipsychotic-driven xerostomia, smoking rates and dietary pattern that separate these groups on every other measure too. Genetically proxied periodontitis against schizophrenia is null. There is currently no defensible claim here in either strength.

## Where this verdict inverts

It inverts if you are asked a *different* question than the one answered here. Psychiatry → mouth is well supported, large, and actionable: the 2.8× edentulism odds in severe mental illness are not in dispute, and oral disease is a genuine contributor to the mortality gap through nutrition, systemic inflammation and avoidable admissions. Nothing in this report argues against dental screening and referral for psychiatric patients — it argues that the justification is the patient's mouth, teeth and general health, not dementia prevention.

It would also invert for a specific subgroup that has never been isolated: severe, clinically confirmed periodontitis with documented systemic inflammatory burden, followed prospectively with pre-morbid cognition measured in childhood or early adulthood. Every existing cohort confounds the exposure with the life course that produced it; none has removed the confounder rather than adjusting for a proxy of it. And the tooth-loss dose–response remains genuinely unexplained by the life-course model at the *edentulous* extreme, where masticatory and nutritional pathways become biologically plausible in a way they are not for two or three missing molars.

## What would change it

Very little is coming, and that is itself the finding. A registry sweep returns **seven** interventional trials touching this question, and the interventional evidence base is effectively empty:

- The single completed adequately powered trial is the gingipain-inhibitor study above (n = 643, completed 2022, results posted, co-primaries not met).
- The trial designed to test periodontal treatment for dementia prevention in mild cognitive impairment was **terminated at n = 26** of its planned enrolment.
- A trial of surgical and non-surgical periodontal treatment against Alzheimer's-associated outcomes is enrolling by invitation at **n = 26**, with primary completion **September 2029**.
- Periodontal therapy in major depression exists only as a **40-patient pilot** whose registry status is "unknown", readout nominally January 2026.
- A 190-patient periodontal-therapy trial in Alzheimer's has been "not yet recruiting" since a stated 2024 start, readout nominally December 2026; a 90-patient antibacterial-photodynamic-therapy trial in periodontitis with mild cognitive impairment is likewise not yet recruiting.
- **One publication-bias flag:** a 92-patient interventional study of inflammatory markers in depression with chronic periodontitis passed its primary completion date of 30 December 2019 and remains listed "active, not recruiting" with no results posted — six and a half years unreported.

So the specific evidence that would move this verdict is a properly powered randomised trial of periodontal therapy against an incident-cognition or standardised mood endpoint, run for years rather than months, with pre-morbid cognitive ability measured or instrumented rather than adjusted for. Nothing currently registered will deliver it before 2029, and the largest registered attempts are n = 26. Until then the honest position is not "unproven, watch this space" but **"tested where it could be tested, and it did not hold."**
