# Which supplement, if any, a healthy adult should take for cognition — and what to stop buying

*2026-07-31 · PubMed 15 · trials 5 · books 0*

> Assumed: "benefit" means an objectively measured change on a cognitive test, not a subjective
> sense of sharpness; "healthy adult" means non-deficient and under ~60, since that is the
> reading you picked and it is the population almost none of this evidence is in.
> Skipped: prescription stimulants and modafinil (you said *supplement*), and supplements for
> cognitive impairment in psychiatric illness or dementia — a different decision you declined.

## The verdict

**Take none of them for cognitive enhancement.** The single conditional exception is creatine
monohydrate 3–5 g/day, worth an 8-week personal trial only because it is cheap and safe — not
because the evidence supports it in someone your age: pooled memory effect in adults aged 11–31
is SMD 0.03 (95% CI −0.14 to 0.20, I²=0%, 8 RCTs), while the entire positive signal sits in
adults aged 66–76 (SMD 0.88, 95% CI 0.22–1.55). Everything else in the aisle has either been
killed by an adequately powered trial (ginkgo, B vitamins, omega-3, cocoa flavanols) or rests on
very-low-certainty evidence (bacopa), and the multivitamin finding people cite is 0.07 SD in
people aged ≥60 inside a single trial family. **Never promise a healthy, non-deficient adult a
detectable cognitive gain from any of these.** Confidence: **high** for the kill list, **moderate-low**
for the creatine exception — it is a bet on plausibility and safety, not on demonstrated effect
in your age band.

## The one pattern that decides every agent below

Read the eight agents together and they say one thing: **these compounds correct deficits; they do
not enhance an intact system.** The effect appears, every time, exactly where a deficiency exists,
and vanishes where it does not.

Vitamin D across 24 trials and 7,557 participants moves global cognition by Hedges' *g* = 0.128
(p = .008) and moves **no individual cognitive domain at all** — but in participants with baseline
deficiency the effect is *g* = 0.480, and in "vulnerable populations" *g* = 0.414. Omega-3 across
24 RCTs improves nothing on any cognitive measure in healthy or neurodevelopmental samples, with
one exception: short-term memory in people with **low n-3 status**. Creatine's pooled memory
benefit is SMD 0.29 (95% CI 0.04–0.53) overall, but that decomposes into 0.88 in 66–76-year-olds
and 0.03 in 11–31-year-olds — an age gradient that tracks falling baseline brain phosphocreatine,
not a dose gradient (2.2–20 g/d and 5 days–24 weeks made no difference to the estimate).

This is why the aisle disappoints and will keep disappointing. If your intake is adequate, the
mechanism a supplement acts through is already saturated, and the honest prior for any new
nutraceutical in a well-fed adult is *no effect*. That prior is what the rest of this report is
scored against.

## The one agent worth a personal trial, and the exact terms

Creatine monohydrate, 3–5 g/day, plain monohydrate, no loading phase, for 8 weeks. The terms
matter more than the agent:

- **What you can expect:** most likely nothing you can perceive. The relevant estimate for you is
  0.03 SD, not 0.29. Take the trial as cheap insurance against being someone in whom it works,
  not as a treatment.
- **What would count as a signal:** a pre-specified, repeated objective task, not a feeling. The
  only positive recent healthy-adult data are a 7-day 20 g/day crossover in 14 physically active
  men showing improved digit-cancellation performance (r = 0.77, p = .013) alongside improved
  *subjective* sleep quality (p = .009) with **no** change in objective sleep efficiency, latency,
  or total sleep time — a pattern that should make you distrust your own sense of improvement.
- **Where it plausibly does work acutely:** under load. A crossover in 40 adolescent athletes
  found acute creatine improved dual-task performance and cut dual-task cost during
  cognitive–motor loading, and the only mechanistic story with any consistency is that creatine
  helps when brain energy demand outruns supply — sleep deprivation, exertion, ageing. That is a
  post-call hypothesis, not a proven indication.
- **Harms:** the trials report none of consequence; weight gain of ~1 kg from intracellular water
  is the usual complaint. Do not take it if renal function is impaired.

Stop at 8 weeks and stop for good if nothing measurable changed. There is no dose to escalate to —
dose did not predict effect anywhere in the pooled data.

## The multivitamin signal is real, and it is not transferable to you

This is the one place where a positive result deserves to be taken seriously and still refused.
Three cognitive substudies found a benefit: COSMOS-Mind (n = 2,262, mean age 73, 3 years) gave
global cognition z = 0.07 (95% CI 0.02–0.12, p = .007); COSMOS-Web (n = 3,562) improved episodic
memory at 1 year (t = 2.25, P = .025) and across 3 years (t = 2.54, P = .011); COSMOS-Clinic
(n = 573) improved episodic memory by 0.12 SU (95% CI 0.002–0.23) while its primary global
cognition endpoint, 0.06 SU (95% CI −0.003 to 0.13), **did not reach significance**. Pooled: global
cognition 0.07 SU (95% CI 0.03–0.11, p = .0009), which the authors translate as ~2 years of
cognitive ageing averted.

Four things kill it as a recommendation for you, in order of weight:

1. **The population is ≥60** — women ≥65, men ≥60. Every participant was decades past the age at
   which the deficit these vitamins might correct begins to open. Importing that estimate into a
   healthy 30-something is exactly the generalisation this report exists to refuse.
2. **The "meta-analysis of three studies" is one trial.** All three substudies are ancillary
   arms of a single parent trial (NCT02422745), one population, one supplement, one funding
   structure. Pooling three looks at the same cohort is not independent replication, and the paper's
   own framing invites the misreading.
3. **Cognition was never a primary endpoint.** COSMOS was powered for cardiovascular events and
   invasive cancer; cognition sits among its "other outcomes."
4. **Nothing is coming to confirm it.** The registry sweep for ongoing multivitamin–cognition
   trials returns a 58-participant perimenopause study and a 30-participant pilot. There is no
   confirmatory trial in the pipeline, so this estimate is as good as it will get.

To its credit the same trial's **cocoa flavanol** arm — the arm its industry collaborator had the
strongest interest in — failed cleanly (global cognition 0.03, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.08, p = .28 at
3 years; −0.01 SU, 95% CI −0.08 to 0.05 at 2 years in the clinic subcohort). That is evidence the
trial was run honestly. It fixes the internal credibility problem and does nothing for the
external one.

## The kill list — and the trial that killed each

| Agent | The killing evidence | The number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ginkgo biloba** | 3,069 adults ≥75, EGb761 120 mg twice daily, median 6.1 y | Dementia HR 1.12 (0.94–1.33); AD HR 1.16 (0.97–1.39); event rate 3.3 vs 2.9 per 100 person-years — numerically **worse** on ginkgo |
| **B vitamins / homocysteine** | 11 trials, 22,000 participants, 2.3–5 y | Homocysteine cut 26–28%; global cognition z-difference **0.00** (95% CI −0.05 to 0.06); a 25% homocysteine reduction buys 0.02 y of cognitive ageing per year (95% CI −0.10 to 0.13) |
| **Omega-3** | Cochrane, 3 RCTs, 4,080 randomised, 24–40 mo; plus 24 RCTs in healthy/ADHD samples | MMSE MD −0.07 (95% CI −0.25 to 0.10); no benefit on word learning, digit span, or verbal fluency; no effect on any measure in the healthy pooled sample |
| **Cocoa flavanols** | 2,262 and 573 participants, 3 y and 2 y, same trial | 0.03 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.08), p = .28; −0.01 SU (95% CI −0.08 to 0.05) |
| **Bacopa monnieri** | 5 RCTs, 125–500 mg twice daily, in Alzheimer dementia/MCI | All 5 at **high risk of bias**; certainty rated **very low**; no difference vs placebo or donepezil. The healthy-adult literature is smaller, shorter, and has never been pooled to this standard |
| **Vitamin D (if replete)** | 24 trials, 7,557 participants | *g* = 0.128 globally, **zero** in every specific domain; the effect is confined to deficiency (*g* = 0.480) |

Two entries deserve a word rather than a row.

**B vitamins are a precise null, not an underpowered one.** With 22,000 participants the confidence
interval excludes any benefit larger than about a month of cognitive ageing per year of treatment.
This is the strongest evidence in the whole report, and it is evidence of *absence*. Homocysteine is
a real risk marker and lowering it is real — it simply does not touch cognition. Do not let the
epidemiology talk you back into the supplement.

**Ashwagandha is not on this list because it is being sold for the wrong endpoint.** Its meta-analyses
are about anxiety (SMD −1.55, 95% CI −2.37 to −0.74, 12 RCTs, n = 1,002; replicated at −1.13, 95%
CI −1.65 to −0.60 in 14 studies at a median 600 mg/day for 8 weeks), stress (SMD −1.75, 95% CI −2.29
to −1.22), and sleep (SMD −1.35, 95% CI −1.79 to −0.91). Heterogeneity is extreme (I² 83–94%) and
certainty is rated low, so even those numbers are soft. There is no cognitive-endpoint meta-analysis
to cite. If you want it, want it for anxiety — and know you are buying a low-certainty effect.

**Caffeine + L-theanine** is the honest acute exception, and a narrow one: 97 mg L-theanine with
40 mg caffeine improved performance on a task-switching measure while improving **neither**
intersensory attention **nor** subjective alertness. That is a within-session effect on one task,
not cognitive enhancement, and it tells you nothing about a daily supplement.

## Where this verdict inverts

Three conditions flip the answer, and all three are the same condition:

- **You are actually deficient.** Correcting a low vitamin D, low B12, or genuinely low n-3 intake is
  a different decision with different evidence (*g* = 0.480 for deficiency correction vs 0.128
  overall). Measure before supplementing; do not supplement in place of measuring.
- **You are over ~65.** The creatine estimate becomes SMD 0.88 and the multivitamin estimate becomes
  applicable. Both remain small.
- **You have a diagnosis** — MCI, dementia, or a psychiatric illness with cognitive features. None of
  this report applies; the evidence base and the decision are different.

Outside those, no supplement in this review has cleared the bar, and the interventions that actually
move cognition in a healthy adult are behavioural — sleep, exercise, treated hearing loss,
cardiovascular risk control. That comparison was outside this report's searches and its magnitudes
are `[unverified]` here, but it is the honest alternative use of the money.

## What would change this verdict

- **NCT06948149** — creatine + resistance training, 200 participants with MCI, 26 weeks, primary
  completion December 2027. The largest creatine–cognition trial ever run. It is in MCI, so a
  positive result still would not license the healthy-adult recommendation — but a null would close
  the creatine question entirely.
- **NCT02422745 (COSMOS)** — completed June 2023; its cognitive substudies are fully reported. There
  is no further readout to wait for.
- **NCT07242430** (58 participants, perimenopause, multivitamin, completion August 2026) and
  **NCT06795789** (30-participant multivitamin + blueberry polyphenol pilot) are the only ongoing
  multivitamin–cognition trials found. Neither is large enough to move anything.
- **NCT00010803 (GEM)** — closed 2008. Ginkgo will not be re-litigated.

The structural thing that would change this report is a trial that **enrols healthy adults under 60
and measures objective cognition as a primary endpoint over ≥12 months.** As of this sweep, no such
trial exists for any agent above. That absence — not any single null — is the strongest reason to
buy nothing.
