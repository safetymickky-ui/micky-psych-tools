## The verdict

**Prescribe exercise to adults with obesity — but not for cognition, and do not let cognition be the reason they agree to it.** The honest claim you can make out loud is: regular exercise reliably improves cardiometabolic risk, fitness, mood and physical function, and *may* produce a small, short-horizon improvement in executive-function and memory test scores. The defensible magnitude is **d ≈ 0.05–0.13**, not the **SMD 0.42** the largest umbrella review advertises — because the difference between those two numbers is publication bias and inactive control groups, and it has been measured. There is **no trial-level evidence that exercise prevents cognitive impairment or dementia in this population**: the one adequately powered test in exactly these patients ran for a decade and returned 6.4% versus 6.6% MCI prevalence.

Do not promise dementia prevention. Do not promise measurable memory improvement to an individual patient. **Confidence: moderate-low** — not higher because the obesity-specific randomised evidence is four small trials and a mostly-rodent systematic review, so the verdict leans on extrapolation from adjacent populations that happens to point the same way.

## What is actually being measured, and why the units matter

Three different outcomes travel under one phrase, and they disagree with each other.

The first is **test-score change** over weeks to months — an executive-function composite, a digit-symbol substitution score, a verbal-learning list. Almost all the positive literature lives here. The second is **incident cognitive impairment** — adjudicated MCI or probable dementia over years. Almost all the negative literature lives here. The third is **structural or perfusion imaging**, which is a mechanism read-out and settles nothing on its own.

The gap between the first and the second is the whole story of this review, and it is not merely a matter of follow-up length. A test score can move because a person got better at the test. In adults with obesity specifically, cross-sectional deficits appear across nearly every cognitive domain examined — complex attention, verbal and visual memory, decision-making — but the seventeen studies establishing that could not separate obesity from its comorbidities, which is the same confound that then contaminates every uncontrolled intervention study.

## How big is the obesity–cognition link the exercise is meant to repair?

Large enough to be worth attacking, and mostly not directly. In more than 20,000 UK Biobank participants followed 8 years (mean age 63), structural equation modelling put BMI, waist-to-hip ratio and body-fat percentage upstream of raised C-reactive protein, dyslipidaemia, hypertension and diabetes; those in turn upstream of cerebrovascular disease; and cerebrovascular disease upstream of lower cortical thickness and volume and of measurable cognitive deficits (largest significant corrected *p* = 0.02). Adiposity reaches cognition **through** its metabolic and vascular consequences — which is why an intervention that changes fitness without changing those consequences should not be expected to move cognition much.

At population scale, physical inactivity carries an unweighted population attributable fraction for dementia of **15.2% (95% CI 12.8–17.7)**, falling to **7.3% (3.9–11.2)** once overlap with other risk factors is accounted for; obesity carries **9.4% (7.3–11.7)** unweighted and **5.3% (3.2–7.4)** weighted. Combine low education, midlife hypertension, midlife obesity, smoking, physical inactivity, depression and diabetes and the unweighted PAF reaches **55.0% (46.5–63.5)** but the weighted value is **32.0% (26.6–37.5)** — the gap between those two is how much these risk factors are the same patients twice.

A PAF is not an effect size. It is the ceiling on what perfect elimination of a risk factor could buy in a population, under the assumption that the association is causal. The formal evidence grading is blunter: in a synthesis of 243 prospective observational studies and 153 RCTs, **physical exercise sits at Class I / Level B — weaker evidence** — alongside midlife obesity and late-life weight loss, while high late-life BMI is Level A.

## Mechanism — plausible, specific, and not the bottleneck

Adipose tissue is not a passive depot with respect to the brain. Adipose-derived extracellular vesicles carry cargo that reaches the brain and promotes cognitive impairment linked to insulin resistance; brain insulin resistance itself sits at the crossroads of metabolic and cognitive disorder in humans. Exercise plausibly intervenes on all of it, and in adults with obesity the biomarker response is real and measurable: in 92 postmenopausal women with obesity randomised across four arms for 3 months, every intervention arm raised plasma BDNF and cut weight, BMI, waist-hip ratio, fat mass and predicted VO₂max relative to control.

This is the trap the review has to name. **The biomarkers moved, the memory scores moved a little, and the disease outcome did not move at all** in the one trial powered to see it. Mechanism is an input to the verdict here, never a substitute for it — a report that lets BDNF stand in for dementia prevention has smuggled in the conclusion.

## What the general adult trials show, once you correct them

Read at face value, the literature is overwhelming. The largest umbrella review — 133 systematic reviews, 2,724 RCTs, 258,279 participants — reports exercise improving general cognition **SMD 0.42**, memory **SMD 0.26** and executive function **SMD 0.24**, with effects *larger* in children and adolescents than in adults, *larger* for low- and moderate-intensity work, and largest for interventions of only 1–3 months. In adults over 50, a 39-study meta-analysis gives **SMD 0.29 (0.17–0.41)**, with 45–60 minutes per session at ≥moderate intensity as the productive prescription. A Bayesian dose–response network meta-analysis of 44 trials (4,793 participants) finds no minimum threshold, a clinically relevant dose around **724 MET-min/week**, and unclear additional benefit beyond **1,200 MET-min/week**.

Then read the correction. An umbrella review restricted to RCTs in healthy populations took the 24 constituent meta-analyses apart and found low statistical power in the primary trials, selective study inclusion, publication bias, and wide variation in analytic choices. Re-meta-analysing the primary RCTs gave **d = 0.22 (0.16–0.28)**; adjusting for active control groups and baseline differences dropped it to **d = 0.13 (0.07–0.20)**; correcting for publication bias dropped it to **d = 0.05 (−0.09 to 0.14)** — a confidence interval that includes zero.

**I believe the corrected estimate.** Three things decide it. The bias-correction analysis works on the same primary trials the optimistic reviews pooled, so it is not a different literature, it is the same literature with the inactive-control and small-study inflation removed. The pattern inside the optimistic review corroborates it — effects that are *larger* in short interventions and *larger* at low intensity are the signature of practice effects and small-study noise, not of a dose-dependent biological response. And the head-to-head trial evidence agrees: in 296 sedentary older adults with amnestic MCI randomised for 18 months, **moderate-high intensity aerobic training and low-intensity stretching / balance / range-of-motion were indistinguishable** (regression −0.078, SE 0.074, *p* = 0.3), with high adherence in both arms (81% and 87%). If intensity does not matter, the mechanism claimed for intensity is not what is operating.

That trial has a genuinely interesting wrinkle: **neither arm declined over 12 months**, and mean hippocampal volume loss was only 0.51%. Compared post-hoc against a propensity-matched no-intervention group, both arms declined less (aerobic β = 0.169, 95% CI 0.011–0.328; stretching β = 0.181, 95% CI 0.007–0.354). That is the strongest available hint that *something* about structured supervised activity protects — but it is a non-randomised external comparison bolted onto a null randomised result, and it cannot carry a recommendation.

Multidomain trials land in the same narrow band. Two years of diet, exercise, cognitive training and vascular monitoring in 1,260 at-risk elderly people beat general health advice by **0.022 NTB Z-points per year (0.002–0.042, p = 0.030)** — with adverse events in 7% versus 1%, mostly musculoskeletal pain. In 2,111 sedentary older adults with suboptimal diets, a structured multidomain programme beat a self-guided one by **0.029 SD per year (0.008–0.050, p = 0.008)**; the benefit was consistent across APOE ε4 status (interaction p = 0.95) and larger for those with lower baseline cognition (p = 0.02). Note what that trial does *not* contain: a no-treatment arm. **Both** groups' composite scores rose (0.243 and 0.213 SD/year), which is what practice effects look like. The largest single-modality signal comes from a 175-participant factorial trial in MCI, where aerobic-resistance exercise beat balance-toning control on ADAS-Cog-13 by **−1.79 points (−3.27 to −0.31), d = 0.64**, and the full multidomain arm by **−2.64 points (−4.42 to −0.80), d = 0.71** — but ADAS-Cog-Plus was not significant, and 175 participants at 20 weeks is exactly the size and duration the bias correction was built for.

## What the trials show in adults with obesity specifically — thin, and honest about it

This section is short because the evidence is. It is written thin and says so rather than being padded.

A systematic review of physical-activity interventions and executive function in overweight and obese adults found **seven** eligible studies, reporting that acute, short-term and long-term activity improve inhibitory control, working memory and processing speed — but the risk-of-bias instrument applied was one designed for observational cohort and cross-sectional studies, which tells you what the included designs were. A systematic review of exercise and memory in obesity gathered 16 studies, of which **the large majority were rodent work** (treadmill 72.7%, wheel running 27.3%; 81.8% of animal studies positive; high-fat-diet induction in 82%), and identified no knockout model at all. It is a preclinical review wearing a clinical title.

The two randomised human trials in the population are small and instructive. In 92 postmenopausal women with obesity over 3 months, the exercise and combined arms improved memory alongside BDNF, insulin, HOMA-IR, body-fat percentage and muscle strength; **only** the combined diet-plus-exercise arm improved executive function; **diet alone produced no cognitive improvement at all**; and critically, **there were no significant differences between the three intervention arms** on any cognitive or cardiometabolic outcome. In 29 patients with major depression, bipolar disorder or schizophrenia and comorbid obesity, 12 weeks of guided moderate exercise beat unguided usual activity on neurocognitive and functional performance (*p* < 0.05, η²p = 0.14–0.15) — a 10-versus-19 allocation, which is a pilot.

The nearest adjacent signal is metabolically-loaded rather than obesity-defined: in 8 trials and 747 older patients with type 2 diabetes, exercise improved cognition at **SMD 0.65 (0.48–0.82)**, with MoCA mean difference 2.22 (1.26–3.18) and MMSE 1.81 (0.71–2.90). An SMD of 0.65 from eight small trials in a field whose bias-corrected estimate is 0.05 should be read as a measurement of the small-study literature, not of the effect.

## The decisive trial, and it is negative

One trial tested this question in this population at adequate scale and duration: **10 years of intensive lifestyle intervention — reduced calories plus increased physical activity — in overweight and obese adults with type 2 diabetes**, against diabetes support and education, with adjudicated cognitive outcomes.

It maintained marked, sustained between-group differences in both weight and self-reported physical activity throughout follow-up. At a mean 11.4 years after enrolment (mean age 69.6), among 3,802 participants with adjudicated outcomes, **prevalence of MCI was 6.4% versus 6.6% and probable dementia 1.8% versus 1.8% (p = 0.93)**. Null consistently across cardiovascular-disease history, diabetes duration, sex and APOE ε4 status (all p ≥ 0.50). On the cognitive battery in 3,751 participants, assignment to intervention produced no significant overall (*p* = 0.10) or domain-specific (all *p* > 0.10) difference — and, decisively for this review, **cognitive function was not associated with changes in weight or fitness (all p > 0.05)**. A frailty-stratified ancillary study in 3,708 participants found no window of opportunity: no cognitive difference between arms in any frailty tertile (all *p* > 0.10).

Worse than null at the high-BMI end. The intervention effect ranged **from benefit to harm across participants ordered from lowest to highest baseline BMI (p = 0.03)**. In exploratory repeated-assessment analyses of 1,091 participants, intervention assignment carried a **−0.082 SD deficit in mean global cognitive function (p = 0.010)**; split by baseline BMI, participants with BMI < 30 ran **+0.099 (95% CI −0.006 to 0.259)** while those with **BMI ≥ 30 ran −0.117 (−0.185 to −0.049)**, interaction *p* = 0.014.

**This is the trial I believe, and the reasons are structural.** It is 20–40 times larger than any obesity-specific exercise-cognition trial in the literature. Its follow-up is 10 years against their 12 weeks to 6 months. Its primary outcome is adjudicated disease, not a test score subject to practice effects. It sustained the exposure rather than measuring intent. And it is in precisely the population this review is about, rather than requiring extrapolation. Against it sit trials of 92 and 29 participants and a mostly-rodent review. Twelve small positive studies and one large rigorous negative trial is not 12–1.

The reconciliation that survives all the data: exercise moves cognitive **test scores** a little, in the short term, in trials with weak controls; it does not move cognitive **disease** in adults with obesity, and the fitness gain is not the operative variable even when it is achieved and maintained.

## What to prescribe, if you prescribe — and you should

The verdict is against the cognitive indication, not against exercise. For every other outcome the prescription is well specified, and the dose data are real even where the cognitive endpoint fails.

Target roughly **724 MET-min/week** as the point where clinically relevant change appears, with unclear additional return beyond **1,200 MET-min/week** — a non-linear curve with no minimum threshold, meaning small doses are not wasted. Sessions of **45–60 minutes at ≥moderate intensity** carry the cognitive point estimates in adults over 50. Modality can follow patient preference rather than physiology: aerobic work has the best signal for global cognition, resistance training for executive function, mind-body work for memory — but the head-to-head intensity comparison was null, and in the obesity trials the three intervention arms were indistinguishable from each other. **Combine with dietary change** if executive function is the target, since that was the only arm to move it. Physical activity is independently a defensible adjunct across mental disorders, which matters when the patient's obesity is antipsychotic-driven.

What to say aloud, verbatim: *"This will improve your fitness, your blood pressure, your blood sugar and your mood, and it may sharpen your concentration a little. I can't tell you it will protect your memory long-term — the best trial we have, ten years long in patients like you, didn't show that."*

## Where the verdict inverts, and the harms in numbers

**Baseline BMI ≥ 30 with type 2 diabetes.** The one population where a decade of sustained lifestyle intervention was associated with *worse* mean global cognition (−0.117 SD, 95% CI −0.185 to −0.049) is the population most likely to be sitting in front of you. The confidence interval excludes zero. This is exploratory and hypothesis-generating, not established harm — but it is the opposite direction from the recommendation, and it is not disclosable as a footnote.

**Established cardiovascular disease.** Trends toward modest cognitive harm from intensive lifestyle intervention appeared in participants with CVD history in two separate analyses of the same trial.

**Men with low cardiorespiratory fitness.** In 164 community-dwelling older adults, BMI × VO₂max × sex interactions showed men with lower VO₂max performing progressively worse on Grooved Pegboard with both hands as BMI rose (both *p* < 0.02), and men but not women with higher BMI performing worse on delayed logical memory (*p* = 0.036). Cross-sectional, so it identifies a phenotype to watch, not a treatment effect.

**Musculoskeletal cost is real and quantified**: adverse events in 7% of a multidomain intervention group versus 1% of controls, predominantly musculoskeletal pain — in a non-obese elderly cohort, so loaded joints in an obese cohort should be expected to fare worse, not better.

## What would change this verdict

Two registered trials are aimed at the exact question and neither has reported. A 50-participant study explicitly designed to separate the **neurological impact of weight reduction from that of fitness** is registered with completion in December 2027 — that is the confounder this whole review turns on, and it is finally being tested directly, though 50 participants will not settle it. A 164-participant phase 4 trial of exercise and diabetes interventions for brain health in older adults with type 2 diabetes reads out in May 2029.

The publication-bias sweep found one item worth naming: a 190-participant neuroimaging study of exercise and memory function **completed in January 2018 with no results posted to the registry** more than eight years later. At that size it is not decisive, but in a literature whose bias-corrected effect estimate is d = 0.05, an unreported trial of that size is exactly the kind of missing datum that inflated the published estimate.

What would genuinely move the verdict is none of the above: it is a trial that randomises adults with obesity to exercise with an **active** control, powered for adjudicated cognitive impairment, running longer than five years. No such trial is registered. Until one exists, the decade-long negative result stands as the best evidence available, and the honest position is that exercise is excellent medicine prescribed for the wrong reason when it is prescribed for cognition.
