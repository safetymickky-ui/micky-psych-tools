## Executive Summary

**The adolescent mental-health deterioration in high-income countries since roughly 2012 is real on hard endpoints, is concentrated overwhelmingly in adolescent girls, and is not explained by any single cause — least of all by "screen time" as that variable is usually measured.** US high-school students reporting persistent sadness or hopelessness rose from 30% in 2013 to 42% in 2021 [3]; past-year major depressive episode in 12–17-year-olds roughly doubled from the mid-2000s to 20% in 2021 [4]; and the suicide rate for ages 10–24 — an endpoint immune to self-report inflation — rose **62%, from 6.8 to 11.0 per 100,000 between 2007 and 2021** [5]. Yet the same cohorts show *record-low* substance use, with past-year alcohol use among US 12th-graders falling from 75% in 1997 to 42% [15], and 2022–2024 brought the first reversals in a decade: youth suicide fell 18% (ages 10–14) and 9% (ages 15–24) in 2022 [5], and adolescent major depressive episode fell from 20% (2021) to 15.4% (2024) [4]. Any account that describes a monotonic, universal collapse is contradicted by the surveillance data it claims to explain.

**The digital-causation literature is the most method-dependent body of evidence in contemporary psychiatry, and its central dispute is a specification problem, not a data problem.** Orben and Przybylski's specification curve analysis across 355,358 adolescents found that digital technology use explains **at most 0.4% of variance in well-being** [18]. Twenge, Haidt and colleagues re-ran the identical datasets, changed four analytic constraints — separating social media from television, analysing girls separately, excluding mediators from covariates, weighting scales equally — and recovered **median betas of −0.11 to −0.24 in girls** [26]. Both reproductions are correct within their own specification space. What resolves the impasse is not dose but *kind of use*: problematic, dyscontrolled use correlates with depression at **r = 0.273** and anxiety at **r = 0.348** [43], and with loneliness at **d = 0.53** [71] — roughly an order of magnitude above any hours-based measure. Prospective ABCD Study data (n = 9,243) show that the pathway from screen exposure to manic symptoms is mediated **47.7% by problematic social media use and 58.0% by problematic gaming, but only 9.0% by sleep duration** [69]. Cyberbullying victimisation — an experience, not a dose — independently predicted manic symptoms after adjustment for total screen time [70].

**Risk is developmentally windowed, sex-specific, and heavily shaped by ascertainment.** Longitudinal analysis of 17,409 UK adolescents identified discrete sensitivity windows in which higher social media use predicted lower life satisfaction one year later: **females 11–13 and 19; males 14–15 and 19** [32]. Simultaneously, a large share of the apparent morbidity increase is diagnostic rather than incident. UK primary-care data show the post-2020 surge concentrated almost entirely in **ADHD diagnoses in females (24.7% above pre-pandemic trend), greatest in 20–24-year-olds in *less deprived* areas, while anxiety and depression diagnoses ran at or below expectation** [7] — the signature of awareness and access, not exposure. Autism identification reached 1 in 31 US 8-year-olds in 2022, but with a **5.5-fold variation between surveillance sites within one country** (California 53.1 vs Laredo 9.7 per 1,000) [82]. Foulkes and Andrews's prevalence-inflation hypothesis — that awareness campaigns produce both genuine improved recognition and overinterpretation of normal distress, the latter potentially self-fulfilling [62] — now has empirical purchase: in the 8,376-pupil MYRIAD trial, universal school mindfulness produced **clinically trivial differential response (d = 0.07)** and worse outcomes in already-at-risk pupils, and 33% of high-quality trials of group mental-health interventions report at least one negative effect, all of them in universal rather than targeted programmes [73][74].

**For clinicians, three things follow immediately.** First, stop asking "how many hours?" and start asking about dyscontrol, night-time displacement, cyberbullying, appearance-focused comparison, and what psychological need the platform is meeting — the effect sizes live there, not in the clock. Second, expect and recognise the new presentations: functional tic-like behaviours, which are **more disabling than Tourette syndrome** on school absence, depressive symptoms and suicidality [55], and which are over-represented in gender-diverse youth (15/35 in one series) [55]; and TikTok-mediated self-diagnosis, which measurably shifts illness certainty in *both* directions [60]. Third, treat AI companions as the exposure of the next five years for which no evidence exists: **72% of US teens have already used one, 31% find the conversations as satisfying as talking to friends** [23a], and the APA's 2025 advisory now asks clinicians to enquire about chatbot use as routine history [75]. Population interventions tested so far have failed — restrictive school phone policies showed **no association with better wellbeing, sleep or attainment** because total daily use did not fall [39] — and Australia's under-16 ban, commenced 10 December 2025 with 4.7 million accounts removed [77], is the definitive natural experiment whose results do not yet exist.

## 1. Background and Context

### 1.1 Historical Context — how generational psychiatry arrived at this point

Generational cohort analysis entered psychiatry through the back door of sociology and marketing rather than through epidemiology, and it carries the methodological debts of that origin. Pew Research Center fixed Generation Z as those born **1997–2012**, reasoning that the defining events of the Millennial cohort — 9/11, the Iraq War, the 2008 financial crisis — fall outside the living memory of anyone born after 1996 [1]. Generation Alpha has no comparable anchoring: the most cited boundary is **2010, chosen because the iPad launched that year**, with an end point at 2024 or 2025, and competing definitions begin the cohort in 2013 [2]. That a cohort boundary should be set by a consumer-electronics release date is itself the most compact possible statement of the field's central hypothesis and its central weakness. In 2023, Pew — the institution most responsible for popularising these labels — announced it would stop defaulting to generational framing unless historical data permit like-for-like comparison at the same life stage [1], a retraction that has been almost universally ignored by the downstream literature.

The empirical claim that something changed arrived in 2017–2019, principally through Jean Twenge's work linking post-2011 increases in US adolescent depression, self-harm and suicide to the diffusion of smartphones and social media [31]. Twenge's causal argument was explicitly abductive rather than experimental: a candidate cause must have begun or accelerated during 2011–2018, affected a large number of people, impacted everyday life, and be associated with depression — and on those four criteria the technological environment was the leading survivor [31]. Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski replied in 2019 with the methodological intervention that has defined the debate ever since, applying **specification curve analysis** to three large datasets totalling 355,358 participants and demonstrating that the negative association, while statistically robust, explained **at most 0.4% of variance** and was of comparable magnitude to associations with eating potatoes or wearing spectacles [18]. Their companion paper used **time-use diaries rather than retrospective self-report** across 17,247 adolescents in Ireland, the US and the UK, and found "little evidence for substantial negative associations" [19].

The dispute escalated from method to metascience. In 2022, Twenge, Haidt, Lozano and Cummins re-ran specification curve analysis on Orben and Przybylski's own datasets, reproduced the original results exactly under the original constraints, then changed four analytic decisions and recovered effects an order of magnitude larger in girls [26]. Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (2024) took the harm thesis to a mass audience, and Candice Odgers's review in *Nature* took it apart, arguing that "hundreds of researchers, myself included, have searched for the kind of large effects suggested by Haidt" and produced "a mix of no, small, and mixed associations," with the longitudinal evidence better fitting reverse causation — that young people already unwell use platforms more and differently [37]. By 2026 the exchange had become a formal methodological war, with Sigaud, Rausch, McClean and Haidt publishing a paper in *Clinical Psychological Science* arguing that three of Vuorre and Przybylski's key studies should not be used to assess adolescent impact at all [38]. Neither camp now holds unchallenged methodological high ground.

Running underneath the digital debate is a second, quieter historical thread: the expansion of psychiatric categories and of help-seeking. Between 2000 and 2022, US autism identification moved from 1 in 150 to **1 in 31** eight-year-olds [82]. NHS England's Gender Identity Development Service saw referrals of adolescent girls rise from **15 in 2009 to 1,071 in 2016** [63]. UK primary-care ADHD incidence in young women ran nearly a quarter above pre-pandemic trend after 2020 [7]. These changes are not artefacts in the dismissive sense — they represent real children receiving real diagnoses — but they are ascertainment phenomena as much as incidence phenomena, and disentangling the two is the core epidemiological problem of the field.

### 1.2 Current Landscape — what is established as of 2026

Four propositions now command broad agreement across otherwise hostile camps. **First, internalising symptoms rose substantially in high-income Anglophone and Nordic countries from approximately 2012, with the increase concentrated in adolescent girls.** US high-school students reporting persistent sadness or hopelessness moved from 30% (2013) to 42% (2021), with female students at 57% in 2021 versus 28% of males, and LGBTQ+ students at 65% versus 31% of cisgender heterosexual peers [3]. **Second, the change is visible on endpoints that self-report inflation cannot reach**: US suicide among 10–24-year-olds rose 62% between 2007 and 2021 [5]; Danish national register data show rising eating-disorder incidence across 2000–2022 [12]; Medicaid claims across 22 states show rising diagnosed mental-health and neurodevelopmental disorder from 2010 to 2019, entirely pre-pandemic [14].

**Third, the effect of digital media measured as time is small, and the effect measured as dyscontrol is not.** The pooled meta-analytic correlation between problematic social media use and depression is r = 0.273, anxiety r = 0.348, stress r = 0.313 across 9,269 participants [43]; a 182-study systematic review (n = 1,169,396) found small but significant associations for use itself and consistently larger ones for problematic use [42]; and internet-use-disorder symptoms associate with loneliness at d = 0.53 across 36,484 people [71]. **Fourth, susceptibility is not uniform**: sensitivity windows fall at ages 11–13 for girls and 14–15 for boys, with a second window at 19 for both [32], and the entire literature is now organised around *differential susceptibility to media effects* rather than uniform dose–response [22].

Two propositions remain genuinely contested. Whether the digital environment is the *dominant* cause of the epidemiological shift is unresolved: the National Academies' 2024 consensus report explicitly declined causal inference, concluding only that specific features — algorithmic amplification of harmful content, displacement of constructive time — can harm *some* young people [36], and the cross-national trend data are markedly heterogeneous [78][79]. And whether the surge in neurodevelopmental and gender-related presentations represents incidence, ascertainment, or social transmission is fought over in public with unusual acrimony [7][63][82].

### 1.3 Why This Matters Now

Three developments make 2026 a decision point rather than an observation point. **Australia's under-16 social media prohibition commenced on 10 December 2025**, barring account-holding on TikTok, Snapchat, YouTube, Reddit, Instagram, Facebook, Kick, Twitch, Threads and X, with penalties to A$50 million, parental consent explicitly *unable* to override the ban, and 4.7 million under-16 accounts removed within days [77]. It is the largest deliberate experiment ever run on adolescent digital exposure, and every other jurisdiction is waiting on its results. **AI companions arrived faster than any prior exposure**: a nationally representative survey of 1,060 US teenagers in April–May 2025 found **72% had used an AI companion, 52% at least monthly, 34% daily or several times weekly, and 31% reported conversations as satisfying or more satisfying than talking with real friends** [23a]. The APA issued health advisories in 2025 noting that adolescents are less likely than adults to question a bot's accuracy or intent and that AI relationships may displace real-world relationship formation, and now asks clinicians to ask about chatbot use as standard history [75].

**And the first population-level interventions have already failed.** The SMART Schools study of 1,227 UK adolescents across 30 schools found no association between restrictive school phone policies and mental wellbeing, sleep, or attainment — because restricting phones in school did not reduce total daily phone or social media use, only in-school use [39]. Meanwhile the MYRIAD trial's machine-learning secondary analysis of 8,376 pupils found that even optimally targeted universal school mindfulness produced differences of d = 0.07 [73], and the broader literature shows that **all** documented negative effects of group mental-health interventions occurred in universal rather than targeted programmes [74]. The field is being asked to act at scale precisely at the moment when its two best-tested scaled actions have returned null or harmful results.

## 3. Key Findings

### Finding 1: "Generation Z" and "Generation Alpha" Are Marketing Conventions, Not Psychiatric Constructs — and the Age–Period–Cohort Confound Makes Most Generational Claims Unfalsifiable As Stated

Pew's Gen Z boundary (1997–2012) is defended on the historical-memory criterion — those born after 1996 have no formative recollection of 9/11, the Iraq War, or the 2008 crisis [1]. Generation Alpha's boundary is defended on nothing comparable: the most-used start year, 2010, was chosen to coincide with the iPad's release, and competing definitions place it in 2013 [2]. The end date drifts between 2024 and 2025 depending on whether the source has commercial reason to announce "Generation Beta." No professional body, diagnostic manual or epidemiological convention ratifies any of these boundaries.

This is not pedantry, because the boundaries do analytic work. When a study reports that "Gen Z has higher anxiety," the finding decomposes into three inseparable components: an **age effect** (adolescents and young adults have always had higher anxiety incidence than the middle-aged, and peak onset for most disorders falls between 14 and 25), a **period effect** (a pandemic, an economic shock, or a change in screening practice that hits all ages at once), and a genuine **cohort effect** (something specific to people born in a particular window that persists as they age). Almost no study in this literature separates the three, and several of the most-cited do not attempt to. Pew's own 2023 decision to stop defaulting to generational labels unless historical data permit comparison at matched life stages is the correct methodological response, and it has been almost universally ignored downstream [1].

The practical consequence for a clinician is direct: a claim like "Gen Z is more anxious" cannot be operationalised at the bedside, whereas "girls aged 11–13 show a sensitivity window in which higher social media use predicts lower life satisfaction one year later" [32] can. Throughout this report, generational language is used as a shorthand for birth-cohort windows and is repeatedly translated into age-, sex- and exposure-specific statements, because those are the only forms in which the evidence is actionable.

### Finding 2: The Internalising-Symptom Rise Is Real and Large, Concentrated in Adolescent Girls, and Has Plateaued or Partially Reversed Since 2021

Across independent US surveillance systems, the same shape appears. CDC's Youth Risk Behavior Survey recorded persistent sadness or hopelessness in **30% of high-school students in 2013, 42% in 2021, and 40% in 2023** — worse than a decade ago but improving from the peak [3]. The sex asymmetry is the dominant feature: **57% of female students in 2021, falling to 53% in 2023, against 28% of male students**; LGBTQ+ students reported 65% against 31% of cisgender heterosexual peers; American Indian/Alaska Native youth reported the highest rates of any racial or ethnic group [3]. SAMHSA's National Survey on Drug Use and Health traces past-year major depressive episode in 12–17-year-olds from roughly 8–9% in the mid-2000s to **20% in 2021**, then **18% in 2023 and 15.4% in 2024**, with female prevalence at 29.2% against male 11.5% [4].

The plateau-and-partial-reversal since 2021 is the single most under-reported feature of this literature and it constrains causal explanation severely. Smartphone ownership, social media penetration and daily use did not decline between 2021 and 2024; if the digital environment were the dominant driver operating through a simple dose mechanism, the improvement should not have occurred. Explanations that fit the reversal better include pandemic recovery (school reopening, restoration of peer contact), a saturation ceiling in a self-report instrument, or genuine multi-causality in which the pandemic contributed a large period effect that has since partly resolved [6][17].

The international picture is consistent in direction but heterogeneous in magnitude. WHO's HBSC network, sampling approximately **280,000 adolescents aged 11, 13 and 15 across 44 countries** in the 2022 wave, documented problematic social media use rising from **7% (2018) to 11% (2022)**, with girls at 13% against boys at 9% [8]. Analysis of 43 HBSC countries (n = 680,269) found psychological complaints rising more steeply in girls than boys across 2002–2018 [10]. But cross-national analyses covering 2002–2018 report only "a small but significant increase in psychosomatic complaints and a stable pattern in life satisfaction," with **country differences in trends described as considerable** [79] — a caution that recurs in §4.2.

### Finding 3: The Signal Survives on Hard Endpoints — Suicide, Register Diagnoses and Hospital Admissions — Which Rules Out Pure Self-Report Inflation

The strongest objection to the entire "crisis" narrative is that adolescents have become more willing to endorse symptom items on questionnaires without any change in underlying pathology. That objection is serious, has real empirical support (see Finding 12), and is nonetheless insufficient, because the trend appears on endpoints where no amount of relabelling can operate.

US vital statistics — death certificates, not surveys — record the suicide rate among 10–24-year-olds rising **62% from 6.8 per 100,000 in 2007 to 11.0 in 2021**, after no significant change between 2001 and 2007, with the single largest annual increase between 2016 and 2017 (9.6 → 10.6) [5]. The racial disaggregation is stark and rarely discussed: **suicide among Black youth aged 10–17 rose 144% between 2007 and 2020**, the fastest-growing rate of any racial group [5] — a pattern poorly explained by a smartphone hypothesis calibrated to affluent white girls. Danish National Patient Register data covering 1995–2022 show rising incidence of anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and other eating disorders on clinician-assigned ICD-10 diagnoses in a whole-population register [12]. Irish hospital data recorded 1,573 paediatric eating-disorder admissions between 2018 and 2022, **76% of them to acute medical rather than psychiatric hospitals** — a marker of physiological severity, not of diagnostic enthusiasm [13]. Medicaid claims across 22 US states show rising diagnosed mental-health and neurodevelopmental disorder among children aged 3–17 across **2010–2019, entirely before the pandemic** [14].

The mechanism of this convergence matters. Self-report scales, clinician-assigned register diagnoses, hospital admissions and death certificates have completely non-overlapping error structures. Symptom-scale inflation cannot produce excess deaths; diagnostic fashion cannot produce medical admissions for bradycardia in anorexia; and neither can produce a pre-pandemic Medicaid trend. When four instruments with independent biases point the same way, the common cause is more plausibly the phenomenon than the instrumentation.

The caveats are specific rather than generic. Suicide is a low-base-rate outcome and 62% of a small number remains a small number in absolute terms; the 2022 reversal — **−18% for ages 10–14 and −9% for ages 15–24** [5] — may be a one-year fluctuation and should not be over-read in either direction. Eating-disorder register data are sensitive to service-capacity changes: a new specialist team creates diagnoses that previously went unrecorded. And Medicaid claims measure billed diagnoses, which respond to reimbursement policy.

### Finding 4: Externalising Behaviour and Substance Use Moved in the Opposite Direction — Which Refutes Any "Everything Is Worse" Account

The Monitoring the Future survey documents the most successful adolescent public-health trend of the last thirty years, running in exact parallel with the internalising deterioration. Past-year alcohol use fell from **75% to 42% among 12th-graders, 65% to 26% among 10th-graders, and 46% to 13% among 8th-graders** since 1997 [15]. Teen use of most substances remained at or near historic lows through 2024 and 2025, drug abstention reached record levels in 2025, and use has not rebounded to pre-pandemic levels [15]. Teen pregnancy, drink-driving, physical fighting and smoking have all fallen substantially over the same window.

This is not a footnote; it is a structural constraint on theory. Any account of Gen Z that invokes generalised dysregulation, generalised impulsivity, generalised loss of self-control or generalised social collapse has to explain why the *same* cohort simultaneously produced the lowest rates of adolescent substance use ever recorded. The pattern is far better explained by a **shift in the locus of adolescent risk from the physical to the psychological and from the external to the internal**: less time unsupervised in physical space, more time in mediated social evaluation. Twenge's own framing captures this as a trade — cohort declines in unsupervised face-to-face interaction reduce exposure to substances, cars and fights while increasing exposure to social comparison and asynchronous peer judgement [31].

There is a further, less comfortable reading. If adolescents are drinking less, fighting less and having less sex, some of the increase in reported internalising distress may reflect a genuine narrowing of adolescent experience — reduced autonomy, reduced unstructured play, reduced risk-taking of the developmentally normative kind — rather than a specific toxic exposure. This is the "decline of independent activity" strand of the argument, which is far less tested than the social-media strand and deserves the research attention it has not received.

### Finding 5: "Screen Time" as a Dose Variable Explains at Most 0.4% of Variance — the Construct, Not the Finding, Is the Problem

Orben and Przybylski's specification curve analysis remains the single most important methodological contribution to this field. Applying SCA across three large-scale datasets totalling **355,358 participants**, they found the association between digital technology use and adolescent well-being to be negative but to explain **at most 0.4% of variance**, concluding the effects were "too small to warrant policy change" [18]. Their widely quoted comparison — that the association is of a magnitude comparable to eating potatoes or wearing glasses — was rhetorically effective and substantively defensible.

Their measurement work is more damaging to the harm thesis than the effect-size work. In three nationally representative datasets from Ireland, the US and the UK (**n = 17,247 after exclusions**), using **time-use diaries** rather than retrospective self-report and employing both exploratory and confirmatory preregistered designs, they found "little evidence for substantial negative associations between digital-screen engagement — measured throughout the day or particularly before bedtime — and adolescent well-being" [19]. The sleep analysis is the most pointed: in the UK Millennium Cohort (**n = 11,884**), median standardised associations were **β = −0.06 on weekdays and −0.03 at weekends**, translating to an additional hour of daily screen time predicting a **9-minute** reduction in weekday sleep and a **3-minute** reduction at weekends, while using screens in the 30 minutes before bed predicted a **1-minute** reduction [20]. Crucially, the negative associations were "mainly driven by retrospective technology use measures" [20].

That last clause is the finding beneath the finding. Retrospective self-reported screen time correlates poorly with logged device use, and the measurement error is not random — distressed adolescents over-estimate their own use. A large fraction of the published screen-time literature therefore measures *self-perception of excessive use* while claiming to measure exposure, which inflates associations with distress by construction. When Ferguson tested the specific prediction that the screen–depression link should have *strengthened* over the sixteen years of smartphone diffusion, he found no evidence of increasing harm [25] — a direct falsification test of the "Great Rewiring" timing claim that the harm literature has not adequately answered.

### Finding 6: Problematic, Dyscontrolled Use Carries Effect Sizes an Order of Magnitude Larger Than Hours — This Is the Central Clinical Reframing

The literature separates cleanly once "how much" is distinguished from "how badly." Shannon and colleagues' meta-analysis of 18 studies (**n = 9,269**) found problematic social media use correlating with **depression r = 0.273, anxiety r = 0.348, and stress r = 0.313, all p < .001**, with no moderation by age, gender or publication year [43]. Ahmed and colleagues screened 6,108 articles, included **182 studies (n = 1,169,396)** in systematic review and **98 (n = 102,683)** in meta-analysis, and found small but significant positive associations for social media use with depression and anxiety — while **problematic social media use was consistently and more strongly associated with depression, anxiety, sleep problems, and reduced wellbeing** [42]. Mestre-Bach and colleagues, in a PROSPERO-registered meta-analysis of 23 studies (**n = 36,484**, ages 13–30), found internet-use-disorder symptoms associated with loneliness at **Cohen's d = 0.53 (95% CI 0.35–0.70)**, with no evidence of publication or small-study bias, and UCLA Loneliness scores of **49.35 versus 43.78** in those with and without symptoms [71].

Set these against the 0.4% of variance from dose measures [18] and the ordering is unambiguous: r ≈ 0.27–0.35 and d = 0.53 are clinically meaningful; β ≈ −0.03 is not. Marciano and colleagues quantified the gap directly within a single meta-analysis: ill-being correlated with social media *use* at **r = 0.171** but with media *addiction* at **r = 0.434** [46].

The interpretive problem is direction. Every one of these designs is cross-sectional or bidirectional, and loneliness plausibly drives compulsive use as readily as the reverse — indeed Orben's longitudinal work found that decreases in life satisfaction predicted subsequent increases in estimated social media use, without the age and sex patterning seen in the forward direction [32]. What the finding licenses is not a causal claim but a **measurement claim**: if you are going to measure anything, measure dyscontrol. And a **clinical claim**: the adolescent who cannot stop is a different patient from the adolescent who uses a lot, and only the first has an effect size worth acting on.

### Finding 7: Susceptibility Is Windowed by Age and Sex — Girls 11–13, Boys 14–15, Both at 19

Orben, Przybylski, Blakemore and Kievit analysed two UK datasets comprising **84,011 participants aged 10–80** cross-sectionally and **17,409 aged 10–21** longitudinally [32]. Three results follow. First, the cross-sectional negative association between estimated social media use and life satisfaction is **most negative in younger adolescents**, and sex differences in that association exist *only* during that period. Second, longitudinal modelling identified **distinct developmental windows in which higher estimated social media use predicted lower life satisfaction one year later: ages 11–13 and 19 in females, and ages 14–15 and 19 in males** [32]. Third, the relationship is bidirectional — decreases in life satisfaction also predicted subsequent increases in estimated use — but the reverse pathway showed **no age or sex patterning**, which is itself informative: the forward pathway looks developmental, the reverse pathway looks like general distress-driven behaviour.

The methodological provenance of this finding is what makes it decisive. Orben and Przybylski are the field's most prominent sceptics, working with their own preferred methods on their preferred datasets, and they nonetheless recovered structured, age- and sex-specific longitudinal effects. This is convergent evidence in the strong sense: a result that survives analysis by researchers motivated to find nothing.

The developmental logic is coherent. The female window at 11–13 aligns with pubertal onset, the peak of appearance-related social comparison, and the reorganisation of peer hierarchy; the male window at 14–15 aligns with later pubertal timing and with status competition shifting into gaming and peer-group ranking; the shared window at 19 aligns with the transition out of home and school and the loss of structural social scaffolding. Orben's mechanistic review proposes the mediating processes: changes to behaviour (risky content sharing, curated self-presentation) and to cognition (altered self-concept, intensified social comparison, heightened responsiveness to social feedback, and amplified experiences of social exclusion), each mapped onto normative adolescent developmental change rather than onto a generic toxicity [33].

Two caveats bound the finding. The exposure measure is **self-reported estimated use**, subject to the measurement error described in Finding 5. And the windows were derived from UK samples; whether the same ages apply in societies with different pubertal timing, schooling transitions and gender norms is untested — a point of direct relevance to Thai and other Asian practice.

### Finding 8: What Happens Online Predicts Better Than How Long — Cyberbullying, Comparison and Content Outperform Dose

The most methodologically interesting result in the recent prospective literature is that an *experience* variable beat a *dose* variable in the same adjusted model. Nagata and colleagues analysed **9,095 ABCD Study participants (mean age 12.0 ± 0.7)** and found lifetime cyberbullying victimisation prevalence of **9.2%** and past-12-month prevalence of **6.1%**. After adjustment for age, sex, race/ethnicity, household income, parental education, baseline manic symptoms, anxiety, depression, **and total screen time**, lifetime victimisation predicted a **0.41-point higher manic symptom score (95% CI 0.18–0.65, p = 0.001)** and past-12-month victimisation a **0.38-point increase (0.11–0.66, p = 0.007)** [70]. Adjusting away total screen time did not abolish the effect of being targeted, which is precisely the dissociation the dose literature keeps failing to find.

Type of use shows the same pattern. Twenge and Farley, analysing **11,427 UK adolescents aged 13–15**, found hours on social media and general internet use far more strongly associated with self-harm, depressive symptoms, low life satisfaction and low self-esteem than hours spent gaming or watching television; heavy internet users were **166% more likely than light users to have clinically relevant depressive symptoms among girls, against 75% among boys** [28]. Marciano's meta-analysis found the mirror image on the protective side: **one-to-one communication and self-disclosure were associated with better outcomes** even as media addiction was associated with worse [46]. West and colleagues' scoping review of 86 studies through a self-determination theory lens concluded that adolescent social media use **both supports and thwarts** relatedness, autonomy and competence depending on how it is used [67].

The mechanistic pathway is legible. Being targeted is a social-defeat exposure with an established stress-physiology signature; curated comparison is a chronic self-evaluative stressor; one-to-one disclosure is social support delivered through a different channel. Bundling all three into "hours on a phone" guarantees a near-null average, because the components have opposite signs. The clinical translation is that a history of "four hours a day" is close to uninformative, whereas "she is in a group chat where people rank each other's appearance" is actionable. Neurodivergent and psychiatrically ill adolescents carry elevated odds of both traditional and cyber-victimisation [48], which compounds the risk in exactly the population already in clinic.

### Finding 9: Prospective Cohort Evidence Supports a Small, Directionally Consistent Effect — the Best-Designed Studies Find Real but Modest Harm

Cross-sectional findings dominate this literature and cannot establish temporal order. The ABCD Study is the principal exception. Nagata and colleagues analysed **9,538 adolescents aged 9–10 at baseline (2016–2018) with two years of follow-up**, using self-reported baseline screen time and **parent-reported** Child Behavior Checklist outcomes — a deliberate separation of informant that removes shared-method variance. In models adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, household income, parental education and study site, higher total screen time was prospectively associated with **all** symptom domains, strongest for **depressive symptoms (B = 0.10, 95% CI 0.06–0.13, p < 0.001)**, then conduct (B = 0.07, 0.03–0.10), somatic (B = 0.06, 0.01–0.11, p = 0.026) and ADHD symptoms (B = 0.06, 0.01–0.10, p = 0.013) [68]. Subtypes with the strongest links to depressive symptoms were **video chat, texting, videos and video games** [68].

Two features of this study deserve emphasis. The association was **stronger in White than Black adolescents, and stronger in White than Asian adolescents for depressive symptoms** [68] — a moderation almost never discussed, and one that undercuts any universal-toxicity model while raising the possibility that the meaning and social context of use, rather than the exposure itself, carries the effect. And the authors' own summary is that "effect sizes are small" [68]. This is the highest-quality prospective evidence available in a US sample, and it lands between the two camps: direction supports the harm thesis, magnitude supports the sceptics.

Complementary prospective evidence comes from time-diary studies. McAllister and colleagues used the **UK Millennium Cohort (n = 4,252, ages 13–15)** with contemporaneous weekday and weekend time diaries — Orben's own preferred instrument — and found girls spending ≥2 hours/day on social media had an adjusted relative risk of **1.46 (95% CI 1.17–1.82)** for self-harm and on internet use **1.80 (1.20–2.70)**, with depression ARRs of 1.29 (1.03–1.63) and 1.75 (1.19–2.59); gaming, texting and television showed few significant associations, and **associations for boys were mostly non-significant** [29]. Robertson and colleagues used **ABCD (n = 11,780)** with the **K-SADS-5 semi-structured diagnostic interview** rather than a symptom scale, finding ≥2 hours/day of screen media associated with meeting criteria for depressive disorders, self-harm and suicidal ideation or attempts after demographic adjustment [30].

### Finding 10: The Causal Pathway Runs Through Problematic Use, Not Through Sleep — Which Contradicts the Most Popular Public Mechanism

The best-known popular account of digital harm nominates sleep deprivation as a primary pathway. The mediation data do not support it as a *primary* pathway. Nagata and colleagues tested it explicitly in **9,243 ABCD participants**, modelling Year-1 screen exposure (ages 10–11) against Year-3 manic symptoms with Year-2 candidate mediators. Total screen time predicted manic symptoms (**B = 0.05, 95% CI 0.03–0.07, p < 0.001**), as did social media (**B = 0.20, 0.09–0.32**), texting (B = 0.18), videos (B = 0.14) and video games (B = 0.09). Formal mediation analysis attributed **47.7% to problematic social media use, 58.0% to problematic video game use, and only 9.0% to sleep duration** [69].

Sleep is not irrelevant — it is a real mediator with a real coefficient — but it is a minor one relative to dyscontrol, and the two prior sources on sleep magnitude disagree sharply with each other in a way that itself illuminates the field. Twenge, Krizan and Hisler, using repeated cross-sectional self-report, found US adolescents in 2015 were **16–17% more likely than in 2009 to report under seven hours' sleep on most nights**, with an inflection after 2011–2013, an exposure–response relationship beyond two hours of device use per day, and — the argument's strongest element — that homework time, paid work and television viewing were **stable or falling** across the same window, narrowing the field of candidate causes [81]. Orben and Przybylski, using **time-use diaries in 11,884 UK adolescents**, found an additional hour of daily screen time associated with a **9-minute** weekday sleep reduction and pre-bed use with a **1-minute** reduction [20]. Both are competently executed. They differ almost entirely by instrument, and the instrument that measures behaviour directly returns the smaller number.

The clinical implication reverses the usual advice hierarchy. Blanket screen curfews target the weakest link in the chain. Assessing and treating dyscontrol — the compulsive checking, the failed self-imposed limits, the withdrawal irritability, the conflict — targets the pathway carrying half the mediated effect. Where sleep *is* disrupted, it is worth treating in its own right as an established transdiagnostic risk factor, but not on the assumption that fixing it will fix the rest.

### Finding 11: The Trend Is Not Globally Uniform — Cross-National Heterogeneity Is the Strongest Structural Argument Against a Single Dominant Cause

If smartphone diffusion were the dominant driver, adolescent mental health should have deteriorated with roughly comparable timing and magnitude across every market where diffusion was comparable. It did not. Cross-national analysis of HBSC trends from 2002 to 2018 found "country differences in trends in both adolescent mental well-being outcomes and schoolwork pressure were considerable," with the aggregate picture showing only **a small but significant increase in psychosomatic complaints and a stable pattern in life satisfaction** [79]. Person-oriented analyses of Nordic 15-year-olds from 2002 to 2022 found Sweden consistently worse than its neighbours but broadly similar trend shapes across countries — that is, level differences without the sharp common inflection the hypothesis predicts [79].

Livingstone's critique makes the sharper point about data selection: the popular account plots the proportion of Nordic teenagers with high psychological distress and highlights its rise between 2010 and 2015, while the **overall HBSC results across more than 100,000 youths worldwide showed no increase in psychological symptoms and no decrease in life satisfaction between 2002 and 2014** [78]. Whether one regards this as cherry-picking or as appropriate focus on the most affected subgroups, it establishes that the global aggregate does not carry the claim.

Non-Western data complicate the picture further and are almost entirely absent from the debate. Thai cross-sectional research reports **72.2% of adolescent participants scoring in the mild (44.5%) or at-least-moderate (27.7%) depression range**, with approximately 37.8% categorised at risk and 22% of those reporting suicidal ideation [17]. Those figures are not comparable to Western prevalence estimates because they derive from screening instruments rather than diagnostic interviews, and screening scales systematically over-identify — which is exactly the point. Meanwhile a 2024 regional survey found Thai Gen Z respondents reporting anxiety or stress "all or most of the time" **falling from 51% to 40%** year-on-year, with top-ranked concerns being cost of living (37%), unemployment (36%) and income inequality (21%) rather than anything digital [17].

The methodological lesson generalises: **an absolute prevalence figure is only interpretable against the instrument that produced it and the population it sampled**, and cross-national comparison of adolescent mental health is currently closer to comparing thermometers than temperatures.

### Finding 12: A Substantial Fraction of the Apparent Increase Is Ascertainment — and the ADHD and Autism Data Show Its Signature Clearly

The strongest single piece of evidence for ascertainment comes from UK primary care. Trafford and colleagues analysed Clinical Practice Research Datalink records for ages 1–24 from January 2010 to March 2022, using negative binomial regression on pre-pandemic trends to predict expected post-March-2020 incidence. In the two years after March 2020, **ADHD diagnosis incidence in females ran 24.7% (95% CI 11.9–38.9) above the predicted rate**, with the excess concentrated in females aged **20–24, then 17–19, and in those from *less deprived* areas**; ADHD medication showed the same pattern. Critically, **incidence of other diagnoses — including anxiety and depression — was at or below expected levels** [7].

Read the shape of that result carefully. A genuine environmental exposure acting on a vulnerable population should produce excess morbidity that is *greater* in more deprived areas, should appear in the age groups most exposed, and should not be confined to a single diagnostic category while others fall. What Trafford found is the inverse on every axis: an excess concentrated in **adult women from wealthier areas in one diagnostic category**. That is the signature of awareness, recognition and access — of a diagnostic category expanding to capture people who previously went unidentified — not of a new toxin. The authors say so: "Increased ADHD awareness may partly explain the study's findings" [7].

Autism surveillance shows the same fingerprint in a different form. CDC's ADDM Network identified ASD in **1 in 31 US eight-year-olds in 2022, up from 1 in 36 (2020), 1 in 54 (2016) and 1 in 150 (2000)**, with **47% of diagnoses made before age four** and — for the first time — higher identified prevalence in Asian/Pacific Islander, Black, Hispanic and multiracial than in White children, reversing a long-standing under-ascertainment pattern [82]. The decisive datum is the geography: identified prevalence ranged from **53.1 per 1,000 in California to 9.7 per 1,000 in Laredo, Texas — a 5.5-fold gap inside a single country** [82]. No biological account explains a 5.5-fold true-prevalence difference between two US surveillance sites; the difference tracks evaluation infrastructure, service eligibility rules and case-finding intensity.

None of this means the increases are illusory. Improved recognition of ADHD in women corrects a genuine historical injustice; earlier autism identification enables earlier intervention. But it means that **time-trend graphs of diagnosed prevalence cannot be read as time-trend graphs of incidence**, and that any causal model calibrated against diagnosed prevalence is fitting a mixture of signal and case-finding.

### Finding 13: Prevalence Inflation and Iatrogenic Harm from Universal Prevention Are Now Measurable, Not Merely Hypothesised

Foulkes and Andrews proposed the prevalence-inflation hypothesis in 2023: that mental-health awareness efforts raise reported rates through two distinct mechanisms — **improved recognition**, in which genuinely under-reported problems are now correctly identified and reported, and **overinterpretation**, in which ordinary psychological experience is relabelled as disorder, which may then become **self-fulfilling** (reading mild anxiety as an anxiety disorder → behavioural avoidance → genuine anxiety) [62]. A 2024 refinement paper works to disentangle overinterpretation from the self-fulfilling-prophecy component, since the two have different implications for intervention [62].

The hypothesis has now met trial data. The MYRIAD cluster-randomised trial delivered school-based mindfulness training to **8,376 pupils across 84 UK secondary schools**, mean age 12.2 (SD 0.6), 54.9% female. The primary trial found that mindfulness training versus teaching-as-usual produced **worse** depression-risk and wellbeing scores among pupils already at risk of mental-health problems, at post-intervention and at one-year follow-up — small differences, not clinically significant, but in the wrong direction [74]. The 2026 secondary analysis applied causal forests and elastic-net regression with school-level nested cross-validation to find who might benefit; both models identified a subgroup, but **group differences were negligible (causal forest d = 0.07, 95% CI 0.02–0.12; elastic net d = 0.08, 0.02–0.13)**, and the authors concluded that machine learning identified "statistically detectable but clinically trivial differential intervention response," highlighting "the substantial challenges in achieving clinically useful personalization in universal school-based prevention programs" [73].

The pattern extends beyond mindfulness. A scoping review of group mental-health interventions found that **9% of all trials and 33% of high-quality trials reported at least one negative effect, and every negative effect occurred in universal rather than targeted interventions** [74]. Students taught CBT skills in universal programmes have sometimes shown *increased* internalising symptoms relative to controls [74].

The plausible mechanisms are specific: universal programmes teach symptom vocabulary to adolescents who did not previously have it, invite introspective symptom monitoring in a developmental period characterised by heightened self-focus, and deliver co-rumination opportunities in peer groups. In a targeted programme these costs are offset by treating genuine pathology. In a universal programme applied to a mostly well population, the costs land on everyone and the benefits on few — which is exactly the arithmetic MYRIAD produced.

### Finding 14: Functional Tic-Like Behaviours Are a Genuinely New Clinical Entity, More Disabling Than Tourette Syndrome, and Diagnostically Unsettled

From 2020–2021, movement-disorder clinics across multiple countries saw an abrupt influx of predominantly adolescent and young-adult females presenting with sudden-onset complex tic-like movements and vocalisations, temporally associated with the proliferation of tic content on TikTok. Müller-Vahl and colleagues assembled the largest series of comorbid presentations: **71 patients with Tourette syndrome plus functional tic-like behaviours** (27 female [38.0%], mean age 21.5, range 11–55). In most, FTLB began **abruptly, on average 15 years after tic onset**, with "treatment-resistant" complex movements and coprophenomena-like vocalisations **preceded by timely-related psychological stressors**. Psychological evaluation found evidence of **internal conflicts in 79%, emotional dysregulation in 56%, and maintaining factors in 70%**, with about a third having a history of other medically unexplained symptoms. Compared against a large TS reference sample (n = 1,032), TS+FTLB patients were **more likely female, and presented significantly more often with coprophenomena-like symptoms, atypical influential factors, atypical descriptions of premonitory sensations, and higher rates of comorbid OCD and self-injurious behaviour** [53]. Their clinical instruction is unambiguous: *before classifying a patient as having treatment-resistant Tourette syndrome, rule out FTLB*.

The public-health investigation of a Minnesota school cluster (September–November 2021) provides the sociogenic-transmission evidence. Eight students developed tic-like behaviours lasting more than 24 hours; **all eight were female, aged 15–17**; **all** either had a documented history of depression or anxiety or screened above minimal on validated instruments. Four had prior confirmed COVID-19, but the interval to symptom onset varied from over a year before to concurrent with onset, and the investigators concluded the outbreak related **more to underlying mental-health conditions than to recent COVID-19** [54].

Berg and colleagues quantified the burden. In 82 age- and birth-sex-matched participants (35 FTLB, 22 TS, 25 neurotypical controls, ages 11–25, **90% female at birth**), FTLB participants had lower quality of life, greater disability, loneliness, social phobia, anxiety, depressive symptoms and suicidality than controls — and against **Tourette syndrome** patients they had **more school and work absence due to tics, more depressive symptoms, higher risk of suicidality, and greater disability in self-care and life-activity domains** [55]. This must be stated plainly because the public framing has been dismissive: **FTLB is more disabling than Tourette syndrome on several measured axes.** A further finding of clinical importance: **15 of 35 FTLB participants identified as transgender or gender-diverse, against 2 of 22 in TS and 3 of 25 controls**, with no differences between cisgender and gender-diverse FTLB participants on any measured domain [55] — the association is with the presentation, not with a different severity profile.

The construct is contested from inside the field, which is a mark of health rather than weakness. Andersen and colleagues identify **circular reasoning** in FTLB diagnosis (there is no independent benchmark, so the criteria risk defining what they were built to detect), potential bias in the reported phenomenology and demographics, contamination of the broader tic literature by mixed samples, and imperfect alignment with established functional-disorder criteria [56]. Cavanna and colleagues have since applied European Society for the Study of Tourette Syndrome criteria to validate 63 TS+FTLB cases against matched controls [57]. Against the baseline that TS affects roughly 1% of school-aged boys, that around 90% of TS patients have at least one psychiatric comorbidity, and that fewer than 25% still have moderate-to-severe tics in adulthood [58], the FTLB population is demographically inverted and prognostically distinct.

### Finding 15: Social-Media-Mediated Self-Diagnosis Measurably Shifts Illness Beliefs in Both Directions

Self-diagnosis has moved from anecdote to measurable phenomenon. Karasavva and colleagues examined belief change after exposure to popular **#ADHD** TikTok content and found a bidirectional effect: participants who had disclosed a self-diagnosis of ADHD became **more certain they had it** after viewing, while participants who did not believe they had ADHD became **less certain that they did not** [60]. That is a media effect on illness cognition in both directions from the same stimulus — precisely the mechanism the prevalence-inflation hypothesis predicts, demonstrated experimentally.

Qualitative work maps the psychological function. Studies applying social-psychological theories of identity, belonging and self-labelling find the drivers to be identity formation and peer-group belonging rather than naivety or attention-seeking [59]. Clinicians report that identity-seeking and peer relationships are salient in how adolescent self-diagnoses form [59]. Analyses of young adults on mental-health Instagram and TikTok describe a culture of self-care, self-diagnosis and the performance of a "mentally healthy self" — in which having and managing a diagnosis is itself a social competence. Psychoanalytically framed critiques warn that adoption of the **sick role** can supply a coherent identity to an adolescent lacking one, but can then entrench a rigid narrative of being unwell that actively impedes therapy [59].

The clinical stance this warrants is neither validation nor dismissal. Treating a self-diagnosis as automatically correct abandons diagnostic responsibility and may consolidate an identity that predicts poor outcome. Treating it as fabrication ruptures alliance immediately and misses the substantial minority whose self-identification is accurate — the same recognition improvement documented in Finding 12 means many self-diagnosing young women *do* have ADHD. The workable position is to take the self-diagnosis seriously as **data about the patient's experience and about what they need the label to do**, while conducting an independent assessment: *what does having this explain for you? what would change if it turned out to be something else?* The second question is frequently more diagnostically informative than the first.

### Finding 16: Gender-Diverse Presentations Rose Steeply, Inverted in Sex Ratio, and Sit on an Evidence Base the Cass Review Found Largely Absent

NHS England's Gender Identity Development Service recorded referrals of adolescent girls rising **from 15 in 2009 to 1,071 in 2016** — approximately a seventy-fold increase over seven years [63]. The Amsterdam Cohort of Gender Dysphoria, covering the first twenty years of the Dutch Protocol, documents a steep referral increase together with an inversion of the sex ratio: among younger presenters **41.1% assigned male at birth and 58.9% assigned female**, and among older presenters **29.8% AMAB against 70.2% AFAB** [64]. Comparative work across four Nordic countries and the UK confirms the pattern is international rather than a single service's artefact [64].

The Cass Review, commissioned by NHS England in autumn 2020 and reporting in April 2024 after four years, reached conclusions on the treatment evidence base that are notable chiefly for what they could not find. It reported **no credible evidence of psychological benefit from puberty suppression** in gender-dysphoric youth and recommended that puberty blockers be available only within a clinical trial; found cross-sex hormones lacked high-quality outcome research, such that "no conclusions can be drawn about the effect on gender dysphoria, body satisfaction, psychosocial health, cognitive development, or fertility"; and concluded that professionals and families "should be aware of the absence of robust evidence of the benefits or harms of social transition." On psychosocial interventions it found that "most analyses of mental health and psychosocial outcomes showed either benefit or no change, with none indicating negative or adverse effects," while noting that the low number and quality of studies limits conclusions [63].

Three things must be held simultaneously and this report will not collapse them. **The epidemiological shift is documented and large.** **The intervention evidence base is weak by the standards applied to any other paediatric intervention**, which is the Cass Review's central and defensible finding. And **the aetiological interpretation is genuinely unresolved and fiercely politically contested** — proposed explanations range from reduced stigma unmasking existing prevalence, through diagnostic and service expansion, to social influence, and the available data do not adjudicate between them. A clinician should be sceptical of any source, in either political direction, that presents this as settled. The clinically actionable residue is that these young people have high rates of co-occurring psychiatric morbidity requiring assessment and treatment in their own right — including, per Finding 14, a marked over-representation among FTLB presentations [55].

### Finding 17: Generation Alpha's Exposure Differs in Kind, Not Degree — It Begins Pre-Verbally and Is Mediated by the Parent

Every finding above concerns adolescents who acquired devices after language, attachment and basic self-regulation were established. Generation Alpha is the first cohort with substantial screen exposure **before those systems consolidate**, and the developmental question is therefore different in kind rather than in dose.

The prospective cohort evidence indicates a dose–response relationship. McArthur and colleagues followed **1,994 mother–child dyads** in the All Our Families cohort; at 36 months, compared with children using screens ≤1 hour/day (47% of the sample), those at 2 hours/day (36%) or **≥3 hours/day (17%)** had increased likelihood of adverse developmental and behavioural outcomes, adjusting for sociodemographic factors and for baseline performance at 24 months [49]. A 2025 study of language development delay — which affects 5–10% of preschoolers globally — found children with LDD had **0.4 hours/day more screen exposure, with 30.97% exceeding two hours daily against 12.26% of controls** [51].

That same study contains the finding that reframes the whole area. **Parents in the LDD group spent significantly more time on screens themselves — fathers +0.34 hours/day, mothers +0.32 hours/day** [51]. This is "technoference": the mechanism may operate less through what the child watches than through the serve-and-return interaction the child does not receive because the caregiver is attending elsewhere. Language acquisition is exquisitely dependent on contingent responsive interaction, and a distracted caregiver degrades it regardless of the child's own screen exposure.

Disconfirming evidence exists and must be weighted. A study of toddlers and preschoolers in Al Qatif, Saudi Arabia found that **daily screen time duration alone was not significantly associated with language scores** once parental education and supervision were modelled [52]. Co-viewing, verbal scaffolding during viewing, content quality and socioeconomic position swamp raw duration — the same lesson Finding 6 established for adolescents, arriving independently in a different developmental period and a different culture.

### Finding 18: Early-Childhood Screen Time Is Partly a Marker of Cumulative Adversity, Not Only an Independent Exposure

The confounding objection is usually asserted. McArthur and colleagues demonstrated it. In a birth cohort of **1,992 mother–child dyads**, they measured prenatal cumulative risk — maternal income, education, depression, stress, marital status, housing instability, unemployment, and maternal history of childhood adversity — and tested whether the association between that distal risk and child socioemotional and developmental outcomes was **mediated by two proximal factors: children's screen time and parenting quality** (ineffective/hostile and positive interactions). It was [50].

The causal structure this implies is not "screens harm children." It is closer to: **adversity constrains parenting capacity; constrained parenting increases screen use; screen use and degraded parenting jointly predict worse child outcomes.** Screen time in early childhood therefore functions substantially as a *marker* of a stressed caregiving environment. A depressed, unsupported, housing-insecure mother hands the child a tablet because it is the only available respite — and in any observational dataset, the tablet will carry statistical blame for the depression, the insecurity and the absent support.

Two consequences follow, one methodological and one ethical. Methodologically, any study of early-childhood screen exposure that does not measure and adjust for parental mental health, socioeconomic position and parenting quality is measuring adversity with a screen-shaped instrument — and much of the published literature does not. Ethically, the clinical and policy response of telling parents to reduce screen time addresses the most visible and least causal element in the chain, at the cost of adding guilt to families already carrying the underlying burden. The intervention target implied by the mediation model is **parenting support and the material conditions that constrain it**, with screen reduction as a downstream consequence rather than the primary lever.

This does not license dismissing screen exposure. Mediation means the pathway runs *through* screen time, which remains a real proximal contributor and a modifiable one. But it forbids the naive reading of the dose–response curve in Finding 17 as a pure exposure effect.

### Finding 19: AI Companions Are the Next Exposure and the Evidence Base Is Empty — Adoption Preceded Any Trial

Common Sense Media surveyed a nationally representative sample of **1,060 US teenagers aged 13–17 in April–May 2025** and found that **72% had used an AI companion; 52% interacted at least a few times a month; 34% used them daily or several times a week; one in three had used them for social interaction and relationships; and 31% reported conversations with AI companions as satisfying or more satisfying than talking with real friends** [23a]. A further **34% of users reported feeling uncomfortable with something a companion had said or done**, and **younger teens (13–14) were more likely than older teens (15–17) to trust the advice these systems gave** [23a].

Set that adoption curve against the evidence base. There is no randomised trial of AI companion use in adolescents. There is no cohort study with meaningful follow-up. PubMed searching in Wave 3 returned a total of three indexed articles for adolescent AI companion mental-health risk. The exposure has reached roughly three-quarters of a national adolescent population **before the first well-powered study has been published** — a faster diffusion-to-evidence gap than social media itself, which at least had a decade of correlational work before saturation.

The American Psychological Association issued two health advisories in 2025 in this vacuum [75]. The clinically operative points: **adolescents are less likely than adults to question the accuracy and intent of information offered by a bot as compared with a human**; adolescents' relationships with AI entities **may displace or interfere with the development of healthy real-world relationships**; chatbots and wellness apps must not replace care from a qualified mental-health professional given the unpredictability of these systems; developers should prioritise features that prevent exploitation, manipulation and erosion of real-world relationships; and adolescent data should not be used for advertising, personalised marketing or third-party sale. The advisory makes one concrete process recommendation to clinicians: **ask patients what generative-AI tools and wellness apps they are using, and educate them about benefits and limitations**, because this helps identify when guidance is unhelpful, unsafe, or inconsistent with the treatment plan [75]. The AAP's Center of Excellence on Social Media and Youth Mental Health has issued parallel counselling guidance [76].

The theoretical risk profile is distinctive and worth stating precisely, because it differs from social media's. Social media harms are hypothesised to run through **social comparison and peer evaluation** — other humans, mediated. AI companions remove the other humans: they offer unconditional positive regard, infinite availability, zero social risk and no reciprocal demand. For an adolescent with social anxiety, that is simultaneously the most attractive available option and the most complete possible avoidance behaviour. The mechanism to watch is not comparison-driven distress but **negative reinforcement of social avoidance during the developmental window in which social competence is acquired** — a hypothesis that is testable, currently untested, and consistent with the APA's displacement concern.

### Finding 20: The Population Interventions Tested So Far Have Failed — and Australia's Ban Is the Live Experiment

The SMART Schools study compared mental wellbeing, sleep and educational outcomes among **1,227 adolescents aged 12–15 across 30 UK schools — 20 with restrictive phone policies prohibiting recreational use, 10 permissive**. It found **no evidence that restrictive school policies were associated with better mental wellbeing, or with lower overall phone and social media use** [39]. The authors' explanation is the important part: the policies reduced use *during school hours* without reducing *total daily* use, and there was consequently no exposure change to produce an outcome change [39]. The accompanying commentary concluded that policy must go "beyond school restriction policies" [40].

This is a null result with a clear mechanism, which makes it far more informative than a bare null. It tells us that displacement, not prohibition, is the binding constraint: adolescents redistributed their use rather than reducing it. It also tells us that the intervention most widely adopted by schools worldwide — the in-school phone ban — is not, on current evidence, a mental-health intervention. It may still be justified as an *educational* intervention: Twenge's PISA analysis of **1,788,128 students across 36 countries** found declines in mathematics, reading and science and increases in school loneliness that were larger in countries with greater increases in smartphone access and in-school leisure device use [9]. Attention in class and adolescent depression are different outcomes and should be argued for separately.

**Australia's Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act commenced on 10 December 2025**, barring under-16s from holding accounts on TikTok, Snapchat, YouTube, Reddit, Instagram, Facebook, Kick, Twitch, Threads and X, with penalties to approximately A$50 million and — a design choice with real consequences — **parental consent explicitly unable to override the restriction**. Within days, platforms removed **4.7 million under-16 Australian accounts** [77]. Early implementation reports document circumvention (children defeating facial age-estimation, assistance from parents and older siblings) and the anticipated equity objection: that prohibition may isolate vulnerable adolescents — LGBTQ+, rural, disabled, and those whose only peer community is online — and displace them into less-moderated spaces [77].

This is the single most consequential forthcoming evidence event in the field. If Australian adolescent mental-health indicators improve on a timescale and magnitude inconsistent with secular trend, the harm thesis gains the causal evidence it currently lacks. If they do not, the strongest available version of the policy prescription will have been tested and failed. Either result will be contested on the ground that a two- or three-year window is too short and that circumvention diluted the exposure contrast — both fair objections, and both foreseeable now rather than after the fact.

### Finding 21: Parental Warmth Predicts Less Problematic Use; Media-Specific Rules Do Not, and May Backfire in Older Adolescents

The most actionable and least publicised finding in this entire literature concerns what parents should actually do. Lukavská and colleagues meta-analysed associations between adolescent problematic internet use and parenting across studies to April 2022, distinguishing **general** parenting from **media-specific** parenting. General parenting showed consistent weak protective associations: **warmth r = −0.17 (k = 24, N = 58,401)**, control r = −0.10 (k = 10, N = 12,199), authoritative parenting r = −0.12 (k = 8, N = 5,431). Media-specific parenting showed **nothing**: active mediation r = −0.02 (k = 11, N = 30,545) and restrictive mediation r = +0.01 (k = 16, N = 36,997), both non-significant. And **in older adolescents, restrictive mediation was significantly *positively* associated with problematic internet use** [72].

The authors' own conclusion is appropriately cautious — "media parenting has only weak association with PIU and thus restrictions should be used cautiously, especially in older adolescents" — and the design is cross-sectional, so the positive association in older adolescents is at least partly reverse causation: parents impose restrictions *because* use has become problematic. But the asymmetry between general and media-specific parenting is not explicable by reverse causation alone, and it is consistent across a very large pooled sample.

This directly contradicts the dominant public advice, which is overwhelmingly rule-focused: screen-time limits, app blockers, device curfews, parental-control software. The evidence says the relationship carries the effect and the rules do not. It converges with Finding 18's mediation result — parenting quality was a mediator of child outcomes alongside screen time [50] — and with Finding 20's displacement finding: restriction in one context produces redistribution rather than reduction [39]. A coherent parental-guidance message from the current evidence would be: **invest in the relationship, keep devices out of bedrooms overnight, co-view with younger children, ask what is happening online rather than counting hours, and expect rules alone to accomplish little — particularly after age 14.**

The meta-observation across this table is the most important single statement in the report: **in this field, the analytic pipeline is a larger source of variance than the phenomenon.** Effect estimates move by an order of magnitude on specification choices [18][26], by a factor of nine on instrument choice for sleep [20][81], and by fivefold on surveillance-site choice for autism [82]. Any single-number claim from this literature — in either direction — should be treated as a claim about a pipeline until the specification space is shown.

### 4.3 Knowledge Gaps

These are specific and named, not "more research is needed."

1. **Clinical populations are nearly absent.** Fassi and colleagues' meta-analysis covered 143 studies and 1,094,890 adolescents, of which **only 11% of effect sizes came from clinical samples** [23]. Practising psychiatrists therefore treat patients about whom the exposure literature is almost silent. Required: cohort studies recruiting from CAMHS caseloads, measuring platform-specific and dyscontrol exposures against clinician-rated outcomes.

2. **No AI-companion evidence exists at all.** 72% adoption, three indexed PubMed articles [23a]. Required urgently: a prospective cohort of adolescent companion-app users measuring social avoidance, real-world relationship formation, and — given documented crisis-response failures — self-harm outcomes, plus experimental work on whether companion use functions as negative reinforcement of social avoidance in socially anxious adolescents.

3. **Generation Alpha adolescence is unobserved.** The oldest Gen Alpha members reach 15–16 in 2025–2026. Every claim about their adolescent psychiatric outcomes is extrapolation from a cohort with a fundamentally different early-childhood exposure history. Required: the ABCD-equivalent for a birth cohort with pre-verbal tablet exposure, which does not yet exist at scale.

4. **Cross-national heterogeneity is documented but unexplained** [78][79]. Nobody has systematically tested which country-level moderators — schooling pressure, welfare generosity, service capacity, gender norms, pubertal timing, screening intensity — account for why comparably saturated markets show divergent trends. Required: multilevel modelling of HBSC/PISA with country-level policy and service covariates.

5. **Non-Western evidence is nearly absent from the causal debate.** The entire dispute is conducted on US, UK, Nordic, Dutch and Australian data. Thai screening data show 72.2% with at least mild depression scores [17], a figure meaningless without instrument-matched comparison. Required: diagnostic-interview-based prevalence studies in Southeast Asia with the same instruments used in Western cohorts.

6. **Sex-difference mechanism is assumed, not demonstrated.** Every dataset shows girls at higher risk [3][4][26][28][29][32], and every explanation — appearance comparison, relational aggression, earlier puberty, differential platform use — is plausible and untested against the others. Required: a study that measures all four candidate mediators simultaneously in the same cohort.

7. **FTLB has no independent diagnostic benchmark**, creating the circular reasoning Andersen and colleagues identify [56]. Required: a biomarker or blinded phenomenological standard permitting FTLB/TS discrimination without reference to the criteria under test, plus prospective outcome data.

8. **No study has tested the displacement hypothesis directly.** SMART Schools showed bans fail because total use does not change [39]. Nobody has yet run an intervention that successfully reduces *total* adolescent use and measured the mental-health consequence — which is the actual test of the causal claim, and which Australia is now inadvertently running [77].

### 4.4 Emerging Signals

Five preliminary signals may shift the consensus and are flagged as unvalidated.

**The 2021–2024 partial reversal.** US adolescent MDE fell from 20% to 15.4% [4], YRBS sadness from 42% to 40% [3], and youth suicide fell in 2022 by 18% and 9% in the two age bands [5]. If this consolidates across three or four more data years while digital exposure remains flat or rises, it substantially weakens simple exposure models and strengthens pandemic-period explanations. If it is a one-year artefact, the harm thesis is unaffected. **This is the number to watch.**

**Problematic use as the unifying construct.** Dyscontrol outperforms dose across every design and outcome examined here [42][43][46][69][71], and mediates half the prospective effect [69]. A field-wide shift from hours-based to dyscontrol-based exposure measurement would probably resolve much of the apparent contradiction — and would move the phenomenon into the behavioural-addiction framework, with all of that framework's own construct-validity problems [80].

**Ascertainment as a quantifiable rather than rhetorical factor.** Trafford's use of pre-pandemic trend prediction to quantify excess ADHD incidence [7] is a template: it converts "some of this is awareness" from an assertion into an estimate. Applying the same method across diagnoses and countries would allow the incidence and ascertainment components to be separated rather than argued about.

**Iatrogenic prevention as a research programme.** MYRIAD's negative personalisation result [73] plus the finding that all documented negative effects occur in universal interventions [74] is converging toward a defensible position that **universal mental-health promotion in schools should be paused pending evidence**, which would be a significant policy reversal.

**AI companions as an avoidance technology rather than a comparison technology.** If the mechanism is negative reinforcement of social avoidance rather than social comparison, the at-risk profile inverts: the vulnerable adolescent is the socially anxious withdrawn one rather than the appearance-focused socially competitive one, and the intervention is exposure-based rather than restriction-based. Entirely untested [23a][75].

## 5. Analysis

### 5.1 Cross-Cutting Patterns

Looking across all twenty-one findings, four patterns emerge that no individual finding states.

**Pattern 1 — every robust result is a disaggregation, and every failed result is an aggregate.** The aggregate association between screen time and well-being is 0.4% of variance [18]. Disaggregate by use-type and it strengthens [28]. Disaggregate by sex and it strengthens further [26][29]. Disaggregate by age window and it acquires structure [32]. Disaggregate by dyscontrol and it becomes clinically substantial [43][71]. Disaggregate by experience — being cyberbullied — and it survives adjustment for dose entirely [70]. The same holds outside the digital domain: "adolescent mental health is worsening" is false as an aggregate (substance use fell [15]) and true as a stratified claim (internalising in girls rose [3]). The field's central methodological error has been to average over heterogeneous populations, exposures and outcomes, and then to argue about the average.

**Pattern 2 — the strongest evidence for harm and the strongest evidence for scepticism come from the same studies.** Orben and Przybylski, the field's leading sceptics, produced the developmental-windows finding [32] that is the most clinically useful harm result available. Nagata's ABCD analyses find prospective associations across every symptom domain and simultaneously report that "effect sizes are small" [68]. Fassi and Orben's meta-analysis finds significant associations in clinical samples and uses them to argue the evidence base is inadequate [23]. This is what a maturing field looks like: the same data support "real but small" from one side and "small but real" from the other, and the disagreement is about decision thresholds rather than about facts.

**Pattern 3 — measurement error is systematically directional, and it inflates.** Retrospective self-reported screen time correlates poorly with logged use, and distressed adolescents over-report [19][20]. Screening scales over-identify relative to diagnostic interviews [17]. Awareness campaigns increase symptom endorsement independent of symptom change [62]. Behavioural-addiction instruments produce prevalence estimates driven by cut-off choice [45][80]. Every one of these biases runs in the direction of finding more pathology and stronger associations. A field in which all the measurement error points one way will systematically overestimate both prevalence and effect size, and the corrections applied so far have been partial.

**Pattern 4 — the interventions that have failed all share a structure: they target the population rather than the individual, and the exposure rather than the vulnerability.** School phone bans failed because they changed context without changing total exposure [39]. Universal school mindfulness failed and harmed the at-risk subgroup [73][74]. Media-specific parenting rules show null associations and backfire in older adolescents [72]. What has *not* failed — because it has barely been tried at scale — is targeting: identifying the girl in her 11–13 window with dyscontrolled appearance-focused use and active cyberbullying exposure, and intervening on her specifically. The evidence architecture points at stratified intervention while the policy architecture keeps building universal ones.

### 5.2 Causal Analysis

**Where the chain is strong.** The link from dyscontrolled use to internalising symptoms is supported by four independent meta-analyses with combined samples exceeding 1.2 million [42][43][46][71], by formal mediation analysis in a prospective cohort attributing 48–58% of effect to this pathway [69], and by consistency across cultures and instruments. The link from cyberbullying victimisation to symptoms survives adjustment for total screen time in a prospective design [70]. The link from cumulative adversity through parenting and screen time to child developmental outcome is formally demonstrated as mediation, not asserted [50].

**Where the chain is weak.** The link from *volume* of use to outcome is real but explains ≤0.4% of variance [18]. The sleep pathway, despite its popularity, carries 9% of mediated effect [69]. The displacement pathway — that reduced face-to-face contact harms even non-users [31] — is theoretically attractive, has ecological support from PISA loneliness trends [9], and has never been tested at the individual level with adequate design. And the top-level claim that the digital environment is the *dominant* cause of the epidemiological shift fails the cross-national uniformity test [78][79] and cannot account for the 2021–2024 partial reversal [3][4][5].

**How the chain could be interrupted.** At the dyscontrol node, by treating problematic use as a clinical target in its own right. At the content node, by reducing exposure to appearance-focused and victimising content — the only point where platform design changes would plausibly matter, and the point the NASEM report identified [36]. At the moderator layer, by parental warmth, which shows the only consistent protective association measured [72]. Notably, *not* at the volume node, where every tested intervention has failed [39][72].

### 5.3 Framework Application

**Framework 1 — Evidence Hierarchy Pyramid.** Distributing the evidence by CEBM level exposes where the field's weight actually sits.

| — | Population surveillance / register | 11 | Trend magnitudes [3][4][5][8][15]; ascertainment signature [7][82]; register incidence [12][14] |
| 4 | Case series | 6 | FTLB phenomenology and burden [53][54][55][57] |
| 5 | Expert opinion / consensus / theory | 20 | NASEM measured conclusions [36]; APA AI advisories [75]; prevalence-inflation hypothesis [62]; mechanism taxonomy [33] |

**Evidence-weight assessment.** The claims about *magnitude* rest on Level 1a–2b evidence and are secure. The claims about *causation* rest on 2b plus abductive timing arguments, because Level 1 evidence for the primary exposure is unobtainable — no ethics committee will randomise childhood smartphone ownership. The claims about *what to do* rest disproportionately on Level 5, with the two Level 1b results both negative [73][41]. This is a field with strong descriptive evidence, contested causal evidence, and weak-to-negative interventional evidence — and public discourse has the ordering exactly reversed, treating the interventional prescriptions as the settled part.

| Period | Key development | Evidence | Quantified impact | Dominant paradigm |

**Narrative arc.** The intellectual history runs from *attribution* (2012–2016: something changed, phones changed, therefore phones) through *deflation* (2017–2020: the effect sizes are trivial and the measures are bad) to *stratification* (2021–2026: the average is trivial because the population is heterogeneous, and the signal lives in specific subgroups, use-types and developmental windows). What drove each transition was methodological rather than empirical: specification curve analysis drove the deflation, and time-use diaries plus prospective mediation analysis drove the stratification. The next transition will be driven by the Australian natural experiment and by whether the 2022–2024 reversal holds.

**Conflict analysis.** The sharpest clash is between regulators and researchers: regulators need a defensible action now, and the evidence supports only stratified, targeted intervention that does not scale into legislation. The second clash is between platforms and everyone else, and it is narrower than it appears — the identified lever is feature-level (algorithmic amplification of harmful content, appearance-focused recommendation) rather than access-level, which is technically addressable without prohibition. The most consequential misalignment is that **adolescents themselves have essentially no voice in a debate conducted entirely about them**, and the interventions being enacted (blanket prohibition) impose the highest costs on the most vulnerable subgroups within that population — LGBTQ+, rural, disabled and isolated young people for whom online community is not a supplement to social life but the whole of it [77].

### 5.4 Comparative Context — Digital Exposure Against Its Competitors

The digital hypothesis is rarely compared against the other exposures acting on the same cohorts over the same window. It should be.

| Exposure | Timing fit (2012 inflection) | Effect size available | Dose–response shown | Cross-national fit | Reversibility | Overall evidential standing |
|----------|------------------------------|----------------------|--------------------|--------------------|---------------|-----------------------------|
| **Social media / smartphones** | Excellent — diffusion matches inflection [31] | Dose trivial (0.4%) [18]; dyscontrol moderate (r = 0.27–0.35) [43] | Yes, for problematic use [42][43] | **Poor** — heterogeneous trends [78][79] | Untested; bans null so far [39] | Real contributor, magnitude contested, not established as dominant |
| **Schoolwork pressure** | Good — rising across HBSC window [10] | Independent national-level association [10][79] | Not established | **Good** — explains cross-national variance [79] | Policy-modifiable | Under-researched; comparable national-level fit |
| **Economic precarity** | Fair — 2008 crisis precedes inflection [78] | 47% of 16–24s struggling [84]; NEET ~20% globally [85]; 18–24 depression >12% vs 8% in 25–64 [—] | Not established | Fair | Slow | Dismissed as "too early," but lagged/cumulative effects unexamined [78] |
| **Pandemic period effect** | Poor for 2012, excellent for 2020–21 | Pooled depression 25.2%, anxiety 20.5% [6] | N/A | **Excellent** — universal | Partial reversal observed [3][4][5] | Strongest explanation for the 2020–2021 peak; cannot explain 2012–2019 |
| **Climate distress** | Fair — salience rose post-2018 | 59% very/extremely worried; >45% functional impact [11] | Correlated with perceived government inaction [11] | Good — 10 countries | Political | Real distress, advocacy-funded measurement, contributory not causal for the trend |
| **Ascertainment / awareness** | Excellent — screening and awareness expanded post-2010 | ADHD +24.7% above trend [7]; 5.5× autism site variation [82] | N/A | Good — tracks service systems | In principle | **Best explanation for the diagnostic-category increases specifically** |
| **Declining independent activity** | Good — long secular trend | Essentially unmeasured | No | Untested | Yes | Theoretically strong, empirically nearly absent — the field's biggest blind spot |

The comparison yields a conclusion that neither camp will like: **no single exposure fits all the data, and the exposure with the best timing fit (digital) has the worst cross-national fit, while the exposure with the best cross-national fit (schoolwork pressure) has no mechanistic account.** The pandemic explains the peak but not the trend; ascertainment explains the diagnostic categories but not the deaths; economic precarity explains the young-adult gradient but is timed wrong for the adolescent inflection. A multi-causal model with digital exposure as one weighted contributor among several — larger in girls, larger in the 11–13 window, larger for dyscontrolled and appearance-focused use, and interacting with pre-existing vulnerability — fits the totality better than any monocausal account in either direction.

## 6. Implications and Recommendations

### 6.1 Practical Implications for Clinical Practice

**Replace the screen-time question with a five-domain digital history.** The hours figure is close to uninformative (Finding 5), and asking for it signals to the adolescent that you are about to lecture them, which costs alliance for no diagnostic yield. What carries effect size: **(1) dyscontrol** — have you tried to cut down and failed? what happens when you can't check? does it cause conflict at home? [42][43][71]; **(2) night-time displacement** — where does the phone sleep? [69][81]; **(3) victimisation** — has anyone targeted, excluded, or shared anything about you? [70]; **(4) comparison content** — whose posts make you feel worse, and do you follow appearance/fitness/body accounts? [33][47]; **(5) function** — what does it give you that you don't get elsewhere? [67]. Domain 5 is the one that changes formulation: a self-determination framing asks which of relatedness, autonomy and competence the platform is meeting or frustrating [67], and the answer usually names the treatment target.

**Add two questions that did not exist five years ago.** Ask about **AI companion use** as routine history, per the APA advisory [75] — what tool, how often, what for, and specifically whether they have discussed distress or self-harm with it, since adolescents are less likely than adults to question a bot's accuracy or intent [75]. And ask about **self-diagnosis** non-judgementally: what have you concluded about yourself, where did that come from, and what does it explain? Take it as data about experience and need rather than as a claim to be adjudicated on the spot (Finding 15).

**Stratify by developmental window.** A girl aged 11–13 and either sex at 19 are in the periods where longitudinal data show forward effects [32]; boys at 14–15 likewise. Assessment intensity and any advice about use should be weighted accordingly rather than applied uniformly across a paediatric caseload.

**Recognise the new presentations.** Before diagnosing treatment-resistant Tourette syndrome, rule out functional tic-like behaviours: abrupt onset, complex from the start, coprophenomena-like vocalisations, atypical premonitory-urge description, timely-related psychological stressor, female predominance, high comorbid OCD and self-injury [53]. Do not treat FTLB as trivial — it produces more school absence, more depressive symptoms and higher suicidality risk than Tourette syndrome [55], and it warrants a functional-disorder treatment framework rather than antipsychotics.

**Counsel parents on relationship rather than rules.** The pooled evidence gives warmth r = −0.17 against media-specific rules at r ≈ 0.00, with restrictive mediation positively associated with problematic use in older adolescents [72]. The message is: keep devices out of bedrooms overnight, co-view with young children, stay curious about what is happening online, and do not expect rules alone to work after 14. This will disappoint parents who came for a screen-time number, and it is what the evidence supports.

**Be cautious about universal prevention.** If asked to endorse a school-wide wellbeing programme, the honest position is that the largest trial found clinically trivial differential benefit [73], that the primary trial found worse outcomes in the already-at-risk group [74], and that every documented negative effect in this literature occurred in universal rather than targeted programmes [74]. Targeted intervention for identified need has a far better evidence base than universal promotion.

| Child & adolescent psychiatrists | Assessment framework is obsolete; new presentations unrecognised | Adopt the five-domain digital history; screen for FTLB before "treatment-resistant TS"; ask about AI companions routinely | Findings 6, 8, 14, 19 [43][70][53][75] |
| General adult psychiatrists | Late-presenting ADHD in women is real *and* ascertainment-driven | Assess rigorously rather than dismissing; expect volume; document functional impairment across settings and childhood | Finding 12 [7] |

### 6.3 Future Outlook (2026–2031)

**Australia's ban will produce the field's first genuine causal evidence, and it will be contested regardless of result.** With 4.7 million accounts removed at commencement [77], the exposure contrast is real if circumvention stays modest. Expect first outcome data on self-harm presentations and survey indicators in 2027–2028. The base case is a small improvement confounded by secular trend, argued over indefinitely.

**Problematic-use measurement will displace screen-time measurement.** The effect-size gap is too large to ignore [43][69][71], and the mediation evidence gives it mechanistic priority. Expect a validated adolescent dyscontrol instrument to become standard in cohort studies, and expect the construct-validity fight that has dogged gaming disorder [80] to follow it.

**AI companion harm will be the dominant question by 2028.** Adoption is at 72% now [23a] with essentially no evidence base. Expect the first large cohort results, the first regulatory action, and — given the crisis-response failures already documented in litigation — the first clinical guidelines that treat companion use as a specific risk factor requiring enquiry. The mechanism to test is social-avoidance reinforcement, not social comparison.

**Generation Alpha's adolescence begins now and will not resemble Gen Z's.** The oldest are 15–16. Their distinguishing exposure is pre-verbal and parent-mediated [49][50][51], not pubertal and peer-mediated, and the outcomes to watch are language, executive function and attention rather than body image and social comparison. Nobody has the cohort to answer this, and building it should be an immediate research priority.

**The 2022–2024 improvement will either consolidate or reverse, and it will decide the field.** If US adolescent depression continues falling toward pre-2012 levels while digital exposure remains at ceiling, the strong version of the digital hypothesis becomes untenable and pandemic-plus-multicausal accounts win. If the improvement stalls or reverses, the harm thesis survives its most serious empirical challenge. This is a two-to-three-year question with a clean answer, which is rare in this field.

## 7. Limitations

**Source limitations.** This review is built on English-language literature indexed in PubMed plus openly accessible institutional publications. Three consequences follow. First, the causal debate is conducted almost entirely on US, UK, Nordic, Dutch and Australian data, and this review inherits that bias — the single Thai data point [17] and the Saudi language study [52] are illustrative rather than representative, and the Thai figures rest on screening instruments not comparable to Western diagnostic estimates. Second, PubMed's automatic query translation over-ANDed several searches and returned substantial off-target literature; queries were reformulated, but some relevant work in psychology and communication journals not indexed in MEDLINE (particularly in *Journal of Communication*, *New Media & Society* and the education literature) will have been missed. Third, no formal grey-literature search of preprint servers was completed; medRxiv and PsyArXiv material entered this review only where it was cited by included sources.

**Methodological constraints.** This is a narrative synthesis conducted by a single analyst without dual screening, without formal risk-of-bias instruments applied study-by-study, and without quantitative pooling. Study selection was purposive rather than exhaustive within each query. Two specific biases are likely present and are declared: a **prominence bias**, since the sources most visible to search are the ones that generated public controversy, and the quiet, well-conducted null studies are systematically less findable; and an **availability bias toward recent work**, mitigated by deliberate inclusion of seminal older sources [80][81] but not eliminated. Where sources carried funding or advocacy conflicts, these are flagged in text — most notably the AVAAZ funding of the climate-anxiety survey [11], the advocacy character of the Surgeon General's advisory [35], and the strong and publicly stated prior commitments of the principal authors on both sides of the specification dispute [18][26][38].

**Scope boundaries — what was deliberately excluded.** Educational attainment is treated only where it bears on mental health [9], not as an outcome in its own right. Physical-health consequences of screen use (obesity, myopia, musculoskeletal) are excluded. Pornography exposure, sextortion, radicalisation and online sexual exploitation are excluded despite clear psychiatric relevance — each warrants its own review. Pharmacological treatment of adolescent depression and anxiety is not covered. Workplace and consumer psychology of Gen Z is excluded as outside the psychiatric remit. Gender-identity treatment evidence is summarised only to the extent of the Cass Review's own conclusions [63]; this report does not adjudicate that dispute and explicitly declines to.

**Temporal constraints.** Several findings will date rapidly and predictably. The 2022–2024 reversal in US indicators [3][4][5] has one to two more data cycles before it is either confirmed as a trend or dismissed as fluctuation. The Australian ban [77] will generate outcome data from 2027 that could materially change §6. The AI-companion evidence base is empty *now* and will not be in two years [23a]. Any reader consulting this after mid-2027 should treat Findings 19 and 20 as the most likely to have been superseded, and Finding 2's trajectory claim as requiring a fresh data check.

## 8. Conclusions

**Held with high confidence.** Something real changed in the psychiatric epidemiology of high-income countries from approximately 2012, it is concentrated in adolescent girls, and it is visible on endpoints that self-report inflation cannot reach — a 62% rise in US youth suicide between 2007 and 2021 [5], rising register-diagnosed eating disorders in Denmark [12], and rising diagnosed morbidity in US Medicaid claims entirely before the pandemic [14]. It is equally certain that this change did not consist of everything getting worse: the same cohorts produced the lowest adolescent substance use ever recorded [15], and every major US indicator improved between 2021 and 2024 [3][4][5]. The digital environment is a real contributor whose magnitude depends entirely on how it is measured: trivial as hours (≤0.4% of variance [18]), clinically substantial as dyscontrol (r = 0.27–0.35 [43]; d = 0.53 [71]), and structured by developmental window (girls 11–13, boys 14–15, both at 19 [32]). A material fraction of the apparent increase in diagnosed neurodevelopmental disorder is ascertainment rather than incidence, and the ADHD and autism data carry that signature unmistakably — a post-2020 excess concentrated in adult women from less deprived areas [7], and a 5.5-fold variation in autism identification between two surveillance sites in one country [82].

**Held with moderate confidence, and stated as such.** The pathway from digital exposure to psychiatric outcome runs principally through problematic dyscontrolled use and through content and experience — not through hours and not, primarily, through sleep, which carries only 9% of the mediated effect against 48–58% for problematic use [69]. Universal school-based mental-health promotion is at best inert and at worst harmful to the pupils it most intends to help [73][74], and the prevalence-inflation mechanism that predicts this is now supported by experimental evidence of media-driven shifts in illness certainty [60]. Functional tic-like behaviours constitute a genuinely new clinical entity that is more disabling than Tourette syndrome on several axes [55] while remaining diagnostically unsettled from inside its own field [56]. Early-childhood screen exposure functions substantially as a marker of constrained caregiving capacity rather than purely as an independent toxin [50].

**The single most important takeaway.** The disagreement in this field is not primarily about facts — it is about *aggregation*. Every claim that fails is an average over heterogeneous people, exposures and outcomes; every claim that survives specifies whom, what kind of use, at what age, and on what endpoint. For a clinician, this converts an unresolvable public argument into a tractable clinical one. You will not settle whether smartphones caused a generational crisis, and you do not need to. You can ask a thirteen-year-old girl whether she has tried to stop and failed, where her phone sleeps, whether anyone is targeting her, whose posts make her feel worse, and what the app gives her that nothing else does — and every one of those questions has an effect size behind it large enough to act on. The generational frame is a marketing convention that answers nothing [1]; the developmental, sex-specific, dyscontrol-focused frame answers a great deal.

## Appendix B: Clinical Quick-Reference

**The five-domain digital history** (replaces "how many hours?")
1. **Dyscontrol** — tried to cut down and failed? distress when unable to check? conflict at home about it? *(r = 0.27–0.35 with depression/anxiety [43])*
2. **Night displacement** — where does the phone sleep? *(9% of mediated effect, but sleep is an independent risk factor [69][81])*
3. **Victimisation** — has anyone targeted, excluded, or shared something about you? *(+0.41 symptom score, independent of total screen time [70])*
4. **Comparison content** — whose posts make you feel worse? do you follow appearance/fitness accounts? *(mechanism: social comparison, self-concept [33][47])*
5. **Function** — what does it give you that you don't get elsewhere? *(self-determination framing: relatedness / autonomy / competence [67])*

**Two new routine questions**
- AI companion use: which tool, how often, what for, and have you discussed distress or self-harm with it? *(72% adoption; adolescents question bots less than humans [23a][75])*
- Self-diagnosis: what have you concluded about yourself, where did it come from, what does it explain? *(take as data about need, assess independently [59][60])*

**Higher-risk developmental windows** — girls 11–13 · boys 14–15 · both at 19 *[32]*

**Red flags for functional tic-like behaviours** *(before diagnosing treatment-resistant TS [53])*
Abrupt onset · complex from the start · coprophenomena-like vocalisations · atypical premonitory-urge description · timely-related psychological stressor · female · comorbid OCD/self-injury · often gender-diverse. **More disabling than TS on absence, depression and suicidality** *[55]*.

**What to tell parents** — warmth (r = −0.17) beats rules (r ≈ 0.00); restrictive mediation is *positively* associated with problematic use in older adolescents; devices out of bedrooms overnight; co-view with under-5s; ask what is happening rather than counting hours *[72]*.

**What not to endorse** — school phone bans as a mental-health intervention (null [39]); universal school wellbeing curricula (d = 0.07, harm in at-risk pupils [73][74]).
