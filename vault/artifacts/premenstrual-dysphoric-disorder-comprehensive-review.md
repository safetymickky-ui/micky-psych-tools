## Definition & nosology

The construct now called premenstrual dysphoric disorder has changed institutional homes four times in thirty-five years, and each move changed what counted as a case far more than it changed what patients reported. "Premenstrual tension" was named in the 1930s and reframed as premenstrual syndrome in the 1950s; the American Psychiatric Association first formalised a severe psychiatric variant as late luteal phase dysphoric disorder in an appendix to DSM-III-R in 1987, renamed it premenstrual dysphoric disorder and moved it to DSM-IV Appendix B — "criterion sets provided for further study" — in 1994, and promoted it to a free-standing category inside the depressive disorders chapter of DSM-5 in 2013. The work group's published rationale is candid about its logic: the disorder affects 2–5% of premenopausal women, the DSM-IV appendix criteria had already generated a large epidemiological and treatment literature, and full categorical status would confer legitimacy and encourage further evidence-based work. That is an argument from accumulated research infrastructure and from patient legitimacy, not from a new validator, and it should be named as such. It is also worth recording what the elevation did not rest on: PMDD was not among the diagnoses for which the DSM-5 field trials produced usable inter-rater reliability estimates. Those trials enrolled 2,246 patients across 11 sites with 279 clinicians and more than 86% of patients interviewed twice; 23 diagnoses reached adequate sample size (15 adult, 8 child and adolescent), of which five achieved intraclass kappas of 0.60–0.79, nine 0.40–0.59, six 0.20–0.39 and three below 0.20. PMDD is in none of those bands. It was promoted to a full category without a field-trial reliability figure of its own, and no multi-rater study has supplied one since.

The DSM-5-TR criteria are a seven-part structure, and the parts do very unequal amounts of work. Criterion A requires that, in the majority of menstrual cycles, at least five symptoms be present in the final week before menses, begin to improve within a few days after onset, and become minimal or absent in the week after menses. Criterion B requires at least one of four core affective symptoms: marked affective lability; marked irritability, anger or increased interpersonal conflict; marked depressed mood, hopelessness or self-deprecating thoughts; marked anxiety or tension. Criterion C supplies seven further symptoms — anhedonia, concentration difficulty, lethargy, appetite change or food cravings, hypersomnia or insomnia, feeling overwhelmed or out of control, and physical symptoms such as breast tenderness, joint or muscle pain, bloating or weight gain — which combine with B to reach five of eleven. Criterion D requires clinically significant distress or interference with work, school, social activities or relationships. Criterion E is the exclusion that decides the most cases: the disturbance must not be merely an exacerbation of another disorder, though it may co-occur with one. Criterion F requires confirmation of Criterion A by prospective daily ratings over at least two symptomatic cycles, with provisional diagnosis permitted beforehand. Criterion G excludes substance and medication effects.

ICD-11 places the same entity at GA34.41, under diseases of the genitourinary system, and cross-lists it among depressive disorders because of the prominence of mood symptomatology; the code sits within the GA34 group alongside dysmenorrhoea and premenstrual tension syndrome. The divergence from DSM is structural rather than doctrinal. ICD is a morbidity and mortality statistical classification organised organ-system-first and used by member states for reporting; PMS already lived in ICD-10's genitourinary chapter, most patients worldwide present to gynaecology, and coding continuity favoured keeping the severe variant beside its predecessor. DSM is a psychiatric nomenclature with no genitourinary chapter, so a full PMDD diagnosis had nowhere else to go. ICD-11's dual parentage is the WHO honestly declining to settle the underlying question — whether PMDD is a reproductive-endocrine hypersensitivity condition whose output happens to be affective, or an affective disorder whose trigger happens to be ovarian. The consequences are practical rather than philosophical: no single specialty owns the disorder, training and reimbursement follow the code, and international practice diverges accordingly. Office-practice data from five Western nations found that few physicians in any country diagnose PMS or PMDD at all, that almost every diagnosed woman is prescribed something regardless of which specialist she sees, and that North American and UK practice favours SSRIs while French practice favours hormones and analgesics and German practice favours *Vitex agnus-castus* — the same nominal entity treated as three different diseases.

The family around PMDD is where the boundaries are genuinely contested, and the contest is quantitative. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists defines PMS by at least one affective symptom and one somatic symptom in the five days before menses in each of three prior cycles, relieved within four days of onset, not recurring until at least cycle day 13, with demonstrable functional impairment. ICD-10's PMS definition required no impairment at all and could be met with one or two symptoms of undefined severity. The International Society for Premenstrual Disorders built, at its inaugural Montreal meeting in 2008, the taxonomy most clinicians now use, and it was endorsed by 100% of the group. Core PMD — comprising PMS and PMDD — requires symptoms in all or part of the two-week premenstrual phase, resolving during or shortly after menstruation, with a clear symptom-free interval between the end of menses and approximately ovulation, recurring in most cycles (typically two of every three), causing impairment or distress, and explicitly not being an exacerbation of another disorder. Variant PMD comprises four separate things: premenstrual exacerbation, symptoms arising from non-ovulatory ovarian activity, progestogen-induced symptoms, and premenstrual disorder without menstruation after hysterectomy, ablation or with a levonorgestrel intrauterine system. Premenstrual exacerbation is defined as luteal magnification of an underlying somatic, medical or psychiatric disorder, with the same symptom profile across the cycle but significantly greater premenstrual intensity, and it is deliberately excluded from Core PMD. Independent comorbidity — a condition unaffected by the cycle — is a third thing again. The society's fourth consensus, assembled from 66 candidate standards submitted by 18 of 19 invited experts, ranked a two-month prospective diary among its highest-priority auditable standards.

What makes these boundaries load-bearing rather than academic is that the definition, not the biology, sets the prevalence. Applying three criteria sets to one sample of 402 women produced ICD-10 PMS in 79.9% (95% CI 75.6–83.7), ACOG PMS in 12.7% (9.6–16.3) and DSM-IV PMDD in 5.5% (3.5–8.2) — a fourteenfold range generated by definition alone. Ascertainment method then halves the remainder: pooling 44 studies contributing 48 independent samples and 50,659 participants gives a point prevalence of 3.2% (95% CI 1.7–5.9) where diagnosis was confirmed by prospective daily monitoring versus 7.7% (5.3–11.0) where it was provisional, with heterogeneity at I² = 99%. Restricting to community samples with confirmed diagnosis yields 1.6% (1.0–2.5) and heterogeneity collapses to I² = 26%. Among 200 women actively recruited for retrospectively reported premenstrual emotional symptoms and then rated prospectively, only 38 (19%) met prospective DSM-5 PMDD, 46 (23%) met non-PMDD menstrually related mood disorder and 116 (58%) had no cyclical diagnosis at all; retrospective report predicted prospective PMDD with an area under the curve of just 0.70 (95% CI 0.62–0.78).

Three criterion thresholds are contested on evidence rather than on opinion. The five-symptom count is arbitrary, and an estimated 13–18% of reproductive-age women carry premenstrual dysphoric symptoms severe enough to impair without reaching it; two independent cohorts, one community and one treatment-seeking, and a third analysis of 267 women contributing 563 cycles, all identify four rather than five as the count that best predicts impairment. The impairment criterion is where the diagnosis is actually cut: in a community cohort of 1,488 women aged 14–24, twelve-month DSM-IV PMDD prevalence was 5.8% while a further 18.6% sat near-threshold, mostly by failing impairment alone. The full-remission requirement is the most consequential of the three. Group-based trajectory modelling of 74 prospectively diagnosed cases identified three temporal groups — moderate symptoms confined to the premenstrual week (65%), severe symptoms across the full two-week luteal phase (17.5%), and severe premenstrual symptoms resolving slowly into the follicular phase (17.5%) — so luteal confinement is a graded property, and a fixed clearance window will misclassify roughly a sixth of true cases. Criterion E's exclusion of premenstrual exacerbation is now the sharpest fault line of all: around 60% of women with mood disorders report premenstrual worsening and are thereby placed in diagnostic limbo, while roughly a third of prospectively confirmed PMDD patients fail to remit under GnRH-agonist ovarian suppression, which weakens the premise that PMDD and premenstrual exacerbation are cleanly separable hormone-sensitive and hormone-insensitive populations. Pooled comorbidity of PMDD or PMS with mood disorders runs 42% (95% CI 30–55) to 49% (38–60) across sampling strategies in 36 pooled studies of 3,646 participants; across five studies, PMDD has been reported in 27–76% of women with bipolar disorder and bipolar disorder in 10–15% of women with PMDD, a 49-point range that is itself the finding, since none of those studies used the prospective monitoring that alone could separate PMDD from premenstrual exacerbation of bipolar disorder.

## Epidemiology

The single most consequential fact in PMDD epidemiology is that the instrument determines the answer, and the field now has a quantitative statement of how much. The definitive meta-analysis — 44 studies, 48 samples, 50,659 participants — gives 3.2% (95% CI 1.7–5.9) for prospectively confirmed diagnosis against 7.7% (5.3–11.0) for provisional, a 2.4-fold inflation, and 1.6% (1.0–2.5) once sampling is restricted to community settings as well. The figure a clinician should carry is 1.6%. But the meta-regression is more interesting than the headline, and it does not say what it is usually reported as saying: continent of sample (p < 0.0001), sample type — community, university or high school (p = 0.007) and risk of bias (p = 0.009) were all *stronger* moderators of heterogeneity than method of diagnosis (p = 0.017). Ascertainment is a large effect sitting inside larger ones, and any two prevalence figures that differ in setting as well as instrument cannot be attributed to the instrument alone.

The best single primary study underwrites the low end. A US household probability sample enrolled 1,246 menstruating women aged 13–55 across two urban and two rural sites; each completed daily symptom questionnaires and provided daily urine specimens across two consecutive ovulatory cycles, with all four DSM-IV research criteria applied. Prevalence: 1.3%. By contrast the largest purely interview-based study, a Dresden cohort of 1,488 women aged 14–24 assessed with a structured diagnostic interview and explicitly without daily ratings, found a twelve-month prevalence of 5.8%, falling to 5.3% after DSM-IV exclusions for concurrent major depression and dysthymia. An intermediate design shows that "prospective" is not one thing: 4,164 women aged 36–44 were retrospectively screened, 976 interviewed, and 513 charted prospectively over a *single* cycle, yielding 6.4% (33/513) — and 2.7% (14/513) once women with a prior depression history were removed. One pre-screened cycle without ovulation confirmation behaves much more like a retrospective estimate than like the 1.3% figure.

PMS, the broader construct, is roughly thirty times more common and is measured almost entirely retrospectively. A 17-study meta-analysis pooled PMS at 47.8% (95% CI 32.6–62.9), with country estimates spanning France at 12% (11–13) to Iran at 98% (97–100) — a spread that is a statement about instruments rather than about biology. The Global Burden of Disease 2019 estimate put 956.0 million prevalent PMS cases worldwide, up 46.5% from 652.5 million in 1990, with an age-standardised prevalence essentially flat at 24,406.51 per 100,000 (average annual percentage change 0, 95% CI −0.01 to 0.01) and 8.0 million years lived with disability. GBD 2021 makes PMS the most prevalent of seven non-neoplastic gynaecological conditions at 24.7%.

Age structure is genuinely unsettled, and the two most recent GBD cycles contradict each other — 2019 placing peak PMS burden at 40–44 years, 2023 concentrating it at 15–30. Neither uses a prospective diagnosis. The largest cross-national symptom dataset, 238,114 menstrual-tracking app users across 140 countries aged 18–55, found premenstrual *mood* symptoms (mood swings and anxiety, 64.18%) did not differ across the 18–27, 28–37, 38–47 and 48–55 bands, while somatic symptoms — sleep change, gastrointestinal symptoms, weight gain, headache, vasomotor symptoms, fatigue — rose significantly with age (all p < 0.001); 28.61% reported interference with daily life in every cycle. A treatment-seeking Japanese series of 199 women aged 20–49 shows the same pattern in reverse, with women in their forties reporting less appetite change (adjusted OR 0.10), overeating (0.19) and food craving (0.09) than women in their twenties, and dysmenorrhoea reported by 89.9% of the twenties subgroup. Consistent with an age effect on premenstrual *worsening* rather than on core PMDD, in a 900-woman community sample rating symptoms daily across two cycles, age was the only predictor of premenstrual exacerbation, with older women more often affected.

Incidence and persistence rest almost entirely on one cohort, and the numbers are not reassuring. Over roughly 42 months the incidence of threshold PMDD was 3.0%, cumulative lifetime incidence by the late twenties 7.4%, and fewer than 10% of baseline cases achieved complete remission over 48 months. The dominant predictor of incident PMDD was subthreshold PMDD at baseline (OR 11.0, 95% CI 4.7–25.9), followed by traumatic events (OR 4.2, 1.2–12.0), prior anxiety disorder (OR 2.5, 1.1–5.5) and elevated daily hassles (OR 1.6, 1.1–2.3). PMDD is therefore best characterised as an early-onset, highly persistent condition of the reproductive years rather than a relapsing-remitting one — and, notably, no population study anywhere reports a mean or median age at onset with a confidence interval.

Individual-level socioeconomic gradients are thin and ecological ones point in opposite directions. The strongest individual data show lower education (OR 2.3, 95% CI 1.1–4.9) and current smoking (OR 4.1, 1.5–11.1) associated with prospectively diagnosed PMDD, and women not working outside the home significantly less likely to meet criteria (OR 0.2, 0.1–0.9). Ecologically, GBD 2019 put the highest age-standardised PMS burden in low-middle socio-demographic index regions and the lowest in high-SDI, whereas GBD 2023 reports prevalence positively correlated with SDI (r = 0.351, p < 0.0001). The direction of the gradient is unresolved and probably reflects ascertainment intensity.

Cross-cultural data are abundant for PMS and effectively absent for prospectively confirmed PMDD — there is no two-cycle daily-rating prevalence estimate from any general population outside the United States and Western Europe. East Asian screening figures run consistently lower: among 1,187 Japanese women aged 20–49, 95% reported some premenstrual symptom but moderate-to-severe PMS was 5.3% and PMDD 1.2%, with only 5.3% of affected women treated; 1,022 Japanese high-school students gave PMS 9.7% and PMDD 2.2%; a nationwide Korean interview survey of 2,499 women aged 18–64 gave a twelve-month PMDD prevalence of 2.4%. South Asian, Middle Eastern and African screening figures are dramatically higher: 25 Indian studies of 8,542 participants gave PMS 43% (35–50) and PMDD 8% (6–10); 24 Iranian studies of 9,147 women gave PMS 70.8% (63.8–77.7); 18 Turkish studies of 6,890 women gave 52.2%; 16 African studies of 6,530 participants gave 46.98% (28.9–65.06). Medical students are a category of their own, pooling at 17.7% for PMDD (95% CI 10.2–28.9) across 7 studies of 1,487 students with I² = 95.12%. The upper bound is Ethiopia, where 12 studies pooled screening-defined "PMDD" among female students at 54.5% (40.8–67.6) — roughly 34 times the community prospectively confirmed estimate, though the gap spans continent, sample type and risk-of-bias stratum as well as instrument, and cannot be read as a pure instrument effect. Southeast Asian data exist only as a pooled medical-student estimate of 39.60% (20.13–63.05) across 17 studies and 7,080 participants from Vietnam, Thailand, Indonesia and Malaysia; there is no dedicated Thai PMDD prevalence study, Thailand appearing only as one national cell of roughly 400 women inside a 7,226-woman, 13-country survey in which a duration-by-severity symptom score — not a prevalence proportion — was associated with age, parity, current smoking and country.

Burden is best documented as lost productivity rather than as healthcare cost. In an HMO cohort of 1,194 women with two cycles of prospective ratings, the 56 (4.7%) meeting PMDD criteria showed greater luteal productivity impairment and continued lower productivity 5–10 days *after* menses onset (p < 0.01), yet healthcare expenditures did not differ across severity groups at all. In 822 women completing two months of daily ratings, moderate-to-severe PMS or PMDD carried an adjusted OR of 3.12 (95% CI 1.75–5.57) for work-productivity impairment, a mean 5.6 versus 1.1 cycle-days with at least moderate productivity reduction, and absenteeism exceeding eight hours per cycle in 14.2% versus 6.0%. Prospective daily-rating data give 2.5 versus 1.3 absence days per month (p = 0.006) and 7.2 versus 4.2 monthly workdays at half or less of typical productivity (p < 0.0001). In a Swedish population survey of 17,284 women, the 10.5% meeting premenstrual disorder criteria showed a quality-of-life decrement of 0.21 standard deviations (0.17–0.26) cross-sectionally and 0.34 (0.24–0.45) prospectively. The single available utility estimate is a retrospective series of 49 previously untreated patients gaining 0.190 EQ-5D points per cycle, about 0.2 quality-adjusted life years, at ¥823,000 per QALY.

Two ascertainment gaps close the section. First, treated prevalence is far below symptomatic prevalence: in Swedish national registers only 13,382 of 1,052,977 women with a recorded birth (1.3%) carried a clinically recorded premenstrual disorder diagnosis or a premenstrual-indicated prescription at any point before pregnancy — a lifetime-before-pregnancy treated prevalence among parous women, and 8.1-fold below the 10.5% survey figure from the same country, though the two denominators and time-frames are not the same object. Second, premenstrual exacerbation is the neglected third entity and is much commoner than PMDD, yet has no prospectively confirmed population estimate at all. A secondary analysis of a large depression trial found 66% (545/821) of premenopausal women with major depression not using hormonal contraception self-reporting premenstrual exacerbation, with shorter time to relapse (41.3 versus 47.1 weeks); the only community daily-rating study reports a symptom count — a mean 1.34 exacerbated symptoms (95% CI 1.02–1.66) among depressed women — rather than a prevalence, and notes that 44% of non-depressed women taking antidepressants still had premenstrual symptoms.

## Etiology & pathophysiology

The central claim of modern PMDD neurobiology is not that affected women make abnormal amounts of hormone, but that they respond abnormally to normal amounts. The experiment that established this randomised 20 women with prospectively confirmed premenstrual syndrome to the GnRH agonist leuprolide or placebo; the 10 who remitted on leuprolide then received four weeks each of estradiol and of progesterone under double-blind crossover while ovarian suppression continued. Symptoms recurred on either steroid, whereas 15 women without premenstrual syndrome run through the identical protocol showed no mood change, and 5 patients given placebo hormone during continued leuprolide stayed well. The design isolates the variable: identical exogenous hormone, opposite behavioural consequence. That result has now been replicated in a substantially larger sample — 34 women with PMDD and 76 healthy comparison participants — with significant diagnosis-by-hormone interactions for anxiety, sadness, irritability and mood swings during both estradiol and progesterone add-back, and with irritability and mood swings tied more closely to progesterone than to estradiol. Bloating and food cravings were elevated in PMDD irrespective of hormone condition, and breast pain tracked estradiol specifically. Ovarian suppression abolishes cyclicity; re-exposure reinstates it only in the susceptible. Everything downstream in this section is an attempt to locate where in the transduction chain that susceptibility sits.

If exposure is normal, the pathology must lie in what the brain does with it. The leading candidate is allopregnanolone, the 3α,5α-reduced metabolite of progesterone, formed in two enzymatic steps — 5α-reductase to 5α-dihydroprogesterone, then 3α-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase — and acting as a potent positive allosteric modulator of the GABA-A receptor at a site distinct from the GABA, benzodiazepine, barbiturate and picrotoxin sites. It potentiates synaptic receptors and directly activates δ-subunit-containing extrasynaptic receptors that carry tonic inhibitory current. Direct measurement, however, repeatedly fails to separate patients from controls. Serum steroid metabolomics in 15 PMDD and 15 control women under GnRH-agonist suppression with randomised add-back measured 49 metabolites: allopregnanolone rose after progesterone with identical statistical strength in both groups (q = 0.001 in each), and no progesterone-derived neurosteroid differed by diagnosis. Only sulfated metabolites separated the groups — an attenuated estradiol-3-sulfate rise in PMDD (q = 0.035) and a fall rather than a rise in dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (q = 0.07) — pointing at sulfotransferase rather than at allopregnanolone synthesis. This is a null in 15 women per group at a permissive false-discovery threshold of q < 0.2 after 21 of 49 analytes were discarded for detectability, so it says the groups did not differ detectably, not that they are identical. An eight-timepoint mass-spectrometry trajectory study realigned to biological cycle phase found only progesterone differing (p ≤ 0.001), with a left-shifted luteal curve producing higher periovulatory levels and a sharper late-luteal withdrawal, and allopregnanolone itself indistinguishable. Older work is contradictory rather than confirmatory: 24 patients versus 12 controls showed *higher* luteal allopregnanolone with lower cortisol and a higher allopregnanolone-to-progesterone ratio, with stress-induced allopregnanolone rises in only 42% of patients versus 83% of controls, while within the patient group greater anxiety and irritability tracked *lower* luteal allopregnanolone. The honest summary is that levels are broadly normal, kinetics may not be, and the ratio and stress-responsivity literature is small and inconsistent. No meta-analysis of allopregnanolone in PMDD versus controls exists, despite this being the most-repeated claim in the field.

Sensitivity, unlike exposure, is measurably abnormal — and the abnormality is not a simple loss of it. Saccadic eye velocity, a GABA-A-dependent oculomotor readout, is reduced by intravenous pregnanolone dose-dependently in controls in both cycle phases but only in the follicular phase in patients (12 versus 12 women), and controls potentiate their response in the luteal phase while patients do not. Diazepam produced a similarly blunted response in 7 patients versus 7 controls. With intravenous allopregnanolone at 0.05 mg/kg in 10 patients and 10 controls, the group-by-phase interaction was highly significant (F(1,327.489) = 12.747, p < 0.001), patients showing a smaller response in the follicular phase (F(1,168) = 7.776, p = 0.006) and controls the opposite pattern (F(1,158.45) = 5.70, p = 0.018). What is lost is the normal cycle-dependent *retuning*, not sensitivity as such. Not every probe agrees: acute intravenous allopregnanolone raising serum concentrations to 50–70 nmol/L altered neither startle magnitude nor prepulse inhibition in 16 patients or 12 controls, and anxiety-potentiated startle did not separate 36 patients from 41 controls across phase, though it fell in the treated luteal phase among sertraline responders specifically.

The receptor-plasticity account that would explain this is almost entirely rodent, and the chapter should be blunt about that. Three weeks of progesterone followed by withdrawal upregulates GABA-A α4 transcription, shortens the GABA current decay time roughly sixfold, produces benzodiazepine insensitivity and seizure susceptibility, and is abolished by α4 antisense. Two to three days of progesterone raises α4 protein two- to threefold with near-total benzodiazepine insensitivity and reduced open-arm entries, all recovering by day 6; 48 hours of pregnanolone raises δ four- to fivefold; finasteride-induced allopregnanolone withdrawal raises α4 eightfold. Across the mouse estrous cycle, δ-containing receptors rise in late dioestrus, increasing tonic inhibition and lowering both seizure susceptibility and anxiety, with antisense or *Gabrd* knockout abolishing the protection. In pregnancy the same system downregulates δ and γ2 and rebounds at parturition, and *Gabrd* heterozygous and null dams show depression-like and abnormal maternal behaviour rescued by a δ-selective agonist. PMDD is therefore modelled as a failure of this reconfiguration — the receptor cannot re-tune its subunit composition to a changing neurosteroid signal — and the tolerance-and-withdrawal framing (direct action, tolerance induction, rebound at withdrawal) is benzodiazepine logic applied to an endogenous ligand.

Human corroboration for that model is a single study, and its limits matter. Whole-blood GABA-A subunit messenger RNA was measured longitudinally in 112 participants across 507 luteinising-hormone-timed visits: *GABRG2* fell in the midluteal phase (b = −0.009, p = 0.05, uncorrected), and both extremes of perimenstrual change predicted cyclical symptoms — irritability b = 0.11 (95% CI 0.03–0.18, p = 0.008), anxiety b = −0.10 (−0.19 to −0.01, p = 0.034), suicidal ideation b = −0.10 (−0.19 to −0.01, p = 0.044) — with stability, not direction, being protective. This is the first human longitudinal test of *peripheral* GABA-A subunit expression across the cycle, and its authors state explicitly that whether it reflects central receptor dynamics is unknown; intraclass correlations ran 0.15–0.30, meaning most variance is within-visit noise, and the headline coefficient is borderline on a tiny effect. It is directionally consistent with the preclinical finding, not a replication of it. A companion line of work finds δ-subunit messenger RNA in circulating monocytes tracking amygdala activation. The core plasticity hypothesis cannot currently be tested in the human brain at all, because no positron-emission-tomography ligand distinguishes δ- or α4-containing receptors. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy, the nearest available proxy, disagrees with itself: an occipital study of 14 patients and 9 controls found cortical GABA rising from follicular to luteal phase in PMDD with reduced follicular levels, while a 3-tesla study of 22 and 22 found GABA+ *lower* in anterior cingulate, medial prefrontal cortex and basal ganglia in the late luteal phase. No adequately powered modern edited-spectroscopy study across the full cycle exists.

The dose–response is held to be non-monotonic, and this is the single most repeated mechanistic claim in the field with the least direct human evidence. Positive GABA-A modulators produce paradoxical anxiogenesis in roughly 3–8% of exposed individuals with moderate symptoms in up to 25%, numerically mirroring PMDD at 3–8% and PMS at up to 25%; negative mood is reported to peak at allopregnanolone concentrations equal to endogenous luteal levels and to fall away at both lower and higher concentrations. No adequately powered human study has ever fitted a quadratic dose–response of allopregnanolone against mood with a confidence interval on the turning point, and the prevalence coincidence is suggestive rather than evidential. Two therapeutic probes nonetheless test the mechanism directly and in opposite directions. Dutasteride at 2.5 mg/day, blocking 5α-reduction so that progesterone never becomes allopregnanolone, significantly reduced irritability, sadness, anxiety, food cravings and bloating versus placebo in prospectively confirmed PMDD, with no effect at 0.5 mg/day — a dose that failed to block the luteal allopregnanolone rise at all — and no effect in controls. That is the cleanest demonstration that the luteal allopregnanolone rise is causal rather than correlated, and it rests on eight treated patients, with no effect size or confidence interval ever published. Isoallopregnanolone, a GABA-A modulating steroid antagonist active at both α1β2γ2L and α4β3δ receptors, reduced total daily symptom scores by 75% versus 47% on placebo (effect size 0.7, p = 0.006) in a post-hoc subgroup of 60 women drawn from a 126-patient phase II trial, but a subsequent 206-patient, 12-centre trial missed its prespecified primary endpoint outright.

Serotonergic and neurotrophic evidence is real but secondary, and it is old. Allopregnanolone potentiates GABA-A-mediated inhibition of dorsal raphe serotonin neurons in vitro while progesterone does not. The strongest molecular imaging result is a longitudinal study of 118 scans in 30 patients and 29 controls showing midbrain serotonin transporter binding potential rising 18% from periovulatory 1.64 (SD 0.40) to premenstrual 1.93 (0.40) in PMDD (t = −3.43, p = 0.0002) while controls fell 10% (1.65 to 1.49, t = −2.73, p = 0.01), with the increase correlating with depressive severity (R = 0.41, p < 0.0015). Older work found serotonin-precursor trapping change correlating inversely with worsening irritability (rs = −0.83) and depressed mood (rs = −0.81) in eight women, and raphe 5-HT1A binding shifting across the cycle in controls but significantly less in patients; neither has been revisited with modern methods in two decades. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor evidence is thinner still: ovariectomised *BDNF* Val66Met heterozygous mice given estradiol develop anxiety- and depression-like behaviour absent in wild-types, with hippocampal transcriptomic overlap with PMDD-derived cell lines; in humans, Met carriers with PMDD showed lower luteal fronto-cingulate activation in a study of 16 versus 15, and one small study found higher luteal serum concentrations.

Familial aggregation is the least contested part of the genetics and the most misquoted. Five twin studies exist; none used prospective criteria, and all measured retrospectively reported premenstrual symptoms. In 827 female same-sex pairs, heritability of premenstrual symptoms was 35.1% and of menstrual symptoms 39.2%, with the genetic factors for premenstrual, menstrual and neurotic symptoms largely distinct. Correcting for measurement unreliability raises it: in 1,312 twins assessed twice over six years, heritability of the *stable* component was 56%, with no shared-family-environment effect and only modest overlap with lifetime major depression. An Australian sample of 720 pairs gave additive genetic influence of 44%, with genetic correlations of 0.62 with neuroticism and 0.70 with lifetime major depression — leaving 39% of the genetic variance explained by neither. An earlier study of 157 monozygotic and 143 dizygotic pairs found correlations of 0.55 versus 0.28, the classic additive pattern. A much-quoted outlier reporting a 95% additive model rests on 176 twins with no confidence interval and should be treated as uninterpretable. None of these papers report retrievable confidence intervals, and the defensible statement is a band of roughly 30–56% for premenstrual symptomatology, partially shared with neuroticism and depression liability, with the heritability of PMDD as diagnosed simply unknown.

Molecular genetics is where replication fails hardest, and the field's most important negative fact is structural: there is no genome-wide association study of PMDD at all. The single positive candidate-gene finding of note came from 91 women with prospectively confirmed PMDD versus 56 controls, in which four single-nucleotide polymorphisms in intron 4 of the oestrogen receptor α gene differed between groups, with the signal appearing only within *COMT* Val/Val carriers and no association for oestrogen receptor β or *COMT* Val158Met itself; it has never been independently replicated in an equivalent design. A Taiwanese study of 100 prospectively diagnosed cases and 96 controls found the inverse relationship between premenstrual oestrogen and anxiety confined to G carriers of an oestrogen receptor α polymorphism — a moderation result rather than a case-control association. Serotonin-system candidates are essentially negative: neither the serotonin-transporter-linked polymorphic region nor *BDNF* Val66Met was associated with PMDD in 31 cases versus 31 controls, and 53 versus 52 found no association for a related variant alone or in haplotype. The one modern genomic result is indirect: in 1,528 Chinese female college students, a depression polygenic risk score interacted with psychosocial exposures, high risk score plus two or more adverse factors giving β = 0.59 (95% CI 0.37–0.81) for symptom severity and OR = 1.79 (1.05–3.11) for probable premenstrual disorder.

The most mechanistically interesting molecular work bypasses genotype entirely. Lymphoblastoid cell lines from women with PMDD who had demonstrated steroid-triggered behavioural sensitivity in a suppression-and-add-back trial showed, on whole-transcriptome sequencing under steroid-free conditions, over-expression of genes of the ESC/E(Z) complex — an ovarian-steroid-regulated gene-silencing complex — with more than half of its 13 genes over-expressed and three significant individually; protein expression of those same three was *decreased*, an unexplained transcript–protein dissociation. Progesterone increased complex messenger RNA in control lines only, while estradiol decreased it in patient lines. This is a genuinely intrinsic cellular difference, present without hormone exposure — and it is one laboratory, in peripheral tissue, never replicated externally, never demonstrated in brain, and never tested against the GABA-A plasticity account it would have to be reconciled with.

Neuroimaging tells a convergent network story with contradictory regional details. Systematic review of 18 publications and 361 participants, updated by a 2026 synthesis of 15 functional studies covering 294 patients and 304 controls, finds altered fronto-limbic reactivity as the recurring theme. Morphometry shows smaller grey matter in ventral posterior cortices and cerebellum (Cohen's d 0.45–0.76), smaller right amygdala and putamen (d 0.34–0.55), thinner cortex (d 0.20–0.74), and machine-learning classification up to 74% accurate. Scanning 28 cases and 26 controls in both mid-follicular and late-luteal phases found no group-by-phase interaction but a main effect of group in cerebellum and cuneus (d 0.4–1.1), arguing for trait rather than state markers — yet an earlier study of 12 and 13 found *greater* posterior cerebellar grey matter in PMDD, significant only above age 30 (p = 0.0002 versus p > 0.1 in younger women), a directional contradiction no meta-analysis has adjudicated. Fluorodeoxyglucose imaging in 12 versus 12 found no group difference in hormone levels but a follicular-to-luteal rise in cerebellar vermis activity in patients only (p = 0.003), correlating with mood worsening (p = 0.018). Amygdala reactivity is the field's replication sore point: one 14-versus-15 study found enhanced bilateral reactivity in the *follicular* phase with no luteal group difference — the opposite of the predicted pattern — with controls, not patients, showing the luteal rise. Resting-state work is more consistent in showing trait-like alterations, with stronger left middle temporal gyrus–executive control network connectivity across all four scanning sessions and no phase effect (18 versus 18), and reduced network segregation with increased integration across both phases in 24 drug-naïve patients versus 27 controls. White matter, uninvestigated as of 2020, now shows greater left uncinate fractional anisotropy (d = 0.69) and right uncinate volume (d = 0.40) across datasets of 67 and 131, and a diffusion connectome model discriminating 42 cases from 42 controls at an area under the curve of 0.855. Treatment leaves it largely alone: within a randomised placebo-controlled trial of a progesterone receptor modulator, there was no longitudinal voxel-wise change in any diffusion metric and no correlation with symptom improvement, though exploratory cross-sectional comparisons during treatment showed lower fractional anisotropy and higher mean, radial and axial diffusivity in several tracts.

Immune findings are inconsistent and, where best powered, negative. In 2,939 midlife women, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein above 3 mg/L was associated with premenstrual mood symptoms (adjusted OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.02–1.58), cramps and back pain (1.40, 1.09–1.80), cravings and bloating (1.41, 1.04–1.89) and breast pain (1.26, 1.02–1.55), but not with headaches or with reporting three or more symptoms. In 277 young women, mid-luteal interleukin-4 was 92% higher (p = 0.01), interleukin-10 87% (p = 0.03), interleukin-12 170% (p = 0.04) and interferon-γ 158% (p = 0.01) in cases — with C-reactive protein not significant, the opposite pattern to the previous study. The one PMDD-specific study, exploratory by its authors' own description, found no main effect of diagnosis, cycle phase or allopregnanolone on interleukin-6, CXCL-8, interleukin-1β, tumour necrosis factor α, or area-under-curve cortisol and corticotropin (all p > 0.05); what it did find was a three-way interaction on cortisol *peak*, higher allopregnanolone predicting lower peak in the sertraline-treated luteal phase (p = 0.042), with treated luteal peak higher than untreated (p = 0.038) — read by the authors as sertraline normalising reproductive–adrenal axis coupling. Inflammation in premenstrual disorders is an unresolved hypothesis with no pooled estimate for any marker.

Autonomic and stress-axis data are similarly modest. Cortisol was significantly lower from 40 to 90 minutes after a standardised social stress test in PMDD (time-by-diagnosis p = 0.008), with attenuated periovulatory cortisol awakening peaks, and premenstrual cortisol peak inversely related to midbrain serotonin transporter binding (r = −0.34, p < 0.01). Meta-analysis of 37 studies and 1,004 women confirms cardiac vagal activity falls from follicular to luteal phase (Hedges g = −0.39, 95% CI −0.67 to −0.11; menstrual-to-premenstrual g = −1.17, −2.18 to −0.17), driven by progesterone rather than estradiol — but the extension to PMDD fails. In 28 patients versus 11 controls, vagal indices were lower in PMDD only in the *follicular*, non-symptomatic phase, and a prospective study of 31 women found ovulatory-to-midluteal vagal decreases did not predict premenstrual affective worsening; only the atypical subgroup with luteal vagal *increases* worsened.

Psychosocially, childhood adversity is the most reliably replicated risk factor in the entire literature. In a nested analysis of 1,018 incident moderate-to-severe PMS cases over 14 years against 2,277 minimally symptomatic women, the highest level of childhood emotional abuse carried OR 2.6 (95% CI 1.7–3.9, p for trend < 0.0001) and severe physical abuse OR 2.1 (1.5–2.9, p for trend < 0.001), with adjustment for social support minimally attenuating. Across 11,973 women, prevalence ratios rose 1.12 per additional adverse childhood experience (1.11–1.13), reaching 2.46 (2.21–2.74) at four or more, with a steeper gradient for PMDD than for PMS (p < 0.001) and all 13 experience types positive. In 3,968 women aged 18–40, trauma with post-traumatic stress disorder gave OR 8.14 (3.56–18.58) and trauma without it OR 2.84 (1.26–6.42) — a dose gradient implying exposure itself, not only the disorder, matters. Clinic series concur, with 83 of 100 PMDD patients reporting early-life trauma, emotional abuse most commonly at 71%. Mechanistically the signature is not singular: patients with prior abuse showed lower resting norepinephrine, greater β-adrenoceptor responsivity and greater luteal norepinephrine stress reactivity than non-abused patients, while abused *controls* showed blunted cortisol and cardiac reactivity. No discordant-twin, sibling-comparison or instrumental-variable design has tested whether the association is causal or reflects shared familial liability, and apart from the nested cohort the exposure is measured retrospectively throughout.

The unifying frame that survives all this is hormone sensitivity as a trait rather than PMDD as an isolated disease. Blinded estradiol withdrawal reproduced depressive symptoms in women with past perimenopausal depression (Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale 2.4 to 8.8; Hamilton scale 3.0 to 6.6, both p = 0.0004) but not in controls. Third-trimester neuroactive steroid ratios predict postpartum depression, with isoallopregnanolone-to-pregnanolone OR 1.64 (95% CI 1.13–2.37), pregnanolone-to-progesterone OR 0.64 and progesterone OR 4.00, at a concordance index of 0.80–0.82 — though that work never measures PMDD or the menstrual cycle, so the bridge between the two is an inference the field draws rather than a finding it has tested. PMDD, postpartum depression and perimenopausal depression are increasingly read as three expressions of one susceptibility, and the strongest argument for that reading is that the same manipulation — remove the steroid, give it back — produces the phenotype in each.

## Clinical features & course

The phenomenology of PMDD is defined less by which symptoms occur than by when they occur, but the "which" question now has a large answer. In a Swedish web survey embedded in a population cohort, 10,457 women of fertile age were screened; 8,528 were menstruating and non-pregnant, 4,890 (57.3%) reported any premenstrual change, 1,145 (13.4%) reported change severe enough to interfere with work, relationships or activities, and 797 (9.3% of the eligible sample) additionally confirmed complete symptom-free intervals about a week after menses onset. Within that impaired group the hierarchy is unambiguously affective: irritability or anger caused impairment in 77.4% (95% CI 74.3–80.3), affective lability in 52.3%, depressed mood or hopelessness in 50.1%, fatigue in 49.6% and anxiety or tension in 39.3%, while no somatic complaint reached 20% — headache 18.8%, bloating 15.5%, joint or muscle pain 12.2%, weight gain 9.4%, breast tenderness 8.1%, appetite change 6.5%. Severity and impact dissociate sharply: only 17% of women rating breast tenderness at the top of a four-point scale called it impairing, against 90% for irritability. Ninety-three percent named at least one impairing mood symptom versus 41% at least one impairing somatic symptom, and 99% of the somatic-impaired also had an impairing mood symptom, so a purely somatic phenotype is close to non-existent — joint or muscle pain and headache as the sole impairing complaint accounted for 1.3% and 1.4% of screen-positives, roughly 0.1% of the whole sample. Hierarchical clustering separated a mood-and-behaviour cluster from a somatic cluster, with headache and joint or muscle pain splitting off as a third, which is the empirical basis for doubting that the eleven DSM items constitute one syndrome. The same data expose a criterion problem: over 88% of screen-positive women endorse five or more symptoms as present, but fewer than 35% report five or more as severe or impairing. A large Brazilian cross-sectional study of 45,160 women aged 20–49 gives the complementary picture from inside the diagnosis — prevalence 3.57% (95% CI 3.40–3.75), and among the 1,614 meeting criteria, anxiety or tension in 99.9% and irritability or anger in 99.8%, at severe intensity in 86.7% and 84.5% respectively. Those near-universal figures are close to tautological, since both are core criterion symptoms, but the severity split is informative.

Timing is the diagnosis, and modelling it correctly requires an ovulation-anchored frame, because follicular length varies while the corpus luteum fixes the luteal phase at 12–14 days. Applied to 74 women prospectively diagnosed across cycle days −15 to +9, group-based trajectory modelling produced three groups with posterior probabilities of 73–99%: a modal group with moderate symptoms confined to the premenstrual week (65%), a group with severe symptoms across the full two-week luteal phase (17.5%), and a group with severe premenstrual symptoms resolving slowly into the follicular phase (17.5%). All followed inverted-U quadratic or cubic trajectories with zero-inflation highest around ovulation. The clinical consequence is arithmetic: a full-luteal patient is symptomatic roughly 14 days per cycle, a late-onset patient roughly four. The subtypes plausibly index different pathophysiology — early onset tracking the post-ovulatory neurosteroid surge, late onset tracking late-luteal withdrawal — which matters because only 50–60% of prospectively confirmed patients respond to GnRH-agonist ovarian suppression. This is the only person-level temporal subtyping study in PMDD, it covers a single cycle in a moderate sample, its authors state that the groups are not literal classes, and within-woman stability across cycles has never been tested. Any claim about "PMDD subtypes" rests on it plus theory. Two further empirical findings should reshape how ratings are read: the symptomatic window is wider than "premenstrual", peaking from four days before through the first two days of menses in treatment-seekers and three days before through three days after onset in community samples; and four rather than five symptoms is the count that optimally predicts impairment in two independent cohorts.

Functional impairment is well quantified for moderate-to-severe premenstrual disorder and less so for strictly diagnosed PMDD. Across five alternative PMS definitions spanning prevalences of 19–30%, mental component summary scores on a generic health survey ran 5–12 points lower with roughly four extra reduced-productivity days per month. A Japanese nationwide survey of 4,818 women found PMDD the strongest driver of both presenteeism and absenteeism among menstruation-related conditions, with a combined population-attributable fraction near 15% for severe work-functioning impairment; oral contraceptives relieved presenteeism only in the PMDD group. Relationship burden extends beyond the patient: comparing 216 patients with 187 controls and 92 partners with 59 partner-controls, quality of life was lower across most domains and relationship quality lower in every domain except love and commitment — for partners as well as patients.

Suicidality is the clinically decisive finding and it is unusually well replicated across designs. Meta-analysis of six studies and 8,532 participants gives OR 2.34 (95% CI 1.50–3.18, I² = 0%) for suicidal ideation, 2.24 (1.03–3.45) for plans and 2.13 (1.05–3.21) for attempts; an independent meta-analysis gives OR 3.95 (2.97–5.24) for ideation and 6.97 (2.98–16.29) for attempt. In a nationally representative sample of 3,965 women aged 18–40, adjusted for demographics and psychiatric comorbidity, PMDD carried OR 2.22 (1.40–3.53) for ideation, 2.27 (1.20–4.28) for plans and 2.10 (1.08–4.08) for attempts, while moderate-to-severe PMS elevated only ideation (1.49, 1.17–1.88) — a dose gradient across the spectrum. Read in the other direction, 23–54% of women presenting to hospital after a suicide attempt retrospectively met PMDD criteria, against 5–6% of controls. Among 110 women with prospectively confirmed PMDD assessed at the baseline visit of two randomised trials, 39.1% reported current late-luteal suicidal ideation, with unadjusted associations for prior psychological treatment (OR 5.63, 1.07–29.49 — an interval spanning an order of magnitude and barely clearing unity) and late-luteal depressive severity (OR 1.17 per point, 1.10–1.25). A narrative systematic review of 18 studies covering some 2.6 million participants reports across-study ranges rather than pooled estimates: 16–30.47% for any suicidality, 26–86% for ideation, 11.5–41.5% for plans and 7.1–60.7% for attempts — and its most important finding is that not one study evaluated any intervention for suicidality in PMDD.

Cycle-phase patterning has moved from cross-sectional inference to prospective daily data. In 38 outpatients with past-month ideation providing 1,524 daily ratings, cycle phase accounted for 25% of the within-person variance in suicidal ideation, peaking perimenstrually and troughing periovulatorily. In 119 naturally cycling outpatients contributing 6,018 hormone-timed observations, ideation was worse perimenstrually than early- or midluteally and planning more likely perimenstrually than midfollicularly, mediated by depression, hopelessness, perceived burdensomeness and anhedonia. A 2026 analysis using phase-aligned time scaling found cyclicity explained 38% of deviance in suicidal ideation and 21.6% in self-harm ideation, peaking late-luteally, improving at menses and re-emerging near ovulation — significant only above the premenstrual-disorder diagnostic threshold (n = 35), not below (n = 29). Against this, aggregate meta-analysis of 32 papers and 3,791 participants found 26% greater risk of suicide death, 17% of attempts and 20% of psychiatric admission at menstruation, with ideation unrelated to phase at group level, and three hospital series found no luteal excess of attempts in PMDD specifically — 34% versus 35% in one. The within-person cyclicity of ideation is therefore prospectively demonstrated and has not been reproduced in attempt-timing data, and that contradiction should be reported rather than smoothed: the likeliest reconciliation, that timing varies between women and that retrospective ascription of PMDD in attempters is unreliable, is a hypothesis. No psychological-autopsy or registry study has linked completed suicide to PMDD, so every statement about suicide risk in this disorder concerns non-fatal outcomes.

Course across the reproductive lifespan is the thinnest domain in the whole chapter. No prospective cohort has followed prospectively diagnosed PMDD from menarche to menopause; available samples are middle-reproductive-age by construction, with means near 28–33 years. There is no age-at-onset distribution and no prospectively ascertained incidence estimate anywhere. Qualitative work describes a diagnostic odyssey and "the lost decades", and patients report a mean of about 12 years and five to seven providers before diagnosis. What is well documented is forward risk: prior PMS roughly doubles the odds of postpartum depression (pooled OR 2.20, 95% CI 1.81–2.68 across 19 studies, low-quality evidence), and comorbid PMDD in 1,099 women with bipolar disorder marked earlier onset (p < 0.001), more rapid cycling (p = 0.039) and a shorter menarche-to-onset interval (p = 0.003). Nothing measures what happens to PMDD *during* pregnancy; the mechanistic expectation that anovulation abolishes it is asserted in reviews without a cohort behind it.

## Diagnosis & assessment

PMDD is unusual among psychiatric diagnoses in that it cannot legitimately be made at the visit where it is raised. Every consensus body converges on the same operational rule: careful history plus two months of prospective charting, with the diagnosis resting on demonstrated cyclicity — premenstrual onset and a clear symptom-free interval between the end of menses and approximately ovulation — rather than on symptom content, which is wholly non-specific. The Montreal consensus adds a rule most clinics ignore: because premenstrual disorders do not occur in every cycle, if only one of two charted cycles is diagnostic a third should be recorded and two of three positive cycles taken as confirmation; the same document mandates two months of prospective charting before randomisation in clinical trials.

The requirement exists because retrospective report is not merely imprecise — it is wrong in a direction that manufactures cases. At population level the pooled inflation is 3.2% confirmed versus 7.7% provisional. At the level of the individual self-referred patient it is starker: of 200 women recruited specifically for retrospectively reported premenstrual emotional symptoms who then completed two to four cycles of daily ratings, 116 (58%) had no cyclical diagnosis, 46 (23%) had a non-PMDD menstrually related mood disorder and only 38 (19%) had PMDD. Four in five self-referred women with a retrospective PMDD complaint do not have prospectively confirmable PMDD. Crucially, the *degree* of retrospectively reported premenstrual change carried almost no diagnostic information: per-unit odds ratios were 1.03 (95% CI 1.01–1.06) and 1.06 (1.03–1.09), discrimination was 0.63 (0.56–0.70) and 0.70 (0.62–0.78), and no usable cut-off could be identified. Smaller series agree — of 16 women with a provisional diagnosis who completed two cycles, exactly half confirmed; and in a smartphone experience-sampling study of nine women meeting screening criteria for PMS, none showed menstrual cyclicity in mood or interpersonal behaviour across two cycles.

The Daily Record of Severity of Problems is the reference instrument: 24 items — 21 symptom items plus three impairment items — rated from 1 (not at all) to 6 (extreme). Its original validation used samples of 27 spanning no symptoms to full PMDD and 243 all meeting criteria, demonstrating high item and summary test–retest reliability, high internal consistency, moderate-to-high correlation with other severity measures, and sensitivity to treatment change. Translations replicate this: a Japanese version in 324 symptomatic women gave Cronbach's α = 0.97 with a correlation of 0.42 against a separate self-report screener, and a later general-population Japanese validation in 113 women recruited regardless of severity gave α = 0.96 with κ = 0.78 for agreement between diary-based and clinical moderate-to-severe PMS/PMDD classification. An independently developed Japanese app-collected version in 304 women found a two-factor structure with suboptimal fit (comparative fit index 0.83, root-mean-square error of approximation 0.12) and intraclass correlations of 0.60 (95% CI 0.48–0.72) luteal and 0.76 (0.69–0.82) follicular, improving to 0.99 and 0.048 with an eight-item short form; an Indonesian version in 315 women gave α = 0.90 and intraclass correlations of 0.72–0.90.

The diary measures; it does not diagnose. That gap is what the Carolina Premenstrual Assessment Scoring System closed. It fixes arithmetic that had varied across laboratories — at least five different equations for "premenstrual change" and thresholds of 30%, 50% or 75% were in circulation — and standardises the whole decision: premenstrual week as days −7 to −1, postmenstrual week as days 4–10, at least three of seven ratings present in each, at least two cycles, premenstrual maximum of 4 or more, postmenstrual maximum of 3 or less, at least two severe premenstrual days, at least 30% relative elevation computed by the range-of-scale-used method, and five symptoms including one core symptom for PMDD (one to four for the subthreshold category). Against expert clinician judgement on the menstrually related mood disorder decision it agreed in 94.3% of the 193 cases with expert ratings, with 11 disagreements; after the authors adjudicated those 11 and judged the algorithm right in seven, they estimated overall correct classification at 98%, with four women (2%) genuinely missed — all with late symptom clearance persisting past day 4. The threshold choice is load-bearing: the same comparison gave 94.3% agreement at 30%, 34% at 50% and 12% at 75%. Prevalence estimates are therefore partly an artefact of arithmetic choice.

Retrospective screeners retain a triage role, and their operating characteristics are entirely reference-standard dependent — which is the key clinical point. The Premenstrual Symptoms Screening Tool, developed in 519 primary-care women aged 18–55, translates diagnostic categories into a graded severity instrument; its adolescent revision was piloted in 578 girls across three international sites, 29.6% screening positive for severe PMS or PMDD with irritability most commonly endorsed. Against a structured-interview diagnosis in 489 Indian college students, sensitivity was 90.9% and specificity 57.0%. Against two cycles of daily ratings in 127 Brazilian women, sensitivity was 79.0% (95% CI 70.0–85.8) and specificity 33.3% (18.6–52.2) for the combined PMS/PMDD determination, with κ = 0.12; and the direction of error is instructive — the screener *under*-detected PMS (41.7% versus 74.8% by diary) while *over*-detecting PMDD (34.6% versus 3.9%), with only 6.8% of screener-positive PMDD cases confirming. Against a different interview standard in 194 Qatari primary-care women, specificity was 95.6% but sensitivity only 26.7%. A screening instrument whose specificity for the severe category is one in three is a referral trigger, not a diagnosis. Observer-rated alternatives persist — premenstrual tension rating scales with visual-analogue extensions showed α > 0.90 across four antidepressant trials totalling 1,208 women — and the older Calendar of Premenstrual Experiences classified 104 of 108 cycles correctly in 36 screened patients and 18 controls with no false positives over two consecutive cycles.

The medical workup is where the evidence thins most honestly, and the chapter should say so rather than imply otherwise. There is no laboratory test, imaging finding or biomarker for PMDD; the international consensus group reviewed saccadic eye velocity, candidate polymorphisms and neuroimaging and concluded there is no objective parameter with which to measure or diagnose premenstrual disorders. Investigations are exclusionary and their yield is essentially unquantified: no study reports the sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios or pre- and post-test probabilities of thyroid function testing, full blood count, ferritin or follicle-stimulating hormone in women presenting with premenstrual complaints. The only figures available are incidental — in 72 adolescent gynaecology attendees, three-quarters with PMS, anaemia was found in 33.3%, thyroid abnormalities in 8.3% and polycystic ovary features in 13.9% — from a small single-centre series never designed as a screening-yield study. What the charting must positively distinguish is premenstrual exacerbation from core PMDD, and that is identified by elevated *follicular*-phase scores rather than by any separate instrument. Safety screening is not optional: among 599 respondents reporting a provider diagnosis made from two cycles of daily ratings, lifetime rates were 72% active suicidal ideation, 49% planning, 42% intent, 40% preparatory behaviour, 34% attempt and 51% non-suicidal self-injury, with 70% carrying at least one comorbid diagnosis — figures from a self-selected online advocacy sample, roughly 93% white and 92% English-speaking, and therefore upper bounds.

The GnRH-agonist trial occupies a peculiar position: it is the field's most persuasive mechanistic probe and has never been validated as a diagnostic test. Efficacy is not in doubt — pooling 11 randomised trials and 275 women, agonists without add-back improved global symptoms with a standardised mean difference of −1.23 (95% CI −1.76 to −0.71; 9 trials, 173 women, I² = 72%, high certainty) — but every one of those trials required diagnosis by at least two prospective cycles as an entry criterion, so the probe presupposes the diagnosis rather than establishing it. The consensus group states explicitly that using ovulation suppression to unmask misattributed symptoms, though theoretically sound and anecdotally supported, has never been formally evaluated as a diagnostic test; no sensitivity, specificity or predictive values exist. Response is not universal — roughly 60–70% of patients improve, with 33 responders against 12 non-responders in the largest series — and, intriguingly, pre-trial daily-rating *dynamics* discriminated responders where mean symptom levels did not, both approximate entropy and "spikiness" being lower in responders (p ≤ 0.005).

The practical clinic problem is attrition and clinician behaviour. Charting fails at scale even under research conditions: of 282 eligible women who completed a screener in a hospital-based validation study, only 127 (45%) returned two complete cycles of daily ratings, 135 abandoning explicitly because of the time burden; in a multinational web-based study 822 of 1,477 (56%) completed as planned; in a US cohort only 513 of 976 (53%) agreed to chart at all. Where charting is supported, adherence is excellent at 3.4% missing days, and 85% of women who dropped out after one cycle had already failed to meet criteria in that cycle — an argument for a low-friction first cycle before committing the patient to two. Actual clinician adherence is the harder number, and the surveyed figures are unambiguous: two months of daily ratings were used by 9.7% of 247 Japanese psychiatrists and 6.6% of 407 obstetrician-gynaecologists, 8.4% in a 2021 survey of 1,267 Japanese obstetrician-gynaecologists and 11.5% in a 2012 US survey, with 86.6% of psychiatrists and 83.5% of obstetrician-gynaecologists diagnosing on interview alone. Of 2,689 respondents to a global patient survey, only 599 (23%) reported that any provider had ever examined two months of daily ratings. Gynaecologists are more likely than general practitioners, psychiatrists or psychotherapists to request daily tracking. App-based tracking is the obvious remedy and its evidence is thin and largely industry-generated: two unblinded pilot randomised trials of a commercial cycle app (321 and 117 participants) report improved health literacy (mean differences 1.11 and 1.20, both p < 0.001), reduced symptom burden (−7.08, p < 0.001) and reduced absenteeism (−1.67, p = 0.01) — outcomes about literacy and burden, not diagnostic accuracy. General app quality is poor: an evaluation of 140 fertility and menstrual apps found a mean quality score of 32.5% with 22.1% containing serious content inaccuracies, and a review of 34 apps found 91% predicted cycles but only 6% offered any health-screening information. No commercially available app has published sensitivity or specificity against expert prospective diagnosis.

The workflow that follows from all of this is unglamorous and defensible: screen with the appropriate screening tool while accepting that it over-calls PMDD and under-calls PMS; issue a daily diary for two cycles with an explicit third-cycle rule; score with a standardised algorithm rather than by eye; read the follicular week as carefully as the luteal week, because that is where premenstrual exacerbation and continuous illness declare themselves; screen for suicidality at every visit; check thyroid function and a full blood count on symptom-overlap grounds while acknowledging the absence of yield data; and reserve GnRH agonists for treatment resistance rather than for diagnosis.

## Differential diagnosis & comorbidity

The differential diagnosis of PMDD is unusual in psychiatry because the discriminating operation is not a symptom list but a timing test, and the single most consequential error is skipping it. Every competing diagnosis here — premenstrual exacerbation of major depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety disorders, borderline personality disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, eating disorders — can present with the identical premenstrual complaint. What separates them is the follicular-phase floor: PMDD requires symptoms to be substantially absent in the week after menses, whereas premenstrual exacerbation is a rise from a non-zero baseline that persists all cycle.

Premenstrual exacerbation is common enough that it, not PMDD, should be the clinician's default hypothesis in a woman already carrying a mood diagnosis. In a cross-sectional analysis nested in a large depression trial, 64% of 433 premenopausal women with major depression not taking oral contraceptives *retrospectively* reported premenstrual worsening; those reporting it had a current episode of 30.7 months (SD 73.7) versus 13.5 (SD 13.2), more general medical comorbidity, and more leaden paralysis and gastrointestinal complaints. The only representative community estimate remains a study of 900 menstruating people aged 13–53 rating symptoms daily across two cycles: of the 58 with a depressive disorder, 58% showed premenstrual exacerbation of one or more depressive symptoms — and 44% of non-depressed women taking antidepressants still had premenstrual symptoms, so "she is already on an SSRI" does not exclude a cyclical component. In bipolar disorder, 191 of 293 reproductive-age women (65.2%) reported premenstrual exacerbation and over one year had more episodes, greater severity and shorter time to relapse. A systematic review of 35 prospective studies found clear evidence of perimenstrual exacerbation for psychotic disorders, panic disorder, eating disorders, depression and borderline personality disorder, less consistent evidence for anxiety and a different pattern in bipolar disorder — against a background the authors describe as mixed overall, with meta-analysis precluded by heterogeneity. The distinction is not academic, because the treatments diverge: ovulation suppression reduced symptoms in prospectively confirmed premenstrual syndrome but not in women with ongoing postmenstrual depressive symptoms; drospirenone-containing contraceptive augmentation of antidepressants in 32 remitted women with a premenstrual rise performed no better than placebo; and the allopregnanolone antagonist's effect grew once women with elevated follicular baselines were excluded. Conversely, luteal-phase dose escalation of an existing antidepressant — meaningless in pure PMDD logic — is the intervention with signal in exacerbation. Yet premenstrual exacerbation has no operational threshold analogous to the 30% rule, so every prevalence figure for it uses incompatible criteria; in one sample, strict fixed-window criteria captured 15.7% while allowing the high- and low-risk windows to float captured 60.5%.

Bipolar disorder deserves separate handling because the error is bidirectional and one direction is dangerous. A systematic review of five studies found 10–15% of PMDD samples carrying a bipolar diagnosis and 27–76% of bipolar samples carrying a PMDD label — a 49-point range that is itself the finding, since none used prospective charting. Among 1,099 women with bipolar disorder, comorbid PMDD marked earlier onset, more rapid cycling, more past-year hypomanic or manic and lifetime depressive episodes, and higher rates of panic disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, generalised anxiety, bulimia nervosa, substance abuse and adult attention deficit (all p < 0.05), plus worse mood on oral contraceptives (p < 0.001). In 423 Korean women with bipolar disorder, 187 (44.2%) met criteria for provisional PMDD, with more bipolar II, earlier onset and an attention-deficit polygenic risk score associated with symptom severity. Treating such a woman's "PMDD" with SSRI monotherapy invites a switch.

Borderline personality disorder supplies the cleanest natural experiment. Fifteen unmedicated women with the disorder and without dysmenorrhoea rated symptoms daily for 35 days with ovulation confirmed by urinary luteinising hormone and salivary progesterone: most symptoms showed midluteal worsening and a perimenstrual peak, the majority met standardised criteria for clinically significant premenstrual exacerbation, all met the emotional-instability criterion — and not one met criteria for PMDD. A usable discriminator emerges from the timing: high-arousal symptoms such as irritability and anger peak premenstrually as in PMDD, but low-arousal depressive symptoms emerge nearer menses and persist into the follicular phase, the opposite of PMDD's rapid postmenstrual remission. Reviews put clinically significant perimenstrual exacerbation in unmedicated borderline personality disorder at 73%.

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder is the fastest-moving edge of this differential. In 715 people assigned female at birth aged 18–34, provisional PMDD by screener was present in 31.4% of those with a self-reported clinical diagnosis and 41.1% of those meeting a symptom-and-impairment threshold, versus 9.8% of 305 referents, with attention deficit plus depression or anxiety carrying a relative risk of 4.53 (95% CI 3.10–6.61). Those absolute figures rest on a screener with roughly one-in-three specificity and are almost certainly inflated; the register-scale number is the trustworthy one. A separate clinic sample of 209 women with attention-deficit disorder screened 45.5% positive for PMDD symptoms on a retrospective interview module, again without daily ratings. The discriminating feature is baseline versus swing: comparing 58 women with PMDD to 50 controls across pre-ovulatory, mid-luteal and late-luteal phases, those without comorbid attention deficit showed a greater pre-ovulatory-to-late-luteal rise than controls, whereas those with it were already elevated pre-ovulatorily and mid-luteally on prospective memory problems and dysfunctional impulsivity. Cyclical inattention with a clean follicular week is PMDD; year-round inattention that worsens premenstrually is attention deficit with premenstrual exacerbation, and the emerging management move — premenstrual stimulant dose elevation — is supported so far only by a nine-woman case series.

For eating disorders the association is specific rather than global. In a nationally representative sample of 8,694 women, PMDD carried seven times the odds of bulimia nervosa (OR 7.2, 95% CI 2.3–22.4) and PMS 2.5 times (1.1–5.7) after adjustment, while neither was significantly associated with binge-eating disorder; PMDD prevalence was 17.4% among those with bulimia versus 4.2% overall. The signal is in binge-purge, not binge alone.

Comorbidity at population level is the rule rather than the exception, and the register data are definitive on scale. Among 3,630,028 Swedish women, 104,972 received a premenstrual disorder diagnosis; 47.8% had a prior psychiatric diagnosis versus 29.5% of matched controls (OR 2.41, 95% CI 2.38–2.44), and women with psychiatric disorders were twice as likely to receive a later premenstrual diagnosis (36.6% versus 21.1%; hazard ratio 2.23, 2.19–2.27), attenuated but preserved in sibling comparison (OR 1.95; HR 1.82). Depression and anxiety carry the highest bidirectional risk — depression OR 2.19 (2.15–2.22) and HR 2.70 (2.63–2.76); anxiety OR 2.26 (2.22–2.30) and HR 2.43 (2.37–2.48) — while attention-deficit disorder, bipolar disorder and personality disorder show the steepest hazards of a subsequent premenstrual diagnosis at 3.55, 3.36 and 3.34. Schizophrenia shows nothing at all (OR 1.01, 0.88–1.16; HR 1.00, 0.59–1.72), a genuinely informative null. In a prospective community cohort of 1,488 women aged 14–24, twelve-month and lifetime comorbidity ran 47.4% for anxiety disorders, 22.9% for mood disorders and 28.4% for somatoform disorders, with only 26.5% carrying no other mental disorder — though the diagnosis there was made by structured interview without the required daily charting, which is the same inflation the section warns about. Substance use is under-recognised: among 151 women in addiction treatment versus 101 in general practice, PMS ran 48.3% versus 26.7% and PMDD 16.6% versus 5.9%. One structural caveat governs all of it: comorbidity estimates from prospectively confirmed cohorts are biased downward because most PMDD research excludes comorbid participants by design.

Medical look-alikes are best handled by one cheap screen rather than an endocrine workup. In 124 women with prospectively confirmed premenstrual syndrome, 13 (10.5%) had grade I or II thyroid dysfunction and 8 of 63 (13%) had elevated thyroid autoantibodies — yet levothyroxine was no better than placebo in 30 women, and an independent double-blind trial in 44 found no significant thyroid disease (one subject with a thyrotropin of 6.2 µIU/mL) with complete symptom relief in 27% on levothyroxine versus 45% on placebo, untested for significance. So: check thyroid function once to exclude thyroid disease as the cause of the syndrome, and do not treat premenstrual syndrome with thyroid hormone. Both trials are more than thirty years old and nothing modern exists. Polycystic ovary syndrome is a real associate rather than a mimic — among 41,515 Swedish women with the syndrome within a cohort of 2,965,178, 1,308 (3.1%) developed a premenstrual disorder over a median 15.3 years, at 4.67 per 1,000 person-years versus 1.77 in those without, age-adjusted hazard ratio 2.26 (95% CI 2.14–2.39), 1.48 (1.40–1.56) fully adjusted and 1.61 (1.36–1.92) in sibling comparison — though anovulation removes the luteal phase PMDD requires, so a patient with polycystic ovary syndrome and premenstrual complaints needs cycle documentation before either label. Menstrual migraine clusters with rather than substitutes for PMDD: among 445 women with menstrual migraine and 156 without, confirmed PMDD ran 5.6% versus 1.9%, a difference that did not reach significance (p = 0.06), while PMDD independently predicted a 3.113-point higher headache-burden score (95% CI 0.607–5.915, p = 0.016). Endometriosis, irritable bowel syndrome, dysmenorrhoea and chronic fatigue enter mainly as a co-travelling pain-and-fatigue cluster; no study reports prospectively confirmed PMDD prevalence in any of them, and in the one relevant cohort premenstrual symptoms did not differ by endometriosis status. Perimenopause is the least quantified boundary, and the practical discriminator is cycle regularity: once cycles become irregular the premenstrual window is no longer definable and prospective charting loses its anchor.

Hormonal contraception complicates the picture in three distinct ways that must be separated. It can *cause* a mood syndrome that mimics PMDD — the consensus taxonomy formally names progestogen-induced premenstrual disorder as a variant, and a Danish national cohort of 1,061,997 women aged 15–34 found first antidepressant use elevated with combined oral contraceptives (relative risk 1.23, 95% CI 1.22–1.25), progestogen-only pills (1.34, 1.27–1.40), patch (2.0, 1.76–2.18), vaginal ring (1.6, 1.55–1.69) and levonorgestrel intrauterine system (1.4, 1.31–1.42), peaking six months after initiation and reaching 1.8 for combined pills and 2.2 for progestin-only pills among 15–19-year-olds, with relative risks of 1.97 (1.85–2.10) for suicide attempt and 3.08 (1.34–7.08) for suicide. It can also produce a real but modest average effect that is *not* depression: in a double-blind placebo-controlled trial of 340 healthy women given levonorgestrel and ethinylestradiol for three months, general well-being fell by 4.12 points (95% CI −7.18 to −1.06) with decrements in positive well-being, self-control and vitality, while depression scores did not differ — consistent with a susceptible minority inside a near-null average. And it *masks* PMDD, because ovulation suppression removes the luteal phase the diagnosis is defined against; a woman on an ovulation-suppressing method cannot be charted for PMDD at all. Disentangling rests on temporal architecture: a contraceptive-induced syndrome tracks the pill packet and the hormone-free interval and begins within weeks to months of initiation; PMDD tracks ovulation and remits with menses. Where safe, two cycles of charting off the method resolves it — a recommendation that is expert opinion, never tested.

How often is all this missed? Among 267 patients who had sought care for PMDD, participants had consulted a mean of 5.1 different providers, judged 51% of them to have poor awareness, and reported medical gaslighting (54%) and misdiagnosis (56%) — naming depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder and personality disorders as the labels received — with fewer than one in five (19%) having experienced diagnostic methods consistent with current criteria. In a UK survey of 339 help-seekers, 75.2% rated care poor and 44.3% felt their symptoms were not taken seriously. Qualitative work in 27 women across nine countries identifies medical gaslighting and diagnostic odyssey as core themes, and a separate scoping review of 19 studies independently identifies difficulty reaching the diagnosis. And in a Japanese gynaecology clinic where diaries were actually used, 10 of 32 women (31%) diagnosed with a premenstrual disorder had premenstrual exacerbation rather than a core disorder — every one with an underlying psychiatric condition, with higher anxiety and depression and lower quality of life than the core group. The differential is therefore not a rare-diagnosis exercise: roughly a third of correctly charted treatment-seekers have the condition that the standard PMDD treatments do not treat. No study has ever taken a consecutive clinical series, recorded the clinician's diagnosis and then applied prospective charting to compute the sensitivity and specificity of clinical judgement, so every misdiagnosis figure above is patient-reported or inferred.

## Treatment

### Pharmacological — serotonergic

Serotonergic antidepressants are the only PMDD treatment with a genuinely mature randomised evidence base, and its size is worth stating precisely: the 2024 Cochrane update identified 34 placebo-controlled trials enrolling 4,563 women with prospectively confirmed severe premenstrual syndrome or PMDD, published between 1991 and 2020, of which 19 were conducted in the United States and at least 23 (68%) were manufacturer-funded. Pooled across 12 trials and 1,742 participants, SSRIs reduced overall self-rated premenstrual symptoms with a standardised mean difference of −0.57 (95% CI −0.72 to −0.42; I² = 51%), graded moderate-certainty. The estimate is remarkably stable across review generations — the 2009 review found −0.53 (−0.68 to −0.39) over 2,294 women, and the 2013 update found −0.65 (−0.46 to −0.84) for moderate-dose *end* scores across nine trials of 1,276 women, graded low-quality with I² = 58%, falling to −0.36 (−0.20 to −0.51) when *change* scores were pooled across four trials of 657 women at moderate quality. That last pair matters: the −0.65 figure that guidelines quote as the headline is the most favourable of several defensible poolings. A moderate effect size in a disorder that is absent for two weeks of every month is nonetheless a large clinical effect, and the benefit is not confined to mood — behavioural symptoms −0.41 (−0.53 to −0.29), physical −0.34 (−0.45 to −0.22), functional impairment −0.30 (−0.43 to −0.17). No Cochrane review reports a number needed to treat for response; derived from individual trials it sits consistently between 3 and 7. Sertraline at 50–150 mg produced "much or very much improved" global ratings in 62% versus 34% on placebo among 243 randomised, and in a three-arm trial of 189 women, at least 50% symptom reduction occurred in 65% on sertraline, 36% on desipramine and 29% on placebo. Luteal escitalopram 20 mg produced at least 80% reduction in irritability in 80% versus 30%. Venlafaxine 50–200 mg produced more than 50% improvement in 60% versus 35% and remission in 43% versus 25%.

The three dosing strategies are the clinically live question, and the literature genuinely disagrees. Placebo-controlled indirect comparison favours continuous dosing: the 2024 review found a formal subgroup difference (p = 0.03), with continuous at −0.69 (−0.88 to −0.51; 7 studies, 1,055 women) against luteal-phase at −0.39 (−0.58 to −0.21; 6 studies, 687 women), reproducing an earlier meta-analysis of 29 studies and 2,964 women (overall OR 0.40, 95% CI 0.31–0.51; continuous 0.28, 0.18–0.42; intermittent 0.55, 0.45–0.68). But indirect comparison across trials confounds regimen with population and instrument, and the only meta-analysis restricted to head-to-head randomisation found nothing at all: 8 trials, 460 participants, response OR 1.0 (95% CI 0.23–4.31, I² = 71%), dropout OR 1.26 (0.39–4.09), symptom standardised mean difference 0.04 (−0.27 to 0.35), with no included study at low risk of bias. The largest single head-to-head trial found both continuous and luteal sertraline superior to placebo and indistinguishable from each other. The defensible reading is that luteal dosing is somewhat weaker on average across trials but has never been shown inferior when randomised directly, and it eliminates the discontinuation syndrome of long-term continuous use. Symptom-onset dosing — starting only when symptoms appear, typically four to seven drug-days — is the thinnest of the three: the definitive 252-woman multisite trial missed its primary endpoint (difference 1.88, 95% CI 0.01–3.75, p = 0.06) while hitting secondaries (clinician-rated depression 5.14, 1.97–8.31, p = 0.02; responders 67.0% versus 52.4%, p = 0.02, derived number needed to treat about 7), and crucially found no excess withdrawal symptoms after abrupt cessation. A 27-woman pilot found 51% symptom reduction with symptom-onset escitalopram over a mean 6.0 drug-days against 57% with luteal dosing over 13.5 days, with more severe patients responding less well to the shorter exposure.

The rapid-onset phenomenon is the mechanistically load-bearing observation and the most useful thing to tell a patient. In a double-blind crossover of 22 previously paroxetine-responsive women instructed to start drug mid-luteally after two days of intense irritability, paroxetine separated from placebo on sustained irritability reduction at 14 hours, with rating scores separating by day 3; nausea appeared even faster, at 4 hours. Hourly ratings in 12 women given luteal fluoxetine 20 mg, compared against 12 untreated women in an open non-randomised design, showed significant improvement by day 2 across irritability, sadness, anxiety and mood swings — with an identical kinetic profile to the spontaneous symptom offset at menses onset, suggesting these drugs recruit an endogenous rapid state-change capacity rather than building an effect. Low-dose clomipramine at 25–75 mg produced more than 70–80% symptom reduction against roughly 40–45% on placebo in two small trials whose authors explicitly noted the lag was shorter than in depression, panic or obsessive-compulsive disorder. This is incompatible with the monoamine-downregulation account of antidepressant latency. The leading alternative is direct enzymatic modulation of neurosteroidogenesis: in purified recombinant human enzyme, fluoxetine, sertraline and paroxetine lower the Michaelis constant of the 3α-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type III conversion of 5α-dihydroprogesterone to allopregnanolone by 10- to 30-fold, while imipramine has no effect; in socially isolated mice, fluoxetine and norfluoxetine raise brain allopregnanolone stereospecifically at doses roughly 50-fold below those needed to inhibit serotonin reuptake, prompting a proposed reclassification as selective brain steroidogenic stimulants. Two caveats belong beside it. That chain is in vitro and rodent. And the first placebo-controlled human study to measure it found post-treatment allopregnanolone significantly *lower* in the 27 improved participants than in the 19 unimproved — pooled across sertraline, desipramine and placebo arms, so an observational within-trial association rather than a randomised comparison, and the opposite direction to a naive account in which SSRIs raise allopregnanolone and allopregnanolone fixes PMDD. Luteal sertraline at 50 mg does raise pregnanolone and the pregnanolone-to-progesterone ratio, so the pharmacology is real; no human study has demonstrated mediation.

Selectivity of response is itself the mechanistic argument, and it rests on a convergence of underpowered trials rather than one decisive comparison. Paroxetine beat the noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor maprotiline on irritability, carbohydrate craving, bloating, breast tenderness and global rating across arms of 22, 21 and 22, with maprotiline no better than placebo. Desipramine at 36% response was indistinguishable from placebo at 29% while sertraline in the same trial reached 65%. Fluoxetine 20 mg beat bupropion 300 mg and placebo on global rating in a 34-completer trial. Buspirone at 21–27 mg was modestly superior to placebo on global improvement (p < 0.001) while nefazodone was not, and notably caused no excess sexual dysfunction — a reasonable option specifically for SSRI-emergent sexual side effects. Dose matters within the SSRIs as well, though not as starkly as often claimed: in 260 women randomised to luteal fluoxetine, 20 mg met the primary daily-rating endpoint (p = 0.005) and additionally beat placebo on breast tenderness (p < 0.001), bloating (p = 0.001) and joint or muscle pain (p = 0.037), while 10 mg missed the primary endpoint (p = 0.100) — but both doses beat placebo on mood-related symptoms (p < 0.05), so 20 mg has better efficacy rather than 10 mg being inert.

Harms are frequent, dose-related and matter disproportionately in an otherwise well population who will be treated for decades. Withdrawal for adverse effects was 2.55-fold more likely on moderate-dose SSRI (95% CI 1.84–3.53; 15 studies, 2,447 women). The 2024 numbers needed to harm are nausea 8 (OR 3.30, 2.58–4.21; absolute risk 200 versus 70 per 1,000), asthenia 9 (OR 3.28, 2.16–4.98), somnolence 13, dry mouth 15, decreased libido 16 (OR 2.32, 1.57–3.42; 14 studies, 2,781 women), tremor 17 (OR 5.38, 2.20–13.16), insomnia 18, fatigue 18, sweating 21, dizziness 22, constipation 27 and diarrhoea 28. Sexual dysfunction is described everywhere as the key adherence problem and has never been measured with a validated instrument in any PMDD trial — the entire quantitative basis is spontaneous reporting of decreased libido. Roughly a third to two-fifths of treated women do not reach responder criteria, and both symptom profile and treatment duration predict outcome: the number needed to treat was 3.9 for a predominantly psychological symptom profile but 7.1 for a predominantly physical one, which did not separate from placebo at all (mean difference 9.50, 95% CI −16.29 to 35.28). Relapse after stopping is fast and common — 60% after four months of treatment versus 41% after twelve, with median time to relapse four versus eight months (hazard ratio 0.58, 0.34–0.98). There is no head-to-head trial of one SSRI against another in PMDD, no systematic estimate of the non-responder rate, and no trial of what to do after SSRI failure; second-line dose escalation, switching and the step to ovulation suppression are all expert consensus only.

### Pharmacological — hormonal and ovulation suppression

Every hormonal treatment is downstream of the suppression-and-add-back result: the disorder is an abnormal response to normal hormone change, not a hormone deficiency, which explains at a stroke both why ovulation suppression works and why progesterone supplementation — the intuitive treatment — does not.

Combined oral contraceptives occupy an awkward position, suppressing ovulation while reintroducing an exogenous steroid cycle. Drospirenone 3 mg with ethinylestradiol 20 µg in a 24/4 regimen was the first hormonal contraceptive to carry a regulatory indication for PMDD, and the approval rests on two trials. The pivotal parallel-group trial randomised 450 women across two run-in and three treatment cycles: total daily symptom scores fell 37.49 points on active drug versus 29.99 on placebo, an adjusted mean difference of −7.5 (95% CI −11.2 to −3.8, p < 0.001) on a 126-point scale, with mood −3.9 (−5.84 to −2.01), physical −2.1 (−3.3 to −0.95) and behavioural −1.5 (−2.25 to −0.73) subscale differences; 48% versus 36% achieved 50% symptom reduction (relative risk 1.7, 1.1–2.6, p = 0.015), giving a number needed to treat of 8. The crossover companion in 64 women, terminated early for recruitment failure, found a difference of −12.47 (−18.28 to −6.66, p < 0.001) and global response in 61.7% versus 31.8% (p = 0.009). Pooled in Cochrane across five trials and 1,920 women, the two PMDD trials give a mean difference of −7.92 (95% CI −11.16 to −4.67), with parallel gains in productivity (−0.31, −0.55 to −0.08), social activities (−0.29, −0.54 to −0.04) and relationships (−0.30, −0.54 to −0.06); the reviewers conclude the combination may help women with severe symptoms, that is PMDD, while flagging a large placebo effect and no data beyond three cycles. The cost is real: nausea OR 3.15 (1.90–5.22), intermenstrual bleeding OR 4.92 (3.03–7.96), breast pain OR 2.67 (1.50–4.78), any drug-related adverse event OR 2.36 (1.62–3.44). Drospirenone also roughly doubles venous thromboembolism risk relative to levonorgestrel — adjusted rate ratio 2.1 (1.6–2.8) across 8,010,290 woman-years, an absolute rate near 10 per 10,000 woman-years, such that some 2,000 women would need to switch progestin to prevent one event annually.

Two design features are held to matter and the evidence for them is asymmetric. The shortened four-day hormone-free interval is well grounded: among 262 contraceptive users, symptoms during the seven-day hormone-free interval versus active-pill weeks ran at pelvic pain 70% versus 21%, headache 70% versus 53%, analgesic use 69% versus 43%, bloating 58% versus 19% and breast tenderness 38% versus 16% (all p < 0.001) — the classic "pill period" is itself a steroid-withdrawal syndrome, and drospirenone's half-life beyond 30 hours carries activity into the shortened gap. The antimineralocorticoid and antiandrogenic profile of drospirenone is the stated reason it should outperform other progestins on bloating, breast tenderness and acne, and cycle-by-cycle subanalysis in 449 women showed superiority over placebo in negative emotions, food cravings and water retention across all three cycles (p ≤ 0.0001). But progestin superiority has never been demonstrated head-to-head in PMDD. A Bayesian network meta-analysis of 9 trials and 1,205 women found combined oral contraceptives beat placebo on overall premenstrual symptomatology (standardised mean difference 0.41, 95% credible interval 0.17–0.67) but not on premenstrual *depressive* symptoms specifically (0.22, −0.06 to 0.47), with no contraceptive superior to any other; a two-year comparison of drospirenone against desogestrel gave OR 0.87 (0.63–1.22) for symptoms. Continuous versus cyclic dosing has exactly one three-arm head-to-head trial — 55 women randomised to placebo, 21/7 or continuous — and all three arms improved robustly and equivalently with a marked placebo response. The favourable extended-regimen data are uncontrolled: 111 women started and 80 completed a year, with mood, headache and pelvic pain better than during their own prior 21/7 cycles (p < 0.001), but this is a single-arm before-after conversion cohort, and 56% had breakthrough bleeding, managed better by a three-day hormone-free interval than by continuing (p < 0.001).

Progesterone and progestogens do not work, and this is among the best-supported negative findings in the field — though the reasoning matters. Meta-analysis of 10 progesterone trials (531 women) gave a standardised mean difference of −0.028 (95% CI −0.017 to −0.040) with OR 1.05 (1.03–1.08), and four progestogen trials (378 women) gave −0.036 (−0.014 to −0.060) with OR 1.07 (1.03–1.11). Both odds ratios nominally favour treatment; the negative verdict rests on the effect being clinically meaningless, not absent, and the guideline grade of A reflects that. Cochrane could admit only 2 of 17 progesterone studies and reached no conclusion. Worse than inert, progestogens can provoke symptoms: adding oestrogen plus cyclical progestogen during GnRH-agonist therapy worsened global symptoms versus placebo add-back (standardised mean difference 0.90, 95% CI 0.17–1.63, 32 women), and in a randomised crossover of three add-back regimens in 27 patients, estradiol 1.5 mg with progesterone 400 mg produced the most pronounced symptom recurrence — hence the recommendation for long-cycle add-back minimising progestogen exposure.

Non-contraceptive estradiol suppresses ovulation without a progestin cycle but needs endometrial protection. Cochrane found 5 trials and 305 women; continuous transdermal or implanted oestrogen with progestogen gave a small-to-moderate standardised mean difference of −0.34 (95% CI −0.59 to −0.10, p = 0.005; 3 trials, 158 women, I² = 63%) — rated very low certainty throughout, with no trial longer than eight months and no endometrial or breast safety data. Luteal-phase oral oestrogen was ineffective and possibly aggravating in an 11-woman crossover. A 100 versus 200 µg patch comparison was inconclusive on efficacy but favoured the lower dose on oestrogen-attributable adverse events (relative risk 0.51, 95% CI 0.26–0.99, 107 women). The founding implant trial in 68 women is chastening for a different reason: 33 of 35 (94%) responded initially to placebo, waning over time, with active superiority across all six symptom clusters emerging only by two months. A levonorgestrel intrauterine system is the pragmatic way to protect the endometrium without systemic progestogen, but there is no randomised trial in prospectively diagnosed premenstrual disorder; the only randomised data come from 236 women treated for menorrhagia, in whom both hysterectomy and the intrauterine system reduced premenstrual symptoms by six months (p ≤ 0.028) with no between-group difference.

GnRH agonists are the most effective ovulation suppressant and the smallest evidence base. Pooling 11 trials and 275 women, agonists without add-back improved global symptoms with a standardised mean difference of −1.23 (95% CI −1.76 to −0.71; 9 trials, 173 women, I² = 72%) at high certainty — a large effect, corroborated by an earlier meta-analysis giving −1.19 (−1.88 to −0.51) and an equivalent odds ratio of 8.66 (2.52–30.26). The cost is equally certain: withdrawal due to adverse events at relative risk 4.24 (95% CI 1.10–16.36; 6 trials, 140 women), and menopausal side effects at 1.93 (0.83–4.48). With add-back, a single trial gave a mean difference of −3.89 (−6.19 to −1.59) in 31 women at low certainty, and the same meta-analysis found add-back attenuated efficacy to 0.12 (−0.35 to 0.58). No trial reported quality of life and no trial reported bone outcomes — the two endpoints that decide whether long-term use is defensible — so the bone cost that motivates add-back is quantified only by extrapolation; the sole long-run data are a 20-patient pilot in which continuous estradiol 1 mg with pulsed norethindrone held bone density stable over a mean 37.7 months. No GnRH *antagonist* has ever been tested in premenstrual disorders despite oral dosing, absence of flare and dose-titratable partial suppression being theoretically better suited to this indication.

Danazol suppresses ovulation at full dose and is largely obsolete: 200 mg twice daily reduced tension-scale scores by 14.0 (SD 10.7) from a baseline of 25.4 versus 3.6 (9.5) on placebo (p = 0.013) in 28 analysable women, with 43.8% versus 8.3% reaching the asymptomatic range — but restricted to the luteal phase in 100 women it improved only mastalgia and neither the global score nor any other symptom, because luteal dosing spares the androgenic side effects and forfeits ovulation suppression, which is the active ingredient. Spironolactone targets physical symptoms: 100 mg daily from day 14 to menses in a 35-woman crossover reduced both negative mood and somatic scores (both p < 0.001), though a smaller crossover found no difference.

Bilateral oophorectomy with hysterectomy is the only permanent cure and has no randomised or controlled evidence whatsoever. The best available series interviewed 47 women operated for severe premenstrual syndrome: median age 42 (interquartile range 39.8–46.6), 9.68 years (SD 6.8) of symptoms and 3.57 years (SD 2.0) of treatment before gynaecological referral; 93.6% reported complete resolution of cyclical symptoms, 96% were satisfied or very satisfied, and 93.6% continued hormone replacement — usually estradiol-plus-testosterone implants — for a mean 3.8 years (SD 1.86). Those are uncontrolled self-reports in a condition with a documented 94% initial placebo-response rate, which is the single most important caveat in this section. The justifying criteria are strict and cumulative: prospectively confirmed severe disorder, failure of SSRIs and of medical ovulation suppression, demonstrated response to a GnRH-agonist trial, completed childbearing, and willingness to take oestrogen to the age of natural menopause. The predictive validity of that agonist trial for surgical outcome has never been quantified — there is no published sensitivity, specificity or positive predictive value for the proposition that remission on chemical menopause predicts remission after surgery — and 63% of women with a retained uterus develop progestogen-intolerance recurrence.

### Psychotherapeutic

Cognitive behavioural therapy is the psychotherapy with any real evidentiary base, and even that base is small and old. The principal meta-analysis pooled nine randomised trials of psychological intervention, of which five tested cognitive behavioural therapy, and reported effect sizes of −0.58 (95% CI −1.15 to −0.01) for anxiety and −0.55 (−1.05 to −0.05) for depression, both with a number needed to treat of 5, plus −0.70 (−1.29 to −0.10) for behavioural change and −0.78 (−1.53 to −0.03) for interference with daily living, number needed to treat 4 for each. Every interval nearly touches zero, each pooled estimate draws on only two or three trials, and the authors graded the evidence low quality. A larger synthesis covering 22 controlled trials put cognitive behavioural therapy at 0.24–0.70 at post-treatment depending on outcome, against 0.29–0.58 for serotonergic antidepressants — roughly equipotent while being far slower to act — with follow-up data existing only for the therapy arms, where effects were 0.46–0.74, slightly larger after treatment stopped than at endpoint. The authors' own verdict was that efficacy for both modalities was not satisfactory.

The single head-to-head trial remains a three-arm pragmatic study in which 108 women with PMDD confirmed over two prospective cycles were randomised to ten sessions of therapy, fluoxetine 20 mg daily, or both, with 60 completing six months. Its conclusion is that the two are equally effective: there was no group difference in the proportion still meeting criteria after treatment, fluoxetine acted faster, therapy showed better maintenance of gains at one-year naturalistic follow-up, and combination conferred no additional benefit over either monotherapy. That speed-versus-durability trade-off, from one trial with substantial attrition and never replicated, is essentially the whole evidence base for sequencing decisions. No trial has tested whether therapy adds to an SSRI in partial responders, which is the commonest real clinical situation — the combination arm tested simultaneous initiation, not augmentation.

Internet-delivered therapy is where the field has moved. The largest trial randomised 174 women with PMDD to eight weeks of therapist-guided internet cognitive behavioural therapy or waitlist and found significant time-by-group interactions on functional impairment, psychological impairment, interference with everyday life, symptom intensity and symptom disability, stable at six months; its published abstract reports no effect sizes, and it drew a formal published critique. A smaller trial in 92 students with moderate-to-severe premenstrual syndrome produced a clean number: total daily symptom score 10.4 versus 20.2, adjusted difference −9.9 (95% CI −13.3 to −6.6), and perimenstrual quality of life +14.1 (8.5–19.8), with no effect on late-follicular quality of life (+1.9, −4.4 to 8.1) — a specificity finding arguing the effect is on the symptomatic phase rather than on global wellbeing. Guided delivery is the established form; fully self-guided psychotherapy in premenstrual disorders is not yet established and is named as a research priority. Mindfulness-based trials are numerous, uniformly positive and uniformly weak, using inactive or no-treatment controls, screened rather than prospectively confirmed samples, and no blinding.

One trial should discipline how every unblinded study in this field is read. Randomising 150 women to treatment as usual, open-label placebo without a rationale, or open-label placebo with a rationale, the rationale arm beat treatment as usual with an effect size of 0.90 and beat the no-rationale placebo at 0.55, while placebo without a rationale was indistinguishable from treatment as usual. That is a larger effect than most therapy estimates in this section, produced by expectancy framing alone — and almost every psychotherapy and lifestyle trial here used a waitlist control, which means their effect sizes are systematically inflated by an amount nobody has quantified.

### Neuromodulation and other somatic

This is the thinnest area in the chapter and the honest summary is that it is pilot-level at best and largely negative. The entire published repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation literature in premenstrual disorders is one case report plus one uncontrolled pilot of ten women given 10–12 right-dorsolateral-prefrontal sessions in the luteal phase across a within-subject cycle-to-cycle comparison, reporting reduced depressive (p = 0.034 mid-luteal, p < 0.001 late-luteal) and anxiety (p = 0.019, p = 0.001) symptoms with no control group. No sham-controlled trial exists, in any protocol, despite PMDD being a depressive disorder with a defined symptomatic window that is theoretically ideal for a short intensive course. For transcranial direct current stimulation there are two trials, neither in prospectively confirmed PMDD: a sham-controlled study of 40 participants over five daily home sessions missed its primary endpoint (group-by-time F(2,64) = 1.09, p = 0.34) with a follow-up between-group effect size of −0.53 corresponding to a mean visual-analogue difference of roughly 16 points (95% CI −37.2 to 5.2), and an acute crossover in 16 women was flatly negative on mood. The nearest positive signal is an amygdala-derived electroencephalographic neurofeedback proof-of-concept in 27 women randomised 2:1, with a core-symptom group-by-time effect at three months (F(1,15) = 4.968, p = 0.042) driven by anger (F(1,15) = 22.254, p < 0.001) and no sham arm.

Chronobiological intervention is the most mechanistically interesting and clinically least useful strand. PMDD carries a genuine circadian signature: luteal melatonin onset is delayed, duration compressed, and amplitude, area under the curve and mean levels reduced relative to the follicular phase, with no such cycle-dependent change in controls; nocturnal core temperature is elevated and slow-wave sleep paradoxically increased. Phase-shift responses to morning bright light are blunted or reversed in the luteal phase specifically (group effect p = 0.045 in the original eight-patient study, replicated at 6,000 lux in 17 patients and 14 controls), while melatonin suppression by 200 lux is exaggerated (median 30.8% versus −0.2%, p = 0.040). Yet light as a treatment does not deliver: pooling the three higher-quality crossover trials, totalling 55 participants, gives −0.20 (95% CI −0.48 to 0.07) — not a null result but an underpowered and inconclusive one, as the reviewers themselves say, justifying neither enthusiastic dissemination nor confident rejection. One early trial found bright morning, bright evening and dim red evening light all reducing depression ratings equally; another reported that active bright white light improved depression and premenstrual tension from baseline while dim red light did not, with no direct between-condition statistic. The one positive chronobiological trial is a sleep-and-light intervention in 44 randomised women, in which one night of late wake therapy plus seven days of morning bright light improved mood more than the phase-delay condition (p < 0.05), with mood improvement correlating with the size of the melatonin-offset phase advance (p < 0.001) — and only late, not early, wake therapy shifted melatonin timing at all. Exogenous melatonin has been tested in five women, open-label; that is the entire interventional literature, which makes it the clearest unexploited mechanistic lead in the chapter.

Acupuncture rests on a review of five trials and 277 women in which the mood and physical benefits versus sham (mean differences −9.03, 95% CI −10.71 to −7.35 and −9.11, −10.82 to −7.40) each come from a single 67-participant low-quality trial, response rate under random effects was 4.22 (0.45–39.88, I² = 82%), and adverse-event data were indeterminate. No acupuncture trial has ever compared it against an SSRI or any guideline-recommended active treatment.

### Psychosocial, lifestyle and nutraceutical

Exercise is guideline-recommended on weak data. The principal meta-analysis identified 15 trials of 717 participants, pooled 7 of 265, and reported a global effect of −1.08 (95% CI −1.88 to −0.29) with I² = 87% — psychological −1.67 (−2.38 to −0.96), physical −1.62 (−2.41 to −0.83), behavioural −1.94 (−2.45 to −1.44) — with 87% of trials at high risk of bias. A 2026 three-arm trial with functional near-infrared imaging in 56 analysed participants found both eight-week aerobic and resistance programmes reduced anxiety and emotional interference versus control, resistance superior to aerobic and associated with greater left dorsolateral prefrontal oxygenation. Yoga has two meta-analyses that answer different questions: one pooled 11 of 14 studies and reported benefit on total premenstrual score and on every sub-domain except the physical, while another pooled 7 of 14 for cardiovascular outcomes and found nothing excluding zero (systolic −0.30, 95% CI −2.29 to 1.69; diastolic −0.25, −0.99 to 0.49; heart rate 0.08, −0.83 to 0.99), with heterogeneity of 79–96% throughout.

Calcium has the only large supplement trial. Randomising 466 evaluable women (497 enrolled, 720 screened) across 12 US centres to 1,200 mg elemental calcium as carbonate or placebo for three cycles produced a 48% reduction in total luteal symptom score versus 30% on placebo by the third cycle, with separation at cycles 2 (p = 0.007) and 3 (p < 0.001) and all four symptom factors significantly reduced; an earlier 33-completer crossover found luteal (p = 0.011) and menstrual (p = 0.032) benefit with no intermenstrual effect and 73% preferring calcium to 15% placebo. The corrective is a three-arm pilot of fluoxetine 10 mg twice daily, calcium carbonate 600 mg twice daily and placebo over four cycles in 39 women, in which fluoxetine produced effect sizes of 0.80–2.08 against placebo and calcium only 0.10–0.44 — the daily-rating estimate for fluoxetine was β = −0.28 (p = 0.02, interval not reliably reported) with a global-improvement estimate of β = −1.03 (95% CI −1.70 to −0.35, p = 0.04) — and whose investigators concluded a larger comparative trial was not warranted.

Vitamin B6 up to 100 mg/day gave an odds ratio of 2.32 (95% CI 1.95–2.54) for overall symptom improvement across 9 trials and 940 patients and 1.69 (1.39–2.06) for depressive symptoms across 4 trials and 541 patients, with the authors explicitly limiting the conclusion by trial quality; prolonged high-dose use risks sensory neuropathy, and UK guidance caps over-the-counter supply at 10 mg/day. Magnesium alone is essentially unsupported — one crossover of 32 women using magnesium pyrrolidone carboxylic acid providing 360 mg elemental magnesium daily in three divided doses from cycle day 15 to menses reduced total and negative-affect scores, and systematic review found 4 of 7 studies positive on poor-quality evidence. Vitamin D pooled across 5 trials of 436 participants gives −0.78 (95% CI −1.30 to −0.26, I² = 58%) for total severity, −1.00 (−1.99 to −0.01) physical and −0.78 (−1.53 to −0.02) depression, with no effect on anxiety, craving or water retention and certainty graded very low; observational 25-hydroxyvitamin D differences are null (weighted mean difference −3.35, 95% CI −7.80 to 1.11, p = 0.14), so any signal is confined to repleting deficient women — consistent with a 158-adolescent trial in girls below 10 ng/mL where anxiety scores fell from 51 to 20. No trial has stratified randomisation by baseline vitamin D status to test that directly.

Chasteberry has the largest pooled effect and the worst bias profile: 14 of 17 trials pooled give Hedges' g of −1.21 (95% CI −1.53 to −0.88) with I² = 91%, high risk of bias, and funnel-plot evidence of publication bias, the meta-analysts stating the estimate at best overestimates the true effect. Its best single trial randomised 67 women and reported symptom sums falling from 29.38 (SD 7.63) to 4.28 (5.76) against 28.76 (8.23) to 11.79 (11.78) on placebo, with between-group significance at cycle 3 reached for the total score, negative affect and water retention but not for food cravings or pain; a Chinese multicentre trial of 217 showed a within-placebo improvement near 50%, which is the real interpretive problem. A systematic review cataloguing 62 herbs, vitamins and minerals promoted for premenstrual syndrome found randomised evidence for only 10, judged only calcium to have good-quality support, and found no benefit for evening primrose oil or magnesium oxide.

For self-management, the strongest evidence-based instruction is diagnostic rather than therapeutic: two months of prospective daily charting is the top-ranked auditable standard. Tracking as treatment is not supported and pure psychoeducation was found ineffective in meta-analysis — though psychoeducation demonstrably changes third-party perception, with observers rating a patient as warmer (p < 0.001), more competent (p = 0.006) and less depressed (p < 0.001) after a factual briefing, which matters for the stigma component of the illness.

## Special populations

Adolescence is where PMDD begins, and prevalence in teenagers is not lower than in adults. A screening tool revised for adolescents and piloted across three international sites in 578 girls found 29.6% positive for severe premenstrual syndrome or PMDD, with irritability the commonest symptom — rates and symptom profile essentially those of adults. Community estimates using structured interview are much lower and more defensible: 5.8% twelve-month prevalence (5.3% after exclusions) in a cohort of 1,488 aged 14–24, with a further 18.6% near-threshold. Screener-based figures vary wildly by setting — 19.05% among 939 Bangladeshi girls aged 11–19, a pooled 54.5% across 12 Ethiopian student studies, 16.5% among 1,062 Lebanese university students — and that spread is an instrument artefact as much as a cultural one, since none used two cycles of prospective rating. The diagnostic difficulty in adolescents is specific and compounding: cycles are frequently anovulatory and irregular in the first gynaecological years, the required two-cycle chart takes longer when cycles are long, and the differential against emerging major depression, bipolar disorder, borderline traits and premenstrual exacerbation is at its hardest precisely when psychopathology is first declaring itself. Neurodevelopmental comorbidity adds to it: in a clinical adolescent series of 290 aged 10–19, moderate-to-severe premenstrual syndrome was associated with attention-deficit disorder (OR 2.43) and with combined autism-plus-attention-deficit (OR 3.27) but not with autism alone — consistent with an adult autistic sample in which PMDD ran 14.3% versus 9.5%, a non-significant difference. Adolescent treatment evidence must be stated plainly: there has never been a randomised controlled trial of any pharmacological or psychological treatment for PMDD in adolescents. The evidence base is three published fluoxetine case reports sustained over two years, plus extrapolation, and the 2023 obstetric guideline states explicitly that its adult-derived recommendations are applied to adolescents on expert consensus.

The perimenopause is where PMDD does not simply fade. In a cohort of 436 women followed five years, premenstrual syndrome declined significantly with age (p < 0.001) and with change in bleeding status (p = 0.003) — but women reporting it at enrolment were substantially more likely over the following five years to report hot flushes (OR 2.09, 95% CI 1.42–3.08), depressed mood (OR 2.34, 1.60–3.43), poor sleep (OR 1.72, 1.16–2.53) and decreased libido (OR 1.54, 1.06–2.24). Rates of major depression and clinically meaningful depressive symptoms rise two- to threefold across the transition; the mechanism usually offered — that fluctuating and extreme estradiol plus a failure of GABA-A-mediated buffering unmasks the same hormone-sensitivity phenotype — is a proposed heuristic model, not a demonstrated pathway. A useful framing distinguishes four sensitivity profiles (increase-sensitive, decrease-sensitive, change-sensitive, insensitive), each predicting different timing of midlife mood disturbance, but no study has followed prospectively confirmed PMDD cases through the staged transition with daily ratings, so the trajectory of PMDD into perimenopause is inferred rather than measured.

The strongest special-population signal is perinatal, and here the register data are unusually strong. Pooled across 19 observational studies, prior premenstrual syndrome roughly doubles the odds of postpartum depression (OR 2.20, 95% CI 1.81–2.68), with risk of bias moderate to critical throughout. Swedish register data are far better: among 1,803,309 pregnancies, prior premenstrual disorder was present in 2.9% of perinatal depression cases versus 0.6% of matched controls (OR 4.76, 4.52–5.01), and the relationship is bidirectional, perinatal depression predicting later premenstrual disorder at hazard ratio 1.81 (1.74–1.88) over a mean 7.40 years, with sibling comparison attenuating but not abolishing both directions. Extending to the full perinatal spectrum across 1,052,977 women, prior premenstrual disorder raised first-onset perinatal bipolar disorder (adjusted OR 3.98, 3.15–5.04) and perinatal depression (2.74, 2.56–2.94), with no association for perinatal psychosis and stronger effects in women without prior psychiatric history. Management in pregnancy itself is an evidence vacuum by construction: PMDD is ovulation-dependent and remits with anovulation, there are no trials of PMDD treatment in pregnancy, and the live clinical question is prophylaxis against a perinatal episode. No trial has tested whether treating PMDD before or between pregnancies reduces subsequent perinatal depression, despite register-level odds ratios of 2.7–4.8.

Medical comorbidity is dominated by one shared mechanism. Catamenial epilepsy is the neurological mirror of PMDD: perimenstrual seizure exacerbation is a neurosteroid-withdrawal phenomenon driven by the same late-luteal fall in allopregnanolone plus a shift in GABA-A subunit composition from neurosteroid-sensitive δ-containing receptors toward benzodiazepine-insensitive α4γ2 assemblies. Animal work shows allopregnanolone's anticonvulsant potency rising 34–127% after neurosteroid withdrawal while diazepam and valproate potency falls or is unchanged, and low-dose fluoxetine given only in the late luteal window raises brain allopregnanolone and blocks withdrawal-induced hyperexcitability — the clearest mechanistic bridge between the two conditions, and one that no trial has crossed. Clinically, strictly defined catamenial epilepsy is uncommon: 5 of 40 women (12.5%) when defined as at least 75% of seizures in a ten-day perimenstrual window, against 78% who claimed the pattern. Three catamenial patterns are described — perimenstrual from day −3 to +3, periovulatory from day 10 to day −13, and an anovulatory luteal pattern from day 10 to +3 — and adjunctive progesterone, negative on the primary comparison of a national trial, carried class 3 evidence for benefit in the robustly perimenstrually exacerbated subtype only. Elsewhere, each 1 kg/m² of body mass index raised incident premenstrual syndrome risk by 3% (95% CI 1.01–1.05) in a large prospective cohort, while there is no literature at all on bariatric surgery and premenstrual disorders. Post-hysterectomy and post-oophorectomy outcomes rest on uncontrolled series of 47 and 40 women reporting 93.6% resolution and near-universal satisfaction, which is low-quality evidence by any standard.

Transgender and gender-diverse menstruators are a genuine literature absence rather than a sparse literature: a 2025 commentary argues that PMDD research has excluded them, and there is no prevalence estimate, cohort, trial or case series, and nothing on how testosterone therapy or menstrual suppression modifies premenstrual mood in this group. Cultural variation is similarly unresolvable from current data, because no standardised multi-country study using identical prospective methods exists; the three- to tenfold range across single-country screener studies is confounded by instrument and sampling frame, so only differences in symptom attribution and help-seeking are documented, and even those only narratively.

## Prognosis

Prognosis is the most quantitatively secure part of the chapter and the news is not reassuring. PMDD is chronic: fewer than 10% of community cases achieved complete remission over 48 months, and across 13 years in 9,671 Australian women the probability of reporting premenstrual syndrome given three prior consecutive reports was 0.75 (95% CI 0.73–0.76), with latent trajectory analysis identifying a chronic group of 29.5%, a recovering group of 26.5%, a late-onset group of 21.9% and a normative group of 22.1%.

Relapse after treatment withdrawal is fast and common. In the definitive 18-month randomised discontinuation study of 174 patients, relapse occurred in 60% after four months of luteal sertraline versus 41% after twelve (hazard ratio 0.58, 95% CI 0.34–0.98, p = 0.04), with median time to relapse four versus eight months; baseline symptom severity predicted relapse (hazard ratio 2.02, 1.18–3.41) and achieving full remission protected against it (0.22, 0.10–0.45). Pooled trial data show symptoms returning within the first cycle after fluoxetine cessation. Acute response is itself incomplete, with response rates across all modalities plateauing below 60% and roughly a third to two-fifths of treated women never reaching responder criteria. Predictors of poor outcome are baseline severity, failure to reach remission, psychiatric comorbidity, childhood trauma and pre-existing anxiety — the same variables that predict incident disease.

Mortality carries a crucial nuance. Across 67,748 Swedish women with premenstrual disorders matched to 338,740 controls, all-cause mortality was *not* elevated (adjusted hazard ratio 0.91, 95% CI 0.82–1.02), but death from non-natural causes was (1.59, 1.25–2.04) and suicide specifically (1.92, 1.43–2.60). Women diagnosed before age 25 were the exception on every count, with elevated all-cause mortality (2.51, 1.42–4.42) and suicide (3.84, 1.18–12.45). Injury risk follows the same shape — any injury 1.37, suicidal behaviour 2.26, accidents 1.32 — and is largest within two years of diagnosis. Cardiovascular risk is modestly raised (1.11, 1.08–1.13; 1.10 in sibling comparison) and again concentrated in early-onset disease. These cohorts rely on diagnostic codes and dispensed medication, so they capture only clinically presenting cases; no mortality or suicide-outcome study has used prospectively confirmed PMDD, and no study has tested whether treating the disorder reduces attempts, injury or the early-onset mortality excess.

Functional outcome is durably impaired. The quality-of-life decrement of 0.21 standard deviations cross-sectionally and 0.34 prospectively survives removal of the anxiety, depression and pain dimensions, so it is not simply a restatement of mood. PMDD produced the largest presenteeism and absenteeism effects among menstruation-related conditions in 4,818 workers, with a population-attributable fraction near 15% for severe work-functioning impairment. Aggregated across a reproductive lifespan of roughly 481 cycles at some 6.4 severely symptomatic days each, the disorder accounts for more than 3,000 symptomatic days — on the order of eight years — a figure worth stating to patients who have been told their condition is minor.

## Controversies & future directions

The guideline landscape is small, specialty-fractured and internally inconsistent in ways that matter clinically. Only two full college guidelines exist in English: a Green-top guideline whose second edition dates from 2017, and a 2023 clinical practice guideline built on a modified evidence-to-decision framework with a literature search restricted to 2000 through March 2021 and to countries ranked very high on the human development index. Between them sit four international consensus statements — on definition and trial design, on management, and on auditable standards. There has never been a full national clinical guideline on premenstrual syndrome from the UK national institute; its coverage is a knowledge summary that is geo-restricted and could not be read for this review, so nothing is asserted here about its content. Outside the Anglosphere the picture thins sharply and is reachable only through secondary description: the Japanese obstetric guideline permits Kampo medicines and diuretics alongside conventional therapy, and a 2025 Korean medication algorithm offers expert-consensus recommendations precisely because its authors judge the evidence base too thin for an evidence-based algorithm.

Where the guidelines agree, they agree strongly — prospective daily recording over two cycles, SSRIs first-line for affective symptoms, rejection of progesterone and progestogens, GnRH agonists with add-back reserved for refractory disease, and oophorectomy conditional on a prior agonist trial. The disagreements are more instructive, because the three schemes are not the same object: one treats premenstrual syndrome as the entity and PMDD as its severe psychological subtype, another treats premenstrual disorders as a spectrum in which PMDD is a psychiatric depressive disorder, and the consensus taxonomy is a third framework encompassing exacerbation, non-ovulatory, progestogen-induced and amenorrhoeic variants that no college operationalises into an algorithm. Grades diverge on identical data. A strong recommendation for combined oral contraceptives is issued on explicitly low-quality evidence by a document that concedes in the same breath that they were not superior to placebo for depressive premenstrual symptoms (0.22, 95% CI −0.06 to 0.47); the other college grades the same class B. A strong recommendation for cognitive behavioural therapy rests on five therapy trials inside a nine-trial meta-analysis whose largest anxiety estimate came from two trials totalling 51 participants. Complementary therapies split the two colleges outright: one keeps vitamin B6 on its first-line ladder while the other makes no recommendation; one rates chasteberry positively while the other declines to recommend it; one conditionally recommends acupuncture on a single 67-participant trial that the other does not address.

Regulation diverges across the Atlantic more sharply than any guideline does, and one college guideline is simply wrong about it. In the United States three SSRIs — fluoxetine, sertraline and paroxetine controlled-release — and drospirenone-containing oral contraceptives carry PMDD indications; the sertraline label rests on two trials of 251 and 281 randomised participants over three cycles, in women whose mean symptom duration was 10.5 years, with efficacy beyond three cycles never systematically evaluated, and the contraceptive indication is restricted to women who are choosing an oral contraceptive for contraception anyway. In March 2024 the original branded fluoxetine-for-PMDD application was withdrawn at the sponsor's own request because the product was no longer marketed — an administrative action, not a safety or efficacy one, leaving the indication alive on other fluoxetine products. Europe's position is the mirror image and is documented in the labels themselves: the UK fluoxetine summary of product characteristics lists only major depressive episodes, obsessive-compulsive disorder and bulimia nervosa, and describes the two PMDD trials in its pharmacodynamics section only to conclude that definitive conclusions on efficacy and duration of treatment cannot be drawn; the UK label for the same drospirenone 24/4 product marketed with a PMDD indication in the United States gives its sole indication as oral contraception. The 2017 Green-top guideline asserts that the pill is licensed in Europe for PMDD. The product labels show that is not so — a factual error sitting inside a current college guideline.

The medicalisation critique has not been answered so much as partly outflanked. Its strongest form holds that PMDD pathologises intelligible anger and distress, that it is culture-bound rather than universal, and that biological correlates, monthly periodicity, apparent drug response and the female preponderance of affective diagnoses jointly establish nothing about disorder status; its policy conclusion is that PMDD belongs in neither classification and that the remedy for disbelieved suffering is changed social attitudes rather than a diagnostic label. The branding of fluoxetine under a separate trade name for PMDD is the case most often cited as evidence that the category served a commercial function. A narrative review enumerated 38 distinct objections across 55 articles, six recurring five or more times, and reported that historical and legal trends do not support the alleged use of premenstrual diagnoses to exclude women from work or to defeat them in custody disputes. The culture-bound claim has fared worst empirically: a three-centre study of 537 adolescents and young women found overall prevalence of 4.1% with no significant difference between Maiduguri (6.5%), Lagos (3.1%) and Akron, Ohio (2.9%), and pooled low- and middle-income-country prevalence of 3.7–11% equals or exceeds high-income estimates of 3–4.1%. Much of this literature nonetheless sits in journals that biomedical databases do not index, so the strongest versions of the critique are under-represented in any review built from them, including this one.

The prospective-rating controversy is the one most damaging to routine practice, because the field's own auditable standard demands that 100% of referred women have the diagnosis confirmed by at least two months of prospective diary, and actual adherence runs between 6.6% and 11.5% across surveyed clinician populations. That gap, not the medicalisation argument, is what makes most PMDD diagnosed in ordinary care not PMDD. The categorical-versus-dimensional question remains formally open — no taxometric or latent-structure study of PMDD exists — though the diagnostic dimensions were normally distributed in the standardisation sample, a subthreshold band of 10–11% sits immediately below the cut, and the consensus group itself describes PMDD as possibly an extreme variant of a physiological influence of sex steroids on the brain, which is a dimensional claim embedded in the document defending the category. A 2025 editorial argues the categorical nosology should give way to a transdiagnostic, dimensional framing that treats cyclical worsening as a modifier applicable across disorders rather than as a disorder of its own — which would dissolve the PMDD-versus-exacerbation boundary that currently strands an estimated 50–60% of women with chronic mood disorders in diagnostic limbo.

The therapeutic pipeline is best read as one mechanistic argument tested in three directions, surrounded by a conspicuous emptiness. Sepranolone — isoallopregnanolone, the 3β-hydroxy epimer of allopregnanolone and a GABA-A modulating steroid antagonist — is the only molecule ever developed specifically for PMDD, and its trajectory is the classic one. A phase I/II study enrolling 26 healthy women and 126 with PMDD, of whom 106 completed, found a significant advantage on total daily symptom score (p = 0.041) with only borderline separation on negative mood (p = 0.051); the widely quoted 75%-versus-47% reduction at an effect size of 0.7 (p = 0.006) comes from a post-hoc subgroup of 60, reached by excluding 19 women with follicular-phase symptoms suggesting another condition and 27 not dosed as intended. The phase IIb randomised 206 women across 12 European centres and failed its prespecified primary endpoint outright, all three arms improving substantially and none separating from placebo; distress did separate (p = 0.037) and a post-hoc per-protocol analysis over a nine-day rather than five-day window in cycle 3 favoured the 10 mg arm (p = 0.008). Tolerability was clean throughout. The sponsor's own behaviour is the most informative datum on current status: the registry shows no PMDD trial since, and the compound was run in parallel in menstrual migraine from 2019 — before the PMDD readout, so not a response to its failure — and afterwards in Tourette syndrome from 2022, with no results posted for either.

Dutasteride attacks the same node one step upstream and remains the cleanest experimental demonstration that the luteal allopregnanolone rise is causal rather than correlated; it also remains eight treated patients, unreplicated more than a decade on, with a five-week half-life and male-fetal teratogenicity that make it a probe rather than a product. Ulipristal acetate takes the third route, removing the substrate by antagonising the progesterone receptor while leaving estradiol at mid-follicular levels, and its proof-of-concept trial is the strongest single positive result in the modern literature: in 95 women on 5 mg/day for three cycles, premenstrual symptom improvement was 41% (SD 18) versus 22% (27), a difference of −18% (95% CI −29 to −8), with depressive subscale 42% versus 22%, anger and irritability 47% versus 23%, no effect on physical symptoms, and remission in 20 of 40 (50.0%) versus 8 of 38 (21.1%) — a derived number needed to treat near 3.5, with anovulation in most participants. A narrative review restates that roughly 85% reached complete or partial remission, but partial remission is undefined in the primary trial and that figure does not appear in it. The obstacle is hepatic: the European regulator suspended the drug for fibroids in 2020 and restricted long-term use in 2021 after cases including acute liver failure requiring transplantation. A claims cohort of 12,166 users against 36,498 controls found only subclinical enzyme rises and no severe hepatotoxicity, but a 95-patient three-month trial is orders of magnitude underpowered for an event this rare, and that is the governing safety fact.

Brexanolone and zuranolone occupy the strangest position of all: they are the field's flagship neuroactive-steroid antidepressants and there is not a single registered trial of either in PMDD anywhere. Their postpartum data are solid — zuranolone 30 mg gave a day-15 depression-scale difference of −4.2 (95% CI −6.9 to −1.5) with response and remission odds ratios of 2.63 and 2.53 and numbers needed to treat of 5 for each, and 50 mg gave −4.0 (−6.3 to −1.7) in 196 women; brexanolone's phase 3 trials gave 60-hour differences of −5.5 (−8.8 to −2.2), −3.7 (−6.9 to −0.5) and −2.5 (−4.5 to −0.5). The mechanistic tension is the point of the whole chapter: these drugs *add* allopregnanolone tone while sepranolone and dutasteride *remove* it, and both cannot be right about a level. They can both be right about plasticity — that PMDD is a failure to re-tune GABA-A subunit composition to a *change* in neurosteroid concentration rather than sensitivity to a concentration — and that is precisely the hypothesis a newly launched phase 4 trial of sertraline against placebo, probing allopregnanolone and subunit plasticity in 288 women, is built to test. It reads out in 2029. A 2025 editorial has formally called for zuranolone to be studied in PMDD; nothing has been registered.

Ketamine, esketamine and psilocybin in PMDD are empty sets rather than thin ones — no trial, no case series, no controlled human data of any kind. The psychedelic literature comprises two uncontrolled surveys, the better of which reports a defined PMDD subsample of 167 with provider diagnosis by two cycles of daily tracking and a validated screening instrument, finding psychedelic use to manage premenstrual mood symptoms in 5.9% overall and 16.2% within PMDD, significantly more in the luteal than the follicular phase (p < 0.005); the other is an LSD microdosing survey reporting "pre-menstrual syndromes" with no denominator. The registry sweep found exactly one registered psychedelic trial, a three-arm study of low-dose LSD in 150 women reading out in 2029.

The registry sweep is itself a finding. Of completed registered PMDD and premenstrual syndrome studies, 90.9% have posted no results, so the true denominator of failed trials is unknowable — and the largest planned PMDD trial ever registered, an intranasal agent with a target enrolment of 1,400, has a status of unknown with an expected completion date nine years past and nothing published. Two industry phase 3 trials of continuous levonorgestrel–ethinylestradiol enrolling 744 and 526 women are complete with no results posted; the 744-participant trial is the largest completed PMDD drug trial in existence and nobody knows what it found. A 187-participant trial of the drospirenone 24/4 product in Chinese patients is likewise complete and silent. The sweep also systematically undercounts European academic work, because the ulipristal trial is registered only in the European register and is invisible to it. Active work is small and mechanistic: the sertraline plasticity trial and an epigenetic biomarker study of 500 participants reading out in 2030, an emotion-regulation internet therapy trial of 164 reading out in 2028, an acceptance and commitment therapy trial of 50 that is the only registered study naming premenstrual exacerbation explicitly, and small neurostimulation feasibility studies.

What the field most needs is legible from the gaps rather than from the pipeline. There is no genome-wide association study of PMDD, no twin study of the prospectively confirmed phenotype, and no ligand that would let anyone test the subunit-plasticity hypothesis in a living human brain. There is no head-to-head trial of any novel agent against an SSRI or a drospirenone-containing pill, so the field cannot say whether any new mechanism beats a cheap generic. There is no operational definition of premenstrual exacerbation, no guideline algorithm for it, and essentially no trial that has enrolled it — which leaves the commonest presentation in psychiatry without an evidence base. There is no validated digital pathway from app data to a diagnosis, despite charting completion of 45–56% being the rate-limiting step in every clinic. No trial has tested whether treating PMDD reduces suicide attempts, injury, or the early-onset mortality excess, and none has evaluated any intervention for suicidality in this disorder at all. And there is no adolescent trial of anything, in a condition that begins in adolescence and persists, by the best available estimate, for something close to eight cumulative years of symptomatic life.
