## 1. What "enhancement" means when there is nothing wrong

Neuroenhancement is the use of a drug, a device, or a training protocol by a person who has no
diagnosis, with the intention of pushing cognition, mood, or motivation above that person's own
baseline. The construct is defined by its *population*, not by its pharmacology: methylphenidate
prescribed to a 20-year-old with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder is treatment, and the
same 20 mg taken by their room-mate before an exam is enhancement. Nothing about the molecule
changes across that line. What changes is the reference point against which benefit is measured
— and with no deficit to correct, the reference point is the healthy human brain operating
normally, which turns out to be a much harder target than a damaged one.

This is why the field belongs to psychiatry rather than to sports medicine or to consumer
technology. Every agent in serious use is a psychiatric or neurological drug used off-label, an
investigational psychiatric intervention repurposed, or a device developed for psychiatric
neuromodulation. The people using them are, by definition, not patients, so they are invisible
to clinical services until something goes wrong; and the harms that do occur — dependence,
diversion, sleep disruption, anxiety — are psychiatric harms. More than 1.5 million US adults
were taking medications labelled for ADHD at the time of the largest cardiovascular safety
cohort, and a substantial and unmeasured fraction of that supply is diverted to people without
the diagnosis.

Three distinctions organise the rest of the chapter, because collapsing any of them is how the
literature has repeatedly confused itself:

**Enhancement versus deficit correction.** An agent that lifts a depleted system back to
normal is not an enhancer, even though the measured change looks identical on a task. Caffeine
in a caffeine-withdrawn drinker, sleep in a sleep-deprived student, and vitamin D in a
deficient older adult all produce real improvements that vanish in a replete population. Most
of what circulates as evidence of enhancement is evidence of deficit correction, and separating
them requires a study design most of the literature does not use.

**Efficiency versus capability.** A drug that lets someone reach their existing ceiling faster,
more reliably, or with less effort is doing something different from a drug that raises the
ceiling. Almost everything with positive evidence is in the first category, and the marketing
of every product in the field implies the second.

**Objective performance versus the experience of performing.** These dissociate, sharply and
reproducibly, and the dissociation is the single most clinically useful fact in the chapter
(§4). Users are not lying about feeling sharper. They are reporting a real subjective effect
that is largely uncoupled from what a task measures.

The boundary case that keeps recurring is *sub-threshold pathology*. Many self-identified
enhancement users have undiagnosed ADHD, chronic sleep restriction, an anxiety disorder, or a
depressive syndrome, and for them the intervention is treatment that has been misrouted. This
is dealt with as a differential-diagnosis problem in §6, because that is what it is.

## 2. Who is actually doing this, and how much

Prevalence estimates for pharmacological neuroenhancement span from under 1% to over 20%
depending on the population sampled, the anonymity of the survey, the time window, and — most
of all — which substances the investigators decided to count. That spread is not measurement
noise; it is the definitional problem of §1 expressed as an epidemiological artefact. A survey
that counts caffeine, guarana, and energy drinks finds a majority of students to be users. A
survey restricted to unprescribed methylphenidate, amphetamine, and modafinil finds a few
percent in the same populations.

The most useful figures are therefore the ones that report both tiers from the same sample. In a
German multistudy report, occupational physicians estimated the prevalence of neuroenhancement
in the working population at 10.9%, while the student sample in the same programme reported
5.4% for prescription and illicit substances against 76.8% for "soft" enhancers, overwhelmingly
caffeine. A cross-sectional online survey of the University of Mainz reached 4,351 of 31,213
registered students, of whom 3,984 answered the enhancement item, and found a 12-month
prevalence of 10.4% for having used at least one substance for enhancement — a figure that sits
between the two tiers because the item was worded to capture both. In a Norwegian two-wave
survey (9,370 respondents at wave 1, 4,783 at wave 2), the lifetime prevalence of stimulant
enhancement was 2.1% rising to 3.6% across one year, with depressant enhancement rising from
1.5% to 3.3% — a reminder that the phenomenon is not exclusively stimulant-shaped and that a
meaningful minority are chemically managing performance *anxiety* rather than attention.

Two recent datasets complicate the picture in opposite directions. A registry-based,
design-weighted national survey of Lebanese pharmacy students (438 of 1,003 eligible, response
rate 43.7%) found a weighted 12-month prevalence of non-medical prescription cognitive-enhancer
use of 28.1% (95% CI 23.7–32.9), rising to 54.6% for a composite that included supplements taken
with explicit cognitive intent. Strikingly, the leading agents were not the classic stimulants:
donepezil at 12.6% and racetams at 11.3% both exceeded methylphenidate at 10.5%. Advanced
academic year (adjusted OR 2.12, 95% CI 1.41–3.18) and lower GPA (<3.2 versus ≥3.6, adjusted OR
1.83, 95% CI 1.15–2.92) independently predicted use — the second of which is the tell that this
is compensatory behaviour rather than optimisation by the already-excelling. In Japanese
undergraduates surveyed across 2017–2019, by contrast, almost no student had ever used a
prescription drug for enhancement, and only 2.5–4.8% said they would want to, while 68.6–72.0%
said they would not; about half had nonetheless used energy drinks before an examination. The
comparison makes the point that prescription-stimulant enhancement is not a universal
consequence of academic pressure but a phenomenon of particular drug-supply and prescribing
ecologies, layered on top of a near-universal caffeine substrate.

Predictors are consistent across samples and unflattering to the "rational optimiser" model. In
the Mainz cohort, 13 variables predicted 12-month enhancement, with the health-behaviour block
showing the strongest relationship — enhancement clusters with other substance use, not with
conscientious self-improvement. A cross-lagged analysis in the German multistudy report found
that stress predicted higher subsequent enhancement (β = 0.179, p < 0.001) and resilience
predicted lower (β = −0.13, p = 0.001), while enhancement itself predicted *higher* subsequent
stress (β = 0.11, p < 0.001). The direction of that last coefficient is the finding: over time,
this is a coping strategy that raises the load it was adopted to manage.

## 3. Why an intact system has so little headroom

The neurobiology explains the effect sizes in §7 better than any account of study quality does.

**The inverted U.** Catecholaminergic signalling in prefrontal cortex has an optimum, not a
monotonic dose–response. Performance rises with prefrontal dopamine and noradrenaline up to a
peak and falls beyond it. A person whose signalling sits left of the peak — because of ADHD,
fatigue, low baseline working-memory capacity, or a genotype that clears synaptic dopamine
quickly — is moved toward the optimum by a stimulant. A person already at or right of the peak is
moved past it. Group means in a healthy sample therefore average an enhancement subgroup against
an impairment subgroup, and the pooled estimate lands near zero not because nothing happened but
because opposite things happened to different people.

**Baseline dependency, demonstrated directly.** The cleanest human demonstration in recent
literature comes from the psychedelic side. In a randomised placebo-controlled study of repeated
15 µg LSD in 53 healthy participants across four dosing sessions over two weeks, stimulatory
effects — reduced resting-state EEG delta, theta and alpha power, enhanced pre-attentive
processing on an auditory oddball task — were strongest in individuals with *low* arousal and
attention at baseline, while inhibitory effects, including blunted induction of visual long-term
potentiation at the fourth dosing session, were strongest in those who were the *best* memory
performers at baseline. One compound, one dose, opposite signs, sorted by where the person
started. Decrements in delta power and enhanced pre-attentive processing were still present at
one-week follow-up, which also shows that "acute effect" is the wrong frame for repeated dosing.

**Mechanism is not specificity.** Modafinil directly raises cortical catecholamines and
indirectly raises serotonin, glutamate, orexin and histamine while lowering GABA — a
pharmacological profile broad enough that "which cognitive domain should improve" is
underdetermined by the mechanism. Its enantiomer chemistry underlines how close it sits to the
classical stimulants: R-modafinil binds the dopamine transporter and inhibits dopamine uptake
with roughly three-fold higher affinity than the S-enantiomer, less potently than cocaine but by
the same mechanism, raising extracellular dopamine in the nucleus accumbens shell with a longer
duration of action, and both enantiomers fully substitute for cocaine in mice trained to
discriminate it from saline. The pharmacology of a "non-addictive wakefulness agent" is the
pharmacology of a weak, slow dopamine-uptake inhibitor.

**Enhancement is a trade, not a gift.** Because the mechanisms are global and the systems are
tuned, gains in one function are routinely paid for in another. The clearest documented case is
divergent thinking: several studies in the modafinil systematic review reported *impairment* of
divergent creative thinking alongside improvements in executive function. In the sub-acute LSD
crossover study (50 µg, 24 healthy volunteers, placebo-controlled, BMI-corrected), the same
24-hour window showed improved visuospatial memory and phonological verbal fluency alongside
impaired cognitive flexibility on the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test — fewer categories achieved,
more perseveration and more errors. The authors named the pattern "afterglow and hangover" in
the same subject, on the same day.

**Complexity of the probe determines the answer.** In the modafinil review, basic testing
paradigms showed enhanced executive function but improvement in attention and learning in only
about half of studies, whereas more complex assessments showed consistent enhancement of
attention, executive function, and learning. This is not a nuisance: it means the size of a
reported effect is partly a property of the test's ceiling, and that batteries designed to detect
impairment in ill populations are the wrong instrument for detecting gain in well ones. A
double-blind, placebo-controlled single-dose crossover of modafinil 200 mg run in parallel across
46 people with schizophrenia of under three years' duration and 28 matched healthy volunteers
made the same point experimentally by deliberately varying the battery: which effects appeared
depended on whether the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery or the CANTAB was used.

## 4. What an enhancement actually feels like, and what it actually does

The signature clinical finding in this field is a dissociation between the subjective and the
objective, and it is large.

In a study of 96 undergraduates selected for risk factors for stimulant misuse — low GPA,
fraternity or sorority involvement, binge drinking, cannabis use — 47 were randomised to ingest
what they were told was methylphenidate but which was placebo, on one of two visits, with the
remainder receiving nothing on either visit. On the administration visit, participants who
believed they had taken methylphenidate reported feeling significantly more "high" and more
stimulated than on their non-administration visit and than controls. Cognitive performance did
not differ between visits or between groups. The subjective enhancement was complete and the
objective enhancement was absent, in the same people, in the same hour.

This is what makes user testimony systematically unreliable in this domain, and it is not a moral
observation about users. It is a statement about which effects are salient. Stimulants reliably
change arousal, energy, mood and task-related motivation; those changes are consciously
accessible. The cognitive change, where it exists, is a shift of about a fifth to two-fifths of a
standard deviation in a specific sub-domain, detectable by a laboratory task averaged over dozens
of trials and completely inaccessible to introspection. A person who feels transformed and
performs the same is having both experiences accurately.

Two corollaries matter clinically. First, the authors of the largest stimulant meta-analysis
concluded explicitly that healthy users may be resorting to stimulants to enhance energy and
motivation more than cognition — which, if true, means the honest description of the intervention
is a mood-and-drive drug being sold as a cognition drug. Second, and more dangerous, the
subjective effect can move in the *opposite* direction from performance: repeated doses of
modafinil across an extended period of sleep deprivation failed to prevent the deterioration of
cognitive performance while maintaining wakefulness, and possibly induced overconfidence in one's
own cognitive performance. An agent that keeps a person awake, lets their accuracy decay, and
raises their confidence in that decayed accuracy is a specific and predictable safety hazard in
exactly the settings — night shifts, sustained operations, long drives — where it is most used.

On the affective side, the acute phenomenology of low-dose psychedelics is now well characterised
and modest. Acute microdoses of LSD dose-dependently alter blood pressure, sleep, neural
connectivity, social cognition, mood, and the perception of pain and time; perceptible drug
effects appear at 10–20 µg but not at 5 µg. What does *not* appear is durable mood change from
repeated dosing (§7.5).

## 5. Measuring an enhancement — why this literature is hard to read

Emphasis in this chapter is on interventions, but the interventions cannot be read without the
measurement problems, because in several cases the measurement problem *is* the result.

**Effect sizes are small and domain-specific, so the analysis choices dominate.** The definitive
pharmacological synthesis identified 47 studies and ran three separate meta-analyses: modafinil
(14 studies, 64 effect sizes), methylphenidate (24 studies, 47 effect sizes) and d-amphetamine
(10 studies, 27 effect sizes) in healthy non-sleep-deprived adults, with subgroup analysis by
cognitive domain. Overall effects were SMD 0.12 (p = 0.01) for modafinil and SMD 0.21
(p = 0.0004) for methylphenidate, and none at all for d-amphetamine. At that magnitude, a
different domain taxonomy or a different inclusion rule changes the headline.

**Publication bias is not hypothetical here; it has been measured and it bites.** In a
meta-analysis of 48 studies and 1,409 participants, prescription stimulants produced small but
significant enhancement of inhibitory control and short-term episodic memory, a small effect on
working memory that reached significance under one of two analytical approaches, and a
medium-sized effect on delayed episodic memory — but the long-term-memory and working-memory
effects were both qualified by evidence of publication bias, leading the authors to downgrade the
overall conclusion to "probably modest". On the device side, a comprehensive meta-analysis of 31
articles found that anodal tDCS over left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex enhanced working-memory
performance, and that this effect was reduced to non-significance after correction for
publication bias. The only effect that survived was stimulation *coupled with* working-memory
training. A meta-analysis of 75 effect sizes from 45 studies of tDCS and inhibitory control found
a small but significant overall effect of g = 0.21 that was further reduced after correction for
publication bias. Three independent literatures, three corrections, three shrinkages.

**Blinding fails in a specific, fixable way.** Transcranial direct current stimulation produces
erythema under the electrodes, which unblinds participants. In 30 healthy volunteers receiving
30 minutes of 2 mA stimulation with anode over F3 and cathode over F4 in a Latin-square design,
erythema was mild but real, lasted 18–24 minutes after stimulation ended, and was more intense
under the anode; topical ketoprofen 2% significantly attenuated it while hydroxyzine and lidocaine
did not. Any sham-controlled tDCS trial that did not manage this has an integrity problem, and
that includes most of the trials in the meta-analyses above.

**The tests were built to detect loss, not gain.** This is the recurring methodological complaint
of the modafinil literature, and the reason its authors closed with an explicit set of
recommendations for detecting valid effects in healthy populations. A battery with a ceiling that
a healthy graduate student already reaches cannot show enhancement no matter what the drug does.

**Practice effects and design.** Where within-subject crossover designs are used without adequate
washout and counterbalancing, learning across sessions is confounded with drug effect; where
between-subject designs are used, the small true effects require sample sizes this literature
almost never has. The single-session, n = 20–40 crossover is the modal study in the whole field.

**A working reading rule.** For any claim in this domain, ask in order: was the comparison
population replete or depleted; was the control an active placebo or a resting baseline; was the
outcome an objective task or a self-report; and was the effect robust to publication-bias
correction. Most positive claims fail at least one.

## 6. The differential — what enhancement is being mistaken for

Because the population is by definition undiagnosed, the most important clinical act is
distinguishing enhancement from four other things.

**Undiagnosed ADHD.** The predictor pattern in §2 — lower GPA, later academic year, escalating
use — is equally consistent with a group of students with untreated attention disorder
self-medicating with borrowed medication. There is no way to separate these from survey data,
and the practical implication is that a patient presenting with "I use my friend's Adderall to
study" is a diagnostic opportunity, not merely a risk behaviour.

**Chronic sleep restriction.** Sleep debt is the single most prevalent cognitive impairment in
the healthy adult population, and the intervention with the best evidence in this whole chapter
targets it directly (§7.7). A stimulant used to offset sleep loss is treating a deficit and is
described by the pharmacology of §4 — wakefulness maintained, accuracy decaying, confidence
rising.

**Caffeine withdrawal.** Habitual caffeine consumers are in mild withdrawal each morning, and a
dose that restores them to baseline looks in any naive design like enhancement. When 96
participants alternated weekly between placebo and caffeine 1.75 mg/kg three times daily across
four weeks, in both rested and sleep-restricted conditions, caffeine produced no significant net
enhancement of performance or mood when participants were rested, and no net restorative effect
when performance and mood had been degraded by sleep restriction. The methodological literature
is explicit that recruiting "caffeine-naive" participants does not solve this, because such
participants are unrepresentative and tolerance confounds remain.

**Anxiety and mood disorder.** The Norwegian data — depressant enhancement rising from 1.5% to
3.3% lifetime, predicted by high anxiety and high conscientiousness — describes people managing
performance anxiety pharmacologically. That is a treatable anxiety presentation wearing an
enhancement label.

The genuine co-travellers are also worth naming. Enhancement use clusters with alcohol use, with
other prescription misuse, and with a general inclination toward substance use rather than with
academic ambition; and enhancement, stress and low resilience form a reinforcing loop over time
(§2).

## 7. The interventions

This is where the locked emphasis sits. Each modality is reported with what has actually been
tested in people without a diagnosis, at what effect size, with what confidence.

### 7.1 Eugeroics and prescription stimulants

**Modafinil.** The pooled estimate across 14 studies and 64 effect sizes in healthy
non-sleep-deprived adults is SMD 0.12 (p = 0.01) — statistically present, clinically negligible —
with the only significant domain-level effect being memory updating at SMD 0.28 (p = 0.03). The
narrative systematic review of the same literature reaches a compatible but more textured
conclusion: enhancement of executive function in most studies using basic paradigms, improvement
of attention and of learning and memory in only about half, impairment of divergent creative
thinking in a few, and consistent enhancement of attention, executive function and learning when
the assessments are complex. No preponderance of side effects or mood changes was observed in
that review, which is the strongest safety statement anywhere in this chapter and is limited to
single doses in laboratory conditions.

The one place modafinil clearly earns its reputation is sleep deprivation, and even there the
claim needs the caveat of §4: modafinil maintains wakefulness, memory and executive function to a
significantly greater degree than placebo in sleep-deprived individuals, but repeated doses over
a longer period of deprivation do not prevent performance deterioration and may induce
overconfidence. Modafinil in a rested person is a marginal cognitive intervention; in a
sleep-deprived person it is a wakefulness drug with a cognitive side-benefit and a calibration
hazard.

**Methylphenidate.** The pooled estimate across 24 studies and 47 effect sizes is SMD 0.21
(p = 0.0004), the largest in the pharmacological literature, and it is driven by three domains:
recall (SMD 0.43, p = 0.0002), sustained attention (SMD 0.42, p = 0.0004) and inhibitory control
(SMD 0.27, p = 0.03). The independent 48-study, 1,409-participant meta-analysis of methylphenidate
and amphetamine together found small but significant effects on inhibitory control and short-term
episodic memory, a medium effect on delayed episodic memory, and a working-memory effect
significant under only one of two analytical approaches — with publication bias qualifying the
long-term and working-memory results. A third meta-analysis, targeting domains the first two did
not cover, found a positive effect of prescription stimulants on processing-speed accuracy at
g = 0.282 (95% CI 0.077–0.488, n = 345) and neither improvement nor impairment for planning time,
planning accuracy, advantageous decision-making, or cognitive perseveration.

Read together: methylphenidate produces a real, reproducible, small-to-moderate improvement in
recall, sustained attention, inhibitory control and processing-speed accuracy in healthy adults,
does not detectably damage planning or decision-making, and does not do anything that would
change an examination grade for a person who is sleeping and studying adequately.

**d-Amphetamine.** No effects, in any domain, across 10 studies and 27 effect sizes. This is the
most surprising single result in the chapter, given that amphetamine is the archetypal "study
drug", and it is a genuine null rather than an absence of evidence.

**Head-to-head, with caffeine as comparator.** A three-arm randomised placebo-controlled study in
male participants comparing caffeine, methylphenidate and modafinil across a multi-domain battery
found moderate effects overall and a clean dissociation by agent: methylphenidate improved
self-reported fatigue and declarative memory at 24 hours after learning, caffeine improved
sustained attention, and modafinil showed no significant effect in that cohort. Resting-state
fMRI in the same 48-participant cohort showed the stimulants modulated frontoparietal-to-default
mode network connectivity, with decreased negative connectivity between right prefrontal and
medial parietal regions predicting latent memory enhancement — a plausible mechanism for a real
but small effect.

**Registered pipeline.** Ongoing registered work on these agents in healthy people is close to
non-existent. A ClinicalTrials.gov sweep of recruiting, not-yet-recruiting and active trials of
modafinil, methylphenidate or lisdexamfetamine with a cognition condition returned 8 records, of
which 7 are in clinical populations — sickle cell disease, postural tachycardia syndrome,
cancer-related cognitive difficulty, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, early psychosis, Alzheimer
disease. The one that includes healthy participants does so as a comparison arm inside an early
psychosis programme.

### 7.2 Caffeine and nicotine — the two everyone actually uses

**Caffeine.** The base rate of enhancement behaviour in every population surveyed. The evidence
that it enhances a rested, non-withdrawn adult is much weaker than its ubiquity implies. The
withdrawal-reversal-controlled study described in §6 — 96 participants, four weeks, alternating
weekly placebo and caffeine 1.75 mg/kg three times daily, crossed with rested and sleep-restricted
conditions — found no significant net enhancement of performance or mood in rested participants
and no net restorative effect in sleep-restricted ones. Against that, caffeine improved sustained
attention in the three-arm head-to-head above. The reconciliation is that caffeine's acute effect
on vigilance in an acutely challenged state is real, and its net effect across a habitual
consumption cycle is close to nil, because the trough it fixes is the one it creates.

**Nicotine.** The most consistently positive small-molecule result in the field, and the one
nobody wants. A meta-analysis of 41 double-blind placebo-controlled studies in healthy adult
non-smokers or minimally deprived smokers found significant positive effects across six of nine
performance domains — fine motor, alerting attention accuracy and reaction time, orienting
attention reaction time, short-term episodic memory accuracy, and working-memory reaction time —
with effect sizes from 0.16 to 0.44, and the authors argued these represent true enhancement
rather than withdrawal relief because the samples were not tobacco-deprived. A later
meta-analysis restricted to transdermal delivery in healthy non-smokers, 31 publications and 978
subjects, found an overall cognitive effect of SMD 0.233 (95% CI 0.111–0.355, p < 0.001) and an
attention effect of SMD 0.231 (95% CI 0.106–0.356, p < 0.001), with memory not reaching
significance (SMD 0.270, 95% CI −0.293 to 0.833, p = 0.347). Nicotine's effect on attention is
about the same size as methylphenidate's, from a compound with an addiction liability that makes
it unusable as an enhancement strategy — which is precisely why it belongs in this chapter rather
than being quietly omitted.

### 7.3 Nutraceuticals — deferred, in one paragraph

This domain is covered in full in the companion vault artifact and is deliberately not
re-adjudicated here. The through-line established there holds and generalises to the rest of this
chapter: these compounds correct deficits and do not enhance an intact system. Every agent with a
positive pooled estimate owes it to a subgroup with a deficit — vitamin D in the deficient
(g = 0.480 versus 0.128 overall), omega-3 in those with low n-3 status, creatine in adults aged
66–76 (SMD 0.88) but not 11–31 (SMD 0.03) — and the agents with adequately powered trials in
replete populations return precise nulls. One adjacent line of evidence is worth flagging as
mechanistically interesting and clinically premature: a meta-analysis of 15 high-quality trials
(401 participants, mean PEDro 8.8) of polyphenol-rich supplementation in healthy adults under 60
found a significant increase in brain-derived neurotrophic factor alongside better serial-7s
subtraction, faster simple reaction time and reduced mental fatigue, mostly from acute rather than
chronic dosing. A biomarker moving is not a capability changing.

### 7.4 Transcranial electrical stimulation

The most commercially aggressive modality and the one with the least stable evidence base.

**Working memory.** The most careful and sympathetic meta-analysis of the enhancement question —
31 articles, four primary analyses covering anodal stimulation of left and right dorsolateral
prefrontal cortex, right parietal lobe, and left DLPFC coupled with working-memory training —
found a small but significant effect only for stimulation *coupled with training*. Left DLPFC
stimulation alone enhanced working memory until publication-bias correction, at which point it
was non-significant. No other effect, and no tested moderator, was significant. An earlier
meta-analysis restricted to n-back, Sternberg and digit-span tasks found in healthy cohorts a
trend toward improved offline accuracy (p = 0.05) and a small significant improvement in reaction
time (p = 0.04), with no significant online effects for either accuracy (p = 0.29) or reaction
time (p = 0.42).

**Other domains.** Across 61 single-session, sham-controlled, within-subject DLPFC studies,
healthy participants responded *faster* after anodal stimulation but not more accurately, while
neuropsychiatric patients responded more accurately — the same population asymmetry that runs
through this whole chapter. For inhibitory control, 75 effect sizes from 45 studies gave g = 0.21,
shrinking further after publication-bias correction, and moderated by electrode placement and by
task. For language, 11 experiments of single-session anodal tDCS in healthy adults produced a
significant effect (p = 0.001), with offline protocols (p = 0.002) and left-prefrontal verbal
fluency (p = 0.035) driving it.

**tACS.** The strongest quantitative claim in the neuromodulation literature comes from
transcranial alternating current stimulation: a meta-analysis of 102 studies, 2,893 individuals
and 304 effect sizes reported modest-to-moderate improvements across working memory, long-term
memory, attention, executive control and fluid intelligence, with offline effects generally
stronger than online, larger effects where current-flow modelling was used to optimise targeting,
and bidirectional effects depending on the relative phase of stimulation across two regions. That
last detail — that antiphase stimulation can *decrease* performance — is the most direct evidence
in the field that these are real physiological manipulations and not elaborate placebos.

**Older adults.** Thirteen studies of 532 healthy older adults (mean age 71.2 ± 5.3) yielded
Hedges' g values ranging from −0.31 to 1.85 for active versus sham — a range that includes
substantial harm and substantial benefit and therefore constitutes no usable estimate. A meta-
analysis of 31 studies focused on reaction time found small significant effects, present for
online but not offline protocols, and increasing with participant age. Six randomised controlled
trials (323 participants) of tDCS combined with cognitive training in healthy older adults gave
SMD 0.35 (95% CI 0.11–0.59, I² = 0%), rising to SMD 0.72 (95% CI 0.22–1.21) with ten or more
sessions and 0.39 (95% CI 0.08–0.70) at 2 mA. Once again the effect lives in the combination with
training, not in the current.

**Consumer devices and safety.** An international consensus statement on non-invasive brain
stimulation and neuroenhancement is unambiguous on three points: there is no area of the field in
which only positive outcomes have been reported; low-intensity transcranial electrical stimulation
is safe *as long as* tested protocols and established engineering risk-management procedures are
followed; and consumer devices marketed without medical claims are not necessarily subject to
medical-device regulation, so devices permitting stimulation outside studied parameters cannot
claim the safety record of the published literature. For a clinician asked about a home device,
that is the answer: the safety data belong to the protocol, not to the hardware.

**Registered pipeline.** A sweep of recruiting and not-yet-recruiting tES trials with a cognition
or healthy-ageing condition returned 60 records, of which the recruiting studies visible on the
first page are almost entirely clinical — mild cognitive impairment, multiple sclerosis,
schizophrenia, stroke, traumatic brain injury, Huntington disease, autism, COPD. Healthy-volunteer
enhancement studies exist but are small, single-site and mechanistic: 30 participants aged 18–35
for tDCS during a face–name associative memory task; 27 for dual-site stimulation of language and
cognition; 43 for cognition in healthy older adults; 15 for the development of a home-use device.
The field's registered activity is a clinical-translation pipeline with a mechanistic tail, not an
enhancement programme.

### 7.5 Psychedelic microdosing

The newest modality and, unexpectedly, one of the better-controlled ones, because the practice
outran the evidence so publicly that rigorous trials were built to catch up.

Fourteen studies meet the bar of double-blind, placebo-controlled, laboratory-setting,
investigator-supplied drug, and all of them involve acute or repeated low doses of LSD at
5–20 µg. Acute microdoses dose-dependently alter blood pressure, sleep, neural connectivity,
social cognition, mood, and the perception of pain and time. Perceptible drug effects occur at
10–20 µg but not at 5 µg. Repeated doses did not alter mood or cognition on any measure studied.
No serious adverse effects were reported.

Two trials carry the weight. In a double-blind study, 56 healthy adults were randomised to
placebo (n = 18), 13 µg LSD (n = 19) or 26 µg LSD (n = 19) and attended four five-hour dosing
sessions at 3–4 day intervals with a drug-free follow-up. The 26 µg dose produced modest subjective
effects — increased "feeling a drug effect", with both stimulant-like and LSD-like qualities — but
did not improve mood and did not affect psychomotor performance or most emotional tasks, and no
residual effects on mood or task performance were detectable at follow-up. Separately, a phase 1
randomised controlled trial gave 80 healthy adult male volunteers a six-week course of 10 µg LSD
or placebo self-administered every third day, with a commercial sleep and activity tracker worn
throughout. Across 3,231 nights of sleep, the LSD group slept an extra 24.3 minutes per night
(95% CI 10.3–38.3) on the night *after* dosing, with no reduction on the dosing day itself and no
change in sleep-stage proportions or physical activity. That is a small, clean, objectively
measured physiological effect that is difficult to explain as placebo — and it is a change in
sleep architecture requirement, not a cognitive enhancement.

The pharmacokinetic substudy of that trial modelled 10 µg sublingual LSD as one-compartment, with
elimination clearance 7.78 L/h/70 kg (95% CI 6.75–8.77), central volume of distribution
32.9 L/70 kg (30.1–36.0), maximal concentration 0.20 µg/L at 1.51 hours and an elimination
half-life of 3.08 hours. Maximal increases in heart rate and in the "feel effect" visual analogue
scale were under 15% above baseline. Four of the 80 participants withdrew because of anxiety, two
of whom had intermediate-to-weak CYP2D6 activity. No alteration in peripheral BDNF was detected —
which matters, because BDNF induction is the most-cited proposed mechanism for microdosing
benefit.

The residual positive signals are subtle and interesting rather than useful. Natural speech under
psilocybin microdoses (0.5 g of dried mushrooms, 34 healthy volunteers, double-blind
placebo-controlled, two measurement weeks each) differed from placebo in verbosity and sentiment
scores, and machine-learning classifiers distinguished the conditions with AUC ≥ 0.8 — the drug is
doing something detectable, just not something the person can use. At a larger, psycholytic
50 µg dose, the sub-acute profile 24 hours later was the mixed afterglow-and-hangover described in
§3.

The honest summary: microdosing produces measurable acute physiological and neural effects, does
not produce the mood, creativity or cognitive benefits claimed for it in the grey literature when
tested under blind conditions, and its most robust objective finding to date is that people sleep
about 24 minutes longer the following night.

### 7.6 Cognitive training

The oldest enhancement industry and the one whose failure mode is best documented: near transfer
is real, far transfer is not.

The foundational meta-analysis of 23 studies and 30 group comparisons across clinical and typically
developing samples found reliable short-term improvements in working-memory skill; for verbal
working memory these near-transfer effects were not sustained at follow-up, while for visuospatial
working memory limited evidence suggested they might be. There was no convincing evidence of
generalisation to non-verbal ability, verbal ability, inhibitory attention, word decoding or
arithmetic. The subsequent dispute over whether working-memory training raises fluid intelligence
is instructive precisely because both sides published their numbers: the original claim was
g = 0.24, and re-analysis accounting for baseline differences reduced it to a statistically
significant but considerably smaller g = 0.13, which lost significance once studies without active
control groups were excluded. Whatever one concludes about the disagreement, an effect that
survives only without active controls is not an effect anyone should buy a subscription for.

Commercial programmes have now been tested on their own terms. A meta-analysis of 43 studies and
2,636 participants across seven named products — BrainGymmer, BrainHQ, CogMed, CogniFit, Dakim,
Lumosity and MyBrainTrainer — in healthy older adults and those with mild cognitive impairment
found small significant near-transfer effects in both groups. Far transfer was absent in the MCI
sample; in the healthy sample it appeared for *subjective* but not objective measures. Domain
analyses showed significant transfer to executive functioning, memory and processing speed, and
after adjustment for publication bias only processing speed remained significant. The authors'
conclusion is worth carrying verbatim into clinical conversation: these programmes are suitable
for enjoyment and entertainment, and there is insufficient evidence that they improve memory,
general cognition or everyday functioning.

The subjective-but-not-objective far-transfer finding is the §4 dissociation appearing for a
third time, in a third modality.

### 7.7 Exercise and sleep — the interventions that work

The two modalities with the best evidence in this chapter are the two nobody markets as
neuroenhancement.

**Exercise.** A systematic review with multilevel meta-analysis screened 12,820 records, included
39 studies, and analysed 333 dependent effect sizes from 36 randomised controlled trials of
physical exercise in community-dwelling adults over 50. Exercise improved cognitive function with
an effect size of 0.29 (95% CI 0.17–0.41, p < 0.01). Aerobic exercise, resistance training,
multicomponent training and tai chi all had significant point estimates; sessions of 45–60 minutes
at least at moderate intensity were associated with benefit; and the result was independent of the
cognitive domain tested and of participants' cognitive status. That effect size is larger than
modafinil's, larger than every publication-bias-corrected tDCS estimate, and roughly comparable to
methylphenidate's — from an intervention with no diversion risk and a large set of collateral
benefits. The caveat is the age band: this is an over-50 evidence base, and the equivalent
literature in healthy young adults is thinner and more mechanistic, largely concerned with
biomarkers such as the acute BDNF rise after high-intensity exercise (pooled ES 0.55, 95% CI
0.12–0.98 versus non-exercise; 0.78, 95% CI 0.15–1.40 versus light-intensity exercise, across 22
studies and 552 individuals).

**Sleep extension.** In eleven healthy varsity basketball players followed through a 2–4 week
baseline and a 5–7 week extension period targeting a minimum of 10 hours in bed, objective nightly
sleep increased by 110.9 ± 79.7 minutes (p < 0.001). Timed sprint improved from 16.2 ± 0.61 to
15.5 ± 0.54 seconds (p < 0.001), free-throw percentage rose 9% and three-point percentage rose
9.2% (both p < 0.001), psychomotor vigilance reaction time and Epworth scores fell (p < 0.01), and
Profile of Mood States vigour rose while fatigue fell (p < 0.001). The sample is small,
uncontrolled and athletic, and the effect sizes still dwarf everything pharmacological in this
chapter. A review of the same literature confirms the pattern across reaction time, mood, sprint
times, serve accuracy and shooting accuracy, and describes the practice of "banking" sleep before
anticipated deprivation.

The interpretation that reconciles this with §1 is that these are not enhancers either — they are
deficit corrections in a population that is chronically deficient. The average adult is
under-slept and under-exercised, so the population-average effect of fixing that is large. This is
the same logic that explains why vitamin D works in the deficient and not in the replete. The
difference is that the deficit here is nearly universal, which makes the correction nearly
universally useful.

## 8. Special populations

**Healthy older adults.** The most-studied enhancement population, because ageing supplies a real
decrement to work against and because the market is large. Effects are correspondingly easier to
find and still small: exercise at ES 0.29 independent of cognitive status; tDCS plus cognitive
training at SMD 0.35 rising to 0.72 with ten or more sessions; brain-training programmes with
near transfer only, and far transfer only on subjective measures. The tDCS monotherapy literature
in this group spans g = −0.31 to 1.85 across thirteen studies and is not usable. The consistent
structural finding across modalities is that combination beats monotherapy, and that the
combination's active ingredient is usually the training, not the adjunct.

**Students.** The population that defines the phenomenon epidemiologically (§2) and the one where
the evidence-to-belief gap is widest. The domains stimulants improve — recall, sustained attention,
inhibitory control, processing-speed accuracy — are plausibly relevant to studying; the effect
sizes of 0.21–0.43 in laboratory conditions are not obviously relevant to a grade; and the users
with the highest prevalence are the ones with the lowest GPA (adjusted OR 1.83 for GPA <3.2 versus
≥3.6). The framing that fits the data is compensation under stress, not optimisation.

**Shift workers, clinicians, military and sustained operations.** The one setting where a
pharmacological agent has a defensible rationale, because the target is genuine sleep deprivation
rather than an intact baseline. The evidence supports modafinil maintaining wakefulness, memory
and executive function relative to placebo in the sleep-deprived — with the two caveats already
established: repeated doses across a longer deprivation period do not prevent performance decay,
and overconfidence may be induced. Anyone deploying this in a safety-critical role needs the
second caveat stated explicitly.

**Populations the literature has simply not studied.** Three gaps deserve naming rather than
inferring across. Almost the entire microdosing evidence base is male — the 80-participant phase 1
LSD programme, which contributes the sleep, pharmacokinetic and several cognitive findings, enrolled
male volunteers only. Adolescents are absent from the healthy-enhancement trial literature
altogether while being well represented in the misuse epidemiology. And there is no controlled
enhancement literature in pregnancy, nor should there be. Where this chapter reports an effect
size, it is an effect size in a young or older adult, usually male, usually a university student
or a volunteer recruited near a university.

## 9. Durability, tolerance and harm

**Durability is essentially unstudied.** This is the largest single hole in the evidence base. The
modal study is a single dose or a single session with an outcome measured the same day. Where
follow-up exists it is short and mostly negative: no residual effects on mood or task performance
at a drug-free follow-up 3–4 days after the last of four LSD dosing sessions; verbal working-memory
near-transfer gains from training not sustained at follow-up; repeated modafinil dosing failing to
sustain performance across extended deprivation. Nothing in this chapter has a demonstrated effect
lasting months, with the arguable exception of exercise, where the trials are weeks-to-months
interventions with end-of-intervention outcomes.

**Tolerance and escalation.** The pharmacology predicts tolerance for the catecholaminergic agents,
and the caffeine literature demonstrates the fully developed version of it: a habitual consumer
whose net effect across the consumption cycle is nil but who experiences each dose as beneficial
because it reverses a withdrawal trough. There is no reason to expect stimulants to behave
differently, and no long-term controlled data in healthy users to check it against.

**Progression to misuse.** The strongest longitudinal signal in the chapter. In national
multicohort panels of 11,066 US 12th-grade students followed biennially into ages 19–24, lifetime
stimulant *therapy* for ADHD in adolescence was not associated with increased odds of later
prescription drug misuse. Adolescent prescription stimulant *misuse* was: past-year prescription
drug misuse in young adulthood reached 53.1% among those reporting both therapy and misuse and
51.5% among those reporting misuse only, with significantly higher adjusted odds of both later
incidence and prevalence compared with controls. In a separate national longitudinal sample of
nearly 72,000 individuals across 30 cohorts followed over five waves from age 18 to 25/26,
non-medical use of prescription stimulants peaked at wave 1 and declined thereafter — but the
decline held for stimulants specifically and not for opioids, sedatives or tranquilizers once
baseline sociodemographic and substance-use behaviours were controlled. Enhancement use in
adolescence is a marker for a broader misuse trajectory, and that trajectory does not reliably
resolve with age across drug classes.

**Cardiovascular safety.** Better than feared, and studied at scale in the prescribed population.
A retrospective cohort across four health systems followed 150,359 adults aged 25–64 dispensed
methylphenidate, amphetamine or atomoxetine, matched to two non-users each (443,198 total), over
806,182 person-years. Crude incidence per 1,000 person-years during current use was 1.34
(95% CI 1.14–1.57) for myocardial infarction, 0.30 (0.20–0.42) for sudden cardiac death and 0.56
(0.43–0.72) for stroke. The multivariable-adjusted rate ratio for serious cardiovascular events
with current use versus non-use was 0.83 (95% CI 0.72–0.96) and for new use 0.77 (0.63–0.94) —
apparent protection that the authors attribute to healthy-user bias. Against the more appropriate
remote-use comparator, the adjusted rate ratio was 1.03 (0.86–1.24) for current use and 1.02
(0.82–1.28) for new use, with the upper bound corresponding to an additional 0.19 events per 1,000
person-years at ages 25–44 and 0.77 at ages 45–64. In privately insured young people aged 6–21
without known cardiovascular risk factors (171,126 individuals), there were 0.92 new cardiac events
and 3.08 new cardiac symptoms per 1,000,000 days of current stimulant use, with no significant
association between stimulant exposure and either endpoint and no difference between methylphenidate
and amphetamine. Two important limits: both cohorts studied *prescribed* use at therapeutic doses
in people screened by a prescriber, which is not the exposure of an enhancement user obtaining
diverted supply; and both excluded or lacked power for people with pre-existing cardiac risk.

**Dependence liability.** Modafinil's positioning as the low-liability option is
pharmacologically softer than its reputation (§3): it is a dopamine-uptake inhibitor at the same
transporter as cocaine, weaker and slower, and it fully substitutes for cocaine in a drug
discrimination paradigm. Nicotine's enhancement effect (§7.2) comes attached to the addiction
liability that makes it the leading preventable cause of death. Neither of these is a reason to
treat modafinil as equivalent to a stimulant of abuse; both are reasons not to describe it as
free of liability.

**Adverse effects in trials.** The reassuring counterweight: no preponderance of side effects or
mood changes in the modafinil systematic review; no serious adverse effects across the 14
controlled microdosing studies, though 4 of 80 participants in the six-week LSD trial withdrew
because of anxiety; and low-intensity transcranial electrical stimulation judged safe by
international consensus within tested protocols. In laboratory conditions, at studied doses, with
screened participants, this is a low-harm field. None of those three conditions describes real use.

## 10. Controversies, and what would settle them

**Is any of this enhancement at all?** The strongest deflationary reading — that every positive
result in the field is deficit correction, expectancy, or publication bias — survives more of the
evidence than is comfortable. It does not survive all of it. The tACS phase-dependence result, in
which antiphase stimulation of two regions degrades performance while in-phase stimulation improves
it, is hard to explain as anything but a real physiological manipulation of cognition. The
methylphenidate domain profile is too specific and too replicated to be pure bias. The 24-minute
sleep extension after LSD microdosing is an objective physiological change measured across 3,231
nights. Something real is happening. It is just much smaller, much more domain-specific, and much
more baseline-dependent than the word "enhancement" implies.

**Medicalisation of ordinary life.** Every intervention here converts a normal state — being
tired, being distractible, being ordinarily intelligent — into a target for pharmacological or
electrical management. The cross-lagged finding that enhancement predicts higher subsequent stress
suggests the conversion is not benign at the individual level. At the population level, the concern
raised in the neuroscience-and-society literature is coercion: if enough colleagues, students, or
surgeons use an agent, abstaining becomes a competitive cost rather than a free choice, and
"voluntary" stops describing the decision. The same literature notes that modafinil affects not
only "cold" cognition but "hot" cognition — emotion recognition and task-related motivation — which
widens the ethical question from performance to disposition.

**Regulatory asymmetry.** A tDCS device sold without medical claims may escape the regulatory
regime that governs the identical device sold for depression, while borrowing the published
literature's implied safety record. The consensus position — that devices permitting stimulation
outside studied parameters cannot claim to be safe on the strength of studies that used different
parameters — is the correct clinical line and is not currently enforced anywhere in the consumer
market.

**Publication bias as a structural, not incidental, feature.** Three separate corrections in this
chapter shrank or eliminated an effect: stimulant working and long-term memory, tDCS working memory,
tDCS inhibitory control, and commercial brain-training far transfer. A field in which small effects
are the whole subject matter and in which correction routinely eliminates them is a field whose
literature should be read as an upper bound.

**The pipeline is not aimed at this question.** Across the registry sweeps in §7.1 and §7.4, trials
of enhancement in genuinely healthy people are rare, small, single-site and mechanistic, while the
overwhelming majority of registered cognition trials recruit clinical populations. Representative
registered healthy-volunteer work: 30 participants aged 18–35 for tDCS during a face–name
associative learning task, completed with results posted; 27 for dual-site non-invasive stimulation
of language and cognition; 43 for tDCS and cognition in healthy older adults; 15 for development of
a home-use tDCS device; 180 for a volunteer cognitive-training programme in healthy adults aged 50
and over, reading out in late 2027. Nobody is running the trial that would answer the question the
public is asking, because there is no indication to register it against and no sponsor whose product
approval depends on it. That structural fact, not any individual null result, is why this literature
will keep looking the way it looks.

**What would settle it.** A trial in genuinely replete participants — screened for sleep debt,
caffeine withdrawal, undiagnosed ADHD and depression — with an active placebo that reproduces the
somatic cues, an outcome that is a real-world performance measure rather than a laboratory task,
sufficient duration to detect tolerance, stratification by baseline capacity so that the inverted-U
subgroups are not averaged away, and pre-registration. No trial in this chapter has all six
features, and several have none.

**The clinical bottom line, stated as a landscape rather than a verdict.** In healthy,
non-sleep-deprived adults, methylphenidate produces small improvements in recall, sustained
attention, inhibitory control and processing-speed accuracy; modafinil produces a negligible overall
effect with a real one on memory updating and a defensible role only under sleep deprivation;
d-amphetamine produces nothing measurable; nicotine produces attention effects comparable to
methylphenidate's at an unacceptable liability; caffeine's net effect across a habitual cycle is
close to nil; transcranial stimulation produces effects that mostly disappear under publication-bias
correction except in combination with training, with tACS the strongest exception; microdosed
psychedelics produce measurable acute physiology and no reproducible mood or cognitive benefit;
commercial brain training produces near transfer and subjective far transfer only; and exercise and
sleep extension produce the largest effects in the chapter. A patient asking which of these to take
is asking a decision question, and a decision question belongs in a decision instrument — route it
to a focused evidence appraisal rather than reading a verdict off this chapter.
