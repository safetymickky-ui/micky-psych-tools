# Crafting the Report

Read before you write. There is no fixed template, no fixed set of frames, and no mandated
shape — the report takes whatever structure serves this question and this reader. What
replaces the old rules is a small set of commitments you understand rather than a skeleton
you fill.

The report is one thing: **the answer, shown its working, at the depth the evidence
deserves.** It is read twice — once now, for the answer, and again months later from the
vault, as a reference. Write for both readers.

## The principles

**1. The answer exists and is marked.** Somewhere in the report — the top, when the reader
is deciding under time pressure; the close, when the argument needs to build — the verdict
is stated explicitly, marked so a skimmer finds it in seconds (a bolded verdict line or its
own heading), and carries an explicit confidence (high | moderate | moderate-low | low)
with the one clause it is not higher. A report that catalogues the literature and never
plants a flag has failed, whatever its depth. Verdict-first is a good default; it is not a
law.

**2. Shape serves the reader.** Choose the structure per report. A decision under time
pressure often wants verdict → argument → boundary conditions. A "help me actually
understand this" question often wants the topic shape — *Mechanism → Efficacy → Harms →
Verdict* — building context before it concludes. Topic-domain headings (*Mechanism,
Adverse effects, Special populations, Pharmacokinetics…*) are legitimate structure
whenever they organize the evidence better than a bespoke heading would. The only
illegitimate shape is the one you didn't choose — a remembered template, or an
encyclopedia's section list copied instead of thought.

**3. Depth is the product.** The report exists so the reader never has to redo the search.
Two rules make that real:

- **Load-bearing studies get the full treatment** — a developed paragraph each: design,
  population and setting, n, comparator, primary endpoint, effect size with CI, the harms
  and dropout that matter, and what makes this study believable or not. Supporting studies
  may be grouped and summarized; the ones the verdict rests on may not be compressed to a
  clause.
- **Mechanism and background are woven in wherever they illuminate** — receptor
  pharmacology, pathophysiology, why the drug *would* work, why a trial population
  generalizes or doesn't. As prose in the flow, or as their own section when the shape
  calls for one. The reader should *understand* the verdict, not merely accept it.

**4. Evidence travels as numbers.** A claim without a magnitude is decoration. Effect sizes,
CIs, n, NNT/NNH, doses, absolute event rates, remission and durability figures live *inside*
the prose — `"CAPS-B2 fell 3.2 points more than placebo (n=304, d≈0.15, non-significant)"`,
never `"reduced nightmares"`. A section with no number in it is not carrying its weight.

**5. Adjudicate, don't list.** When the trials disagree, say which one you believe and why —
size, control quality, blinding, funding, population, endpoint validity — and say it in the
flow of the argument, not in a summary paragraph bolted on at the end. A neutral catalogue of
both sides is not balance; it is a refusal to do the one thing the reader cannot do for
themselves.

**6. Read clean.** No inline citations taxing the prose — provenance lives in a compressed
`## Sources` block at the end, one auditable line per source (topic phrase + DOI; a web
document fetched via firecrawl cites URL + access date instead — nothing else). See *The
citation discipline* in the SKILL.

**7. As deep as the evidence deserves — in both directions.** Never cut a real finding, a
load-bearing source, or needed background to look decisive: compression is this skill's
characteristic failure mode. Never pad with repetition, throat-clearing, or evidence that
bears on a neighbouring question: padding is the lesser failure, but still one. The length
of the report is a *consequence* of what the literature contains, not a target.

**8. Declare confidence and what would move it.** End the verdict with its confidence level
and the one clause it is not higher. Somewhere the report names the live disagreements, the
replication status, and the completed-unpublished or ongoing trials with expected readout
that would change the verdict. If nothing would change it, say so — that is itself a
finding.

## Choosing the shape

Ask three questions of the locked brief, then commit:

- **How will this be read?** Under time pressure at the point of decision → answer first.
  As study material or a reference → context may build first, verdict at the close.
- **What does the reader need to *understand*, not just know?** If the verdict only makes
  sense once the mechanism or the trial landscape is laid out, give those their own
  sections and let them come first.
- **What organization does the evidence itself suggest?** A replication series wants
  chronology. A drug with efficacy solid but tolerability contested wants
  *Efficacy / Harms / The trade-off*. A head-to-head choice wants the contenders as
  sections. Take the structure the material offers instead of forcing one on it.

Then write the outline in one line before drafting, and check it against the anti-goal in
the brief. If the outline could not answer the question, no draft under it can.

## Worked shapes — illustrations, never a template

These show the range. None is *the* format; each is what a particular question demanded.

- **A treatment choice under time pressure** runs verdict-first: the verdict (dose,
  sequence, what not to promise, confidence) → the argued evidence, with each load-bearing
  trial given its full paragraph — the small positive crossovers and the large negative
  multisite trial each laid out in design, n, endpoint, and effect size before the
  adjudication lands → where it breaks (the comorbid, unmonitored patient it is wrong for)
  → what would change it → sources.
- **An "understand the evidence" question** runs topic-shaped: *How it is meant to work*
  (the pharmacology, honestly labelled where mechanism is conjecture) → *What the trials
  show* (the evidence base study by study, full detail) → *What the harms cost* (absolute
  rates, discontinuation, the monitoring burden) → *Verdict* (marked, confidence-rated,
  built from everything above). The verdict closes rather than leads because the reader
  asked to understand, not merely to be told — but it is still there, still marked, still
  explicit.
- **A contested claim** often wants chronology: the verdict (true/false/unsettled + why the
  disagreement exists — leading, because the claim is the whole question) → the replication
  series in order, effect-size drift shown study by study with each trial's design and
  power laid out → what would settle it → sources.
- **A teaching decision** puts the *verbatim sentence to be spoken* in the verdict slot,
  with one memorable correct number and its expiry conditions, then the textbook-vs-evidence
  gap as the body — which position the trainee should hold, which they should merely
  recognise on an exam — then sources.

The lesson across all four: the shape was *chosen* for the question and the reader. Build
yours the same way — from the brief and the material, not from a remembered outline.

## Signs you've drifted

Diagnostic, not a checklist. The failure now has two directions — watch both:

**Toward the unanswered survey:**

- No explicit, marked verdict anywhere — or one hedged into uselessness.
- A section that reads as "here is what the literature contains" with no argument moving
  toward the answer.
- Two trials described, neither believed.

**Toward the hollowed answer:**

- A load-bearing study reduced to a name and a conclusion — no design, no population, no n,
  no effect size.
- Mechanism or background the reader needs, stripped to keep the report short.
- A real finding cut, or a source dropped, for leanness rather than irrelevance.
- A paragraph with no number.

**Either way:**

- A structure you didn't choose — a remembered template, or Wikipedia's section list.
- An outline that could not answer the question no matter how well it were written.
